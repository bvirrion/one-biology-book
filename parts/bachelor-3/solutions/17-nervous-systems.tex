\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:nervous-systems} --- Organization of Nervous Systems}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:1}
\omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{Astrocytes}: buffer potassium and glutamate, feed neurons, induce the
blood--brain barrier, regulate local blood flow. \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{Oligodendrocytes}:
myelinate axons in the central nervous system. Schwann cells:
myelinate peripheral axons (and guide their regeneration). \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{Microglia}:
the brain's \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{macrophages}, pruning synapses and clearing debris.
(Ependymal cells line the ventricles and make \omterm{prop:b3:nervous-systems:barrier-budget}{cerebrospinal fluid}.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:2}
The stain blackened a few neurons completely and left the rest
invisible, so a single cell could be seen entire. Cajal concluded that
neurons are separate cells contacting one another without fusing, with
a fixed direction of flow from dendrites to axon; Golgi concluded that
they formed a continuous network. Cajal was right; the electron
microscope showed the synaptic gap in 1954.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:3}
A tap stretches the quadriceps; its muscle spindles fire; the Ia
afferents enter the cord and synapse directly on the quadriceps motor
neurons, which fire and contract the muscle, while an \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{interneuron}
inhibits the hamstring motor neurons. One synapse, short axons at
\qty{80}{m/s}: about \qty{30}{ms}. A voluntary kick passes through the
brain --- perception, decision, motor planning, descending tracts,
several synapses --- and takes \qtyrange{200}{300}{ms}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:4}
Sympathetic (noradrenaline at the target): heart faster and stronger,
pupil dilated, gut motility and secretion reduced, airways dilated.
Parasympathetic (acetylcholine): heart slowed, pupil constricted, gut
motility and secretion increased, airways constricted.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:5}
$\lambda = \sqrt{R_{m}d/4R_{i}} = \sqrt{10^{4}\times 10^{-4}/400}$ cm
$= \qty{0.05}{cm} = \qty{0.5}{mm}$. After \qty{2}{mm}: $e^{-4} \approx
2\,\%$. Doubling $\lambda$ needs four times the diameter,
\qty{4}{\micro m}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:6}
Sensory: $12\times 6 = \qty{72}{m/s}$, $1.2/72 = \qty{17}{ms}$. Motor:
\qty{90}{m/s}, $1.1/90 = \qty{12}{ms}$. Two synapses, \qty{1}{ms}: about
\qty{30}{ms}. Unmyelinated: $1.1\sqrt{12} = \qty{3.8}{m/s}$ and
$1.1\sqrt{15} = \qty{4.3}{m/s}$: $315 + 258 + 1 \approx \qty{570}{ms}$
--- too slow to save the foot.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:7}
$2.4\times 10^{20}/8.6\times 10^{10} \approx 2.8\times 10^{9}$ ATP per
neuron per second; at $2\times 10^{9}$ per spike, about \qty{1.4}{Hz}
on average. The code must be sparse: few spikes, information carried by
which neurons fire and when, not by high rates everywhere.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:8}
Two neurons that inhibit each other without tiring form a switch: the
first to fire suppresses the other for as long as the drive lasts, and
nothing ever hands over. Oscillation needs a slow process that weakens
the winner's grip --- adaptation of its firing, depression of its
inhibitory synapse, or a rebound of the loser after release --- whose
time constant sets the period.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:9}
L-dopa is carried across the barrier by the transporter for large
neutral amino acids; dopamine, polar and charged, is not. Patients take
L-dopa (with an inhibitor of the peripheral enzyme so that it is not
converted before crossing), and the brain's own enzyme makes dopamine
from it where it is needed. Antibodies, at \qty{150}{kDa}, cross
neither the tight junctions nor any transporter; delivery needs a
shuttle that hijacks a receptor for transcytosis, or injection into the
\omterm{prop:b3:nervous-systems:barrier-budget}{cerebrospinal fluid}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:10}
The membrane current per unit length is the resistive $V/r_{m}$ plus
the capacitive $c_{m}\,\partial V/\partial t$; charge conservation gives
$\partial I/\partial x = -(V/r_{m} + c_{m}\,\partial V/\partial t)$, and
with $\partial V/\partial x = -r_{i}I$, $\frac{1}{r_{i}}\,\partial^{2}V/
\partial x^{2} = V/r_{m} + c_{m}\,\partial V/\partial t$; multiplying by
$r_{m}$: $\lambda^{2}\,\partial^{2}V/\partial x^{2} = V + \tau\,\partial
V/\partial t$. With $\partial V/\partial t = 0$ the theorem's equation
returns. $\lambda$ is a length and $\tau$ a time, so $\lambda/\tau$ is
a speed --- the natural scale for how fast a disturbance spreads.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:11}
$v \propto \sqrt{d}$: four times the speed needs sixteen times the
diameter, \qty{8}{mm}. Area $\pi(4\,\text{mm})^{2} \approx \qty{50}{mm^2}$
against $\pi\,(10\,\unit{\micro m})^{2} = \qty{314}{\micro m^2}$: a ratio of
$1.6\times 10^{5}$. A nerve of fast unmyelinated fibres is impossible
--- one such fibre would be as thick as the nerve --- so an animal with
many fast channels needs \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{myelin}, which is why vertebrates (and a few
invertebrates independently) evolved it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:nervous-systems:12}
$a = 0.4/30^{0.75} = 0.4/12.8 = 0.031$; for \qty{70000}{g}: $0.031\times
\num{70000}^{0.75} = 0.031\times 4300 \approx \qty{134}{g}$. The human
\qty{1350}{g} is ten times the prediction. Brain mass follows body
mass because a larger body needs more sensory and motor neurons, and
because neurons themselves grow with brain size; what tracks cognition
is the number of neurons in the cortex, which depends on how densely
they are packed --- primates pack more per gram than other mammals,
and humans most of all.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:nervous-systems:1}
\textbf{1.} $\lambda = \sqrt{2\times 10^{4}\,d/400}$: for $d =
\qty{1e-4}{cm}$, $\sqrt{5\times 10^{-3}} = \qty{0.071}{cm} =
\qty{0.71}{mm}$; for \qty{4}{\micro m}, \qty{1.41}{mm}. $\tau = 2\times
10^{4}\times 10^{-6} = \qty{20}{ms}$.
\textbf{2.} $5\,e^{-0.3/0.71} = \qty{3.3}{mV}$; $5\,e^{-0.3/1.41} =
\qty{4.0}{mV}$.
\textbf{3.} Distal inputs arrive attenuated, so where a synapse lands
sets its weight; a potential decays in about $\tau$, so two inputs sum
only if they fall within some \qty{20}{ms} of each other --- the neuron
is a coincidence detector with a \qty{20}{ms} window.
\textbf{4.} Myelinated \qty{60}{m/s}; unmyelinated $1.1\sqrt{10} =
\qty{3.5}{m/s}$; to reach \qty{60}{m/s} unmyelinated, $d = (60/1.1)^{2}
\approx \qty{3000}{\micro m}$: three millimetres.
\textbf{5.} $\pi(5)^{2} = \qty{79}{\micro m^2}$ against $\pi(1500)^{2} =
7\times 10^{6}\,\unit{\micro m^2}$: about \num{90000} myelinated axons
in the space of one.
\textbf{6.} \qty{2}{cm} at \qty{60}{m/s} is \qty{0.3}{ms}; at
\qty{3.5}{m/s}, \qty{5.7}{ms}: some \qty{5}{ms} of extra delay. Worse,
the bare membrane has a large capacitance and few channels, so the
current from the last intact node may fail to bring it to threshold:
conduction block.
\textbf{7.} Sensory $1.2/60 = \qty{20}{ms}$; two synapses \qty{1}{ms};
motor $1.1/90 = \qty{12}{ms}$: about \qty{33}{ms} (plus a millisecond
at the neuromuscular junction).
\textbf{8.} To the brain: $0.6/60 = \qty{10}{ms}$ plus two synapses,
\qty{11}{ms} after the signal enters the cord at \qty{20}{ms}: the
signal reaches the cortex at about the time the foot moves; the
conscious experience of pain needs a few hundred milliseconds more of
processing, so the foot is withdrawn before the pain is felt.
\textbf{9.} $1.1\sqrt{1} = \qty{1.1}{m/s}$: $1.8/1.1 \approx
\qty{1.6}{s}$. The sharp first pain arrives by thin myelinated fibres
within tens of milliseconds; the dull, burning second pain by the
unmyelinated C fibres a second or more later.
\textbf{10.} Sensory $3.2/60 = \qty{53}{ms}$, motor $3.1/90 =
\qty{34}{ms}$, synapses \qty{1}{ms}: about \qty{90}{ms}. Long animals
have slow reflexes; they compensate with thicker axons and by
delegating more to local circuits.
\textbf{11.} $0.8/80 = \qty{10}{ms}$ each way, \qty{20}{ms}; synapse
\qty{0.5}{ms}, junction \qty{1}{ms}, force \qty{5}{ms}: \qty{26.5}{ms},
with the spindle's own transduction and the conduction into the muscle
making up the rest of the \qty{30}{ms}.
\textbf{12.} Unmyelinated axons conduct at a metre or two per second,
so loops that take \qty{30}{ms} in an adult take hundreds, with poor
timing; myelination raises the speed ten- to fiftyfold and makes timing
precise, which is what walking, grasping and speech require.
\textbf{13.} $20/50\,000 = 4\times 10^{-4}$ mol/s $= 2.4\times 10^{20}$
ATP per second; $2.8\times 10^{9}$ per neuron per second.
\textbf{14.} $4\times 10^{8} + 7000\times 2\times 10^{4} = 4\times 10^{8}
+ 1.4\times 10^{8} = 5.4\times 10^{8}$ ATP.
\textbf{15.} All on spikes: $2.8\times 10^{9}/5.4\times 10^{8} \approx
\qty{5}{Hz}$; half: about \qty{2.6}{Hz}.
\textbf{16.} At \qty{1}{Hz}, $5.4\times 10^{8}/2.8\times 10^{9} \approx
\qty{20}{\%}$ of the budget: the code is sparse --- most neurons silent
most of the time, information in which few fire and when.
\textbf{17.} $8.6\times 10^{10}\times 50\times 5.4\times 10^{8} = 2.3
\times 10^{21}$ ATP/s $= 3.9\times 10^{-3}$ mol/s $\approx \qty{190}{W}$
--- twice the whole resting body.
\textbf{18.} $5\times 16 = \qty{80}{kJ/h} = \qty{22}{W}$: just enough,
with no reserve. When the supply stops, the pumps stop within seconds,
the gradients run down, and consciousness is lost in about ten seconds.
\textbf{19.} $a = 0.4/12.8 = 0.031$; \qty{70}{kg}: $0.031\times 4300 =
\qty{134}{g}$; \qty{5000}{kg}: $0.031\times 1.06\times 10^{5} \approx
\qty{3300}{g}$.
\textbf{20.} Human $1350/134 \approx 10$; elephant $4800/3300 \approx
1.5$.
\textbf{21.} In the \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{cerebellum} --- \qty{98}{\%} of them --- coordinating
the trunk's forty thousand muscles. Cortical neuron number, not total,
tracks cognition; the \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{cerebellum} tracks the motor task of running a
huge body.
\textbf{22.} Axons and dendrites lengthen with the brain, so each neuron
occupies more volume and the number of neurons grows more slowly than
mass. In primates neuron size stays nearly constant as the brain grows,
so neuron number grows almost in proportion to mass, and a primate
brain of a given weight holds several times the neurons of a rodent
brain of that weight.
\textbf{23.} An arm can coordinate its own suckers and joints, reach,
grasp, pass food along itself and avoid grasping its own skin, by local
circuits; it cannot see, choose a target or learn a discrimination.
Test: cut the arm's nerve cord from the brain and touch the arm with
food --- it grasps and passes it toward where the mouth would be; an
amputated arm does the same for an hour.
\textbf{24.} About $23$ synapses per neuron against $7000$: three hundred
times fewer. The complete map tells which neuron can influence which,
but not the strength or sign of each connection, which neuromodulators
change them, or the dynamics --- so even for the worm the wiring
diagram predicts behaviour only in part.
\textbf{25.} $\lambda = \qty{0.71}{mm}$ for the \qty{1}{\micro m}
dendrite; withdrawal latency about \qty{33}{ms}; mean firing rate
about \qty{2.6}{Hz} with half the budget on spikes.
\end{solution}
