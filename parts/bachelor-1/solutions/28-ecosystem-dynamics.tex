\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b1:ecosystem-dynamics} --- Ecosystem Dynamics}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:1}
Primary: on new substrate without soil (a moraine, a lava flow);
secondary: where soil and seed bank remain (an abandoned field, a
burnt wood). Secondary is faster, by a factor of ten or more, because
the soil and its nutrients and seeds do not have to be made.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:2}
Facilitation, inhibition, tolerance. On lava, facilitation: the
pioneers (\omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:symbiosis}{lichens}, mosses, nitrogen fixers) must build soil before
anything else can \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:3}
Resistance: remaining unchanged through a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}{disturbance}; \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}{resilience}:
returning quickly afterward. A reef is neither resistant to warming
nor resilient; a meadow has low resistance to a fire and high
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}{resilience}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:4}
$16^{0.25} = 2$: \num{24} species; $625^{0.25} = 5$: \num{60} species.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:5}
The till has almost no nitrogen; spruces cannot fix it and grow only
where it is supplied. The alders, which fix nitrogen and raise the
soil's content to hundreds of kilograms per hectare, are therefore
what makes the site fit for spruce: facilitation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:6}
$S^* = I_0 P/(I_0 + eP) = 1600/(8 + 10) = 89$ species; turnover
$eS^* = 4.4$ species a year. With $I_0 = 4$: $800/14 = 57$ species,
turnover \num{2.9}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:7}
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{Decomposers} went on mineralising the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}{litter} and humus to ammonium,
and nitrifiers converted it to nitrate, a soluble anion the soil
does not hold; with no \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} taking it up, it was washed out. Regrowth
restored uptake: the new plants took the nitrate as fast as it
formed, and the stream ran clean again.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:8}
$k = \ln 2/1 = \qty{0.69}{yr^{-1}}$; after three years $e^{-2.08} =
\qty{12.5}{\%}$ left. Conifer: $k = -\ln 0.85 = \qty{0.16}{yr^{-1}}$,
half-life \num{4.3} years, \qty{61}{\%} left after three years --- the
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}{litter} accumulates.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:9}
Ungrazed: tall competitive grasses shade out the rest, few species.
Moderate grazing: the dominants are cropped, light reaches the
ground, small herbs coexist with grasses: maximum diversity. Heavy
grazing: only prostrate, unpalatable or fast-growing species survive
the trampling and cropping: few species again.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:10}
Original: $c\,1000^{0.25} = 5.6c$; one fragment $10^{0.25}c = 1.78c$,
\qty{32}{\%} of the original. Ten isolated fragments hold, if their
lists were entirely different, $17.8c$ --- more than the original,
which is absurd: the fragments share most species, so the sum
overcounts. The true total lies between $1.78c$ (identical lists) and
about $5.6c$ (the original), less what the isolation and edges
remove; the species lost first are those that need more than
\qty{10}{km^2} each.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:11}
Exact recovery would suggest a fixed \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community} determined by the
island's conditions; recovery of the number with a different list,
and a list that keeps changing, is what a balance between random
immigration and extinction predicts --- the number is a property of
area and distance, the identities are chance.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:12}
Not closed: it takes in rain, dust, nitrogen from the air (by fixation
and deposition) and ions from weathering rock, and loses water,
dissolved ions and gases through its stream and its air; but the
inputs and outputs are small compared with the internal cycle from
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}{litter} to soil to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}, so that the forest looks closed to
the resolution of a year's budget. The felling showed where the
apparent closure came from: the uptake by living vegetation; with it
gone, the same soil and the same \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers} made the valley leak
its nutrients in a season.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:1}
\textbf{1.} 10--40 years: $140/30 = \qty{4.7}{kg/ha}$ a year; 40--80:
$150/40 = \num{3.75}$; 80--200: $20/120 = \num{0.17}$. Fastest in
the alder stage.
\textbf{2.} Facilitation, for alder to spruce: the nitrogen the alders
add is what the spruces need.
\textbf{3.} The forest has reached a steady state: inputs (fixation,
deposition) balance losses (leaching, denitrification, wood
removed) and the nitrogen cycles internally rather than accumulating.
\textbf{4.} \qty{150}{kg/ha} retained over 40 years against
\num{2000} fixed: \qty{7.5}{\%}; the rest is held in the alders'
own biomass and \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}{litter}, leached, or denitrified.
\textbf{5.} Plant spruce seedlings on 20-year till with and without
alders and with and without nitrogen fertiliser: if spruce grows
without alders when fertilised, and not without fertiliser, the
alders' effect is nitrogen (facilitation); if spruce grows equally
in all plots and simply slower than alder, tolerance.
\textbf{6.} It assumes every site started alike and followed the same
path, which climate, chance arrivals and the glacier's varying till
may have broken; only a permanent plot proves the sequence at one
place.
\textbf{7.} By \qty{330}{mm}, \qty{40}{\%}: the trees no longer
transpired their share of the rain.
\textbf{8.} Control: \qty{2}{kg/ha} in \qty{8200}{m^3}: \qty{0.24}{mg/L}.
Felled: \qty{120}{kg} in \qty{11500}{m^3}: \qty{10.4}{mg/L}, forty
times more.
\textbf{9.} Control: $6.5 - 2 = +\qty{4.5}{kg/ha}$ a year, accumulating.
Felled: $6.5 - 120 = -\qty{113.5}{kg/ha}$ a year, losing.
\textbf{10.} $120/3850 = \qty{3.1}{\%}$ in a year; the pool would last
some \num{30} years at that rate --- but the rate falls as regrowth
returns.
\textbf{11.} Calcium tenfold, potassium eighteenfold. Potassium is held
mostly in living \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} and easily leached from \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}{litter}; calcium is
also bound on soil clays and in the rock, released more slowly.
\textbf{12.} From the mineralisation of \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}{litter}, humus and the felled
slash: ammonification (organic N to ammonium) by \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers}, then
nitrification (ammonium to nitrate) by nitrifying bacteria, faster in
the warmer, wetter, uncovered soil.
\textbf{13.} Falling from \num{120} to \num{20} to \num{5}: below the
\qty{6.5}{kg/ha} input by about year 6; the uptake of a new cover of
pin cherry, raspberry and saplings restores the retention.
\textbf{14.} $\log A$: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4; $\log S$: 1.08, 1.32, 1.58,
1.83, 2.08 --- a straight line.
\textbf{15.} $z = (2.08 - 1.08)/4 = 0.25$; $c = 12$. Middle islands:
$12\times 10^{0.25} = 21.3$, $12\times 10^{0.5} = 37.9$, $12\times
10^{0.75} = 67.5$: all within one species.
\textbf{16.} $12\times 250^{0.25} = 12\times 3.98 = 48$ species.
\textbf{17.} $38 = 6\times 300/(6 + 300e)$: $6 + 300e = 47.4$, $e =
\qty{0.138}{yr^{-1}}$; turnover $0.138\times 38 = 5.2$ species a year.
\textbf{18.} $3\times 300/(3 + 41.4) = 20$ species.
\textbf{19.} $30\times 300/(30 + 41.4) = 126$ species. Extinction on a
\qty{100}{km^2} island is still fast: even with free access, the
species that cannot maintain a \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{population} on that area keep dying
out; area, not access, sets the ceiling.
\textbf{20.} A higher $z$ means species number falls faster with
area: small islands relatively poorer. Biologically, \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} on
small islands go extinct more readily, or the islands are so isolated
that few species reach them (isolation raises $z$).
\textbf{21.} $(1000/100\,000)^{0.25} = 0.01^{0.25} = 0.32$: about a
third.
\textbf{22.} Isolated, each holds $(100/100\,000)^{0.25} = 0.18$ of the
species; if their lists were independent they would together hold
more than the one reserve, but they overlap heavily and each loses
the species needing more than \qty{100}{km^2}, so fewer in practice.
Connected by corridors they behave as one \qty{1000}{km^2} reserve for
species that use the corridors: about a third again, with the
advantage that a local catastrophe does not empty all of them.
\textbf{23.} \qty{1000}{km^2} is \qty{31.6}{km} a side: interior
$(31.6 - 0.6)^2 = \qty{961}{km^2}$, \qty{96}{\%}. \qty{100}{km^2} is
\qty{10}{km} a side: $(10 - 0.6)^2 = 88$, \qty{88}{\%}.
\textbf{24.} A slow decline from the original number toward the new
equilibrium $cA^z$, over decades: the species that will go extinct
are still present at first, in \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} too small to persist ---
the extinction debt is the difference between the present count and
the equilibrium, still to be paid.
\textbf{25.} $z = 0.25$; the \qty{100}{km^2} island holds \num{38}
species at equilibrium and about \num{126} once bridged.
\end{solution}
