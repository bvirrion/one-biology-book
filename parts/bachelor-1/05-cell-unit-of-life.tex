\chapter{The Cell: Unit of Life}\label{ch:b1:cell-unit-of-life}

In 1665 Robert Hooke put a sliver of cork under a lens and saw it
divided into tiny boxes he called \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, after the rooms of a monastery.
Ten years later a Dutch draper, Antoni van Leeuwenhoek, grinding his
own lenses to a power no one else could reach, saw living things
swimming in a drop of pond water, in the scrapings of his teeth, in his
own semen. It took another century and a half to state what the two
had seen: that every \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} is made of \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, and that every \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}
comes from a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}. This chapter states the \omterm{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:theory}{cell theory} and its
evidence, contrasts the two kinds of \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, and describes the
instruments --- the microscopes, the centrifuge, the culture flask ---
that let us see \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, take them apart, and keep them alive outside an
\omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism}.

\section{The cell theory}

\begin{definition}[Cell]\label{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}
A \emph{cell}\index{cell} is the smallest unit of living matter: a
volume of aqueous \emph{cytoplasm}\index{cytoplasm} bounded by a
\emph{plasma membrane}\index{plasma membrane}, containing the genetic
material (DNA) and the machinery that expresses it, able to take in
nutrients, transform energy, maintain its own organisation and, when
conditions permit, divide into two cells.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The cell theory]\label{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:theory}
\index{cell theory}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Every \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} is made of one or more \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, and the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} is
        the unit of structure and function of living things
        (Schleiden and Schwann, 1838--1839).
  \item Every \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} arises from a pre-existing \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} by division
        (Virchow, 1855: \emph{omnis cellula e cellula}); there is no
        spontaneous generation.
  \item The hereditary material is passed from \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} to \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} at
        division, so that the continuity of life is the continuity of
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Hooke's cork (1665) and the plant sections of Grew and Malpighi showed
plant \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} as arrays of chambers; Leeuwenhoek's lenses (1670s) showed
single-celled \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms}, blood \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} and sperm. Two centuries of
improving lenses found \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} in every \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} examined, animal as well as
plant, once Schwann recognised nucleated \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} in cartilage and embryos.
Division was seen directly in algae and in embryos, and Virchow found
no \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue}, healthy or diseased, whose \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} did not come from other
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}. Pasteur's swan-necked flasks (1861) --- broths that stayed sterile
for months when dust could not reach them, and teemed within days when
it could --- closed the question of spontaneous generation. The third
statement is the work of the later nineteenth century, which followed
the chromosomes through division (\cref{ch:b1:replication-mitosis}).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[One cell, many cells]\label{ex:b1:cell-unit-of-life:onemany}
A bacterium, a yeast, an amoeba are whole \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} of one \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, doing
everything --- feeding, moving, dividing --- within a few micrometres.
A human is some $\num{3e13}$ \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} of two hundred kinds, none of which
could live alone in a pond, each of which descends by unbroken
divisions from one fertilised egg. The rabbit of
\cref{ch:b1:mammal-organization} and the oak of
\cref{ch:b1:flowering-plant-organization} are the same theory applied
twice.
\end{example}

\section{Two kinds of cell}

\begin{definition}[Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells]\label{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}
A \emph{prokaryotic cell}\index{prokaryotic cell} (bacteria, archaea)
has no nucleus: its DNA, usually one circular chromosome, lies in a
region of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cytoplasm} called the \emph{nucleoid}\index{nucleoid},
and it has no membrane-bounded compartments. It is small
(\qtyrange{0.5}{5}{\micro m}) and bounded by a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{plasma membrane} and,
outside it, a \emph{cell wall}\index{cell wall}. A \emph{eukaryotic
cell}\index{eukaryotic cell} (protists, fungi, plants, animals) keeps
its DNA, as several linear chromosomes, inside a
\emph{nucleus}\index{nucleus} bounded by a double membrane, and divides
its \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cytoplasm} into membrane-bounded \emph{organelles}\index{organelle}
(\cref{ch:b1:eukaryotic-cell}). It is larger
(\qtyrange{10}{100}{\micro m}) and has an internal skeleton of protein
filaments.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The bacterial cell]\label{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:bacterium}
\emph{E. coli}, the model bacterium, is a rod \qty{2}{\micro m} long and
\qty{0.8}{\micro m} wide: a volume of about \qty{1}{\micro m^3} holding
one chromosome of $\num{4.6e6}$ base pairs (\qty{1.5}{mm} of DNA folded
ten-thousand-fold), some \num{20000} ribosomes, a few million protein
molecules of \num{2000} kinds, and often one or more
\emph{plasmids}\index{plasmid} --- small circular DNA molecules carrying
extra genes. Its envelope is, from inside out, the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{plasma membrane}, a
thin wall of \emph{peptidoglycan}\index{peptidoglycan} (a mesh of sugar
chains cross-linked by short peptides, which resists the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s
internal pressure), and an outer membrane. Bacteria with a thick
peptidoglycan wall and no outer membrane retain the crystal-violet
stain of the \emph{Gram stain}\index{Gram stain} (Gram-positive); those
with a thin wall under an outer membrane lose it (Gram-negative, as
\emph{E. coli}). Flagella rotate like propellers; pili attach the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}
to surfaces and to other \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[The Gram stain]\label{met:b1:cell-unit-of-life:gram}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fix a thin smear of bacteria to a slide by heat.
  \item Stain with crystal violet (all \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} purple), then with iodine,
        which forms a large complex with the dye inside the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
  \item Rinse with alcohol: the thick peptidoglycan of Gram-positive
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} shrinks and traps the complex; the thin wall of
        Gram-negative \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, once the alcohol has dissolved the outer
        membrane, lets it wash out.
  \item Counterstain with a pink dye (safranin): Gram-positive \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}
        appear purple, Gram-negative pink. The result predicts the wall
        structure and, with it, the sensitivity to many antibiotics.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Why the eukaryotic cell is compartmented]\label{ex:b1:cell-unit-of-life:whycompart}
A \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} of \qty{20}{\micro m} has a thousand times the volume of a
bacterium but only a hundred times its membrane surface
(\cref{prop:b1:organism-environment:sv}). Membranes are where a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}
carries its energy-transducing chains and its transport systems; the
eukaryote recovers surface by folding membranes inside itself --- the
inner membrane of a mitochondrion, the sheets of the endoplasmic
reticulum --- and gains, besides, separate chambers in which
incompatible chemistries (digestion in the lysosome, synthesis in the
cytosol) can run side by side.
\end{example}

\section{Seeing cells: microscopy}

\begin{definition}[Magnification and resolution]\label{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:resolution}
A microscope \emph{magnifies}\index{magnification} (enlarges the image)
and \emph{resolves}\index{resolution} (separates neighbouring points).
Only the second limits what can be seen: the \emph{resolving
power}\index{resolving power} is the smallest distance $d$ between two
points that still appear distinct,
\[
d = \frac{0.61\,\lambda}{\mathrm{NA}},
\]
where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the illumination and $\mathrm{NA}$
the numerical aperture of the objective (the sine of its half-angle of
light collection times the refractive index of the medium, at most
about $1.4$ with oil). Magnifying beyond the resolving power enlarges
the blur, not the detail.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Light and electron microscopes]\label{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:microscopes}
With visible light ($\lambda \approx \qty{550}{nm}$) and
$\mathrm{NA} = 1.4$, the \emph{light microscope}\index{light microscope} resolves \qty{0.24}{\micro m}: \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, nuclei, chloroplasts,
mitochondria as dots, bacteria as rods --- and living, moving specimens.
Contrast in a transparent \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} is obtained by staining (which usually
kills), by \emph{phase contrast} (converting differences of refractive
index into differences of brightness), or by \emph{fluorescence}
(labelling one molecule with a dye or a fluorescent protein and
imaging only its light). The \emph{electron microscope}\index{electron microscope} uses electrons of wavelength a few picometres; its
practical resolution, limited by its lenses, is about \qty{0.2}{nm} in
transmission (TEM: electrons pass through a section \qty{50}{nm} thick
and stained with heavy metals, giving the internal structure of
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{organelles}) and a few nanometres in scanning (SEM: a beam swept over a
metal-coated surface, giving its relief in depth). Electron microscopy
requires a vacuum and dead, fixed, dehydrated specimens.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Choosing the instrument]\label{met:b1:cell-unit-of-life:choose}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item To follow a living process (\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} division, movement, a
        fluorescent protein moving between compartments): light
        microscopy, phase contrast or fluorescence, on unfixed \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
  \item To count and identify \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} in a \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue}: a fixed, sectioned,
        stained preparation under the \omterm{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:microscopes}{light microscope}.
  \item To see the internal structure of an \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{organelle}, a membrane, a
        virus: transmission electron microscopy of thin sections, or
        of particles negatively stained.
  \item To see the surface relief of a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} or a \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue}: scanning
        electron microscopy.
  \item To locate one protein: immunofluorescence (light) or
        immunogold labelling (electron), using an antibody against it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[A bacterium and a mitochondrion]\label{ex:b1:cell-unit-of-life:seeing}
Under the \omterm{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:microscopes}{light microscope} at $1000\times$, an \emph{E. coli} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} is a
rod \qty{2}{mm} long on the retina's scale, with no visible interior;
a mitochondrion is a dot at the limit of resolution. In a TEM section
the same bacterium shows its two membranes, its wall, its \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{nucleoid} as
a pale fibrous region and its ribosomes as dark grains; the
mitochondrion shows its double membrane and the folds of the inner one.
Neither is alive.
\end{example}

\section{Taking cells apart and keeping them alive}

\begin{definition}[Cell fractionation]\label{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}
\emph{Cell fractionation}\index{cell fractionation} separates the
components of a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} for study. The \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} is
\emph{homogenised}\index{homogenate} --- \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} broken open in a cold,
isotonic buffer by grinding or shearing, leaving the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{organelles} intact
--- and the homogenate is subjected to \emph{differential
centrifugation}\index{differential centrifugation}: a series of spins
of increasing force and duration, each of which pellets the largest
and densest particles remaining in suspension. Each fraction is
identified by its appearance under the \omterm{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:microscopes}{electron microscope} and by
\emph{marker enzymes}\index{marker enzyme}, activities known to reside in
one compartment only.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Sedimentation]\label{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:stokes}
A spherical particle of radius $r$ and density $\rho_p$ in a fluid of
density $\rho_f$ and viscosity $\eta$, in a centrifugal field $g_c$
(a multiple of $g$), sediments at the steady speed
\[
v = \frac{2}{9}\,\frac{r^2\,(\rho_p - \rho_f)\,g_c}{\eta}
\]
(Stokes' law). Speed grows as the square of the radius: a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{nucleus} of
\qty{5}{\micro m} falls a hundred times faster than a mitochondrion of
\qty{0.5}{\micro m}, which falls a thousand times faster than a
ribosome of \qty{15}{nm}. This is why the same tube separates them at
increasing speeds.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At steady speed the drag of Stokes, $6\pi\eta r v$, balances the net
weight of the particle in the fluid,
$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3(\rho_p - \rho_f)g_c$; solving for $v$ gives the
formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Cell culture]\label{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:culture}
\emph{Cell culture}\index{cell culture} keeps \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} alive and dividing
outside the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism}, in a sterile medium supplying nutrients, salts,
growth factors and, for animal \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, a surface to attach to, at the
\omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism}'s temperature. Bacteria and yeasts grow in simple media and
double in minutes to hours; animal \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} taken from a \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue}
(\emph{primary cultures}) divide a limited number of times, while
\emph{\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} lines} derived from tumours or transformed \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} divide
indefinitely. Culture makes the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} an experimental object: one type,
one medium, one variable at a time.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[A growth curve]\label{ex:b1:cell-unit-of-life:growthcurve}
A flask of \emph{E. coli} in a rich medium at \qty{37}{\celsius}: a lag
of half an hour, then exponential growth --- the count doubles every
\qty{20}{min}, so $N = N_0\,2^{t/20}$ --- until a nutrient runs out or
wastes accumulate and the culture reaches a plateau near $10^9$ \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}
per millilitre. From a single \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, $2^{30} \approx 10^9$ \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} in ten
hours. The same curve, with a doubling time of a day, describes a
culture of human \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: Fractionating a Liver}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- ten grams of rat liver taken apart
by centrifugation: pellets, markers, Stokes' law and the balance sheet
of an enzyme, ending on the purification factor of the mitochondrial
fraction}]
\label{pb:b1:cell-unit-of-life:1}
\qty{10}{g} of rat liver is \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{homogenised} in \qty{50}{mL} of cold
isotonic sucrose and spun in three steps: $1000\,g$ for \qty{10}{min}
(pellet 1), $\num{10000}\,g$ for \qty{20}{min} (pellet 2),
$\num{100000}\,g$ for \qty{60}{min} (pellet 3), leaving the final
supernatant. The protein content and two enzyme activities are measured
in the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{homogenate} and in each fraction:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
fraction & protein (mg) & succinate dehydrogenase (U) & lactate dehydrogenase (U) \\
\hline
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{homogenate} & 2000 & 100 & 400 \\
pellet 1 & 500 & 15 & 20 \\
pellet 2 & 200 & 60 & 12 \\
pellet 3 & 300 & 5 & 8 \\
final supernatant & 950 & 4 & 350 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Take $\eta = \qty{1e-3}{Pa.s}$ for the medium, a sedimentation path of
\qty{5}{cm}, and $g = \qty{9.81}{m/s^2}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- What is where.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Name the main \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{organelle} in each pellet.
  \item Why must the homogenisation medium be cold and isotonic?
  \item Succinate dehydrogenase is a marker of which compartment?
        Lactate dehydrogenase of which?
  \item Compute the recovery of protein: the sum over the fractions as
        a percentage of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{homogenate}. Comment.
  \item Compute the recovery of each enzyme activity in the same way.
  \item Compute the protein content per gram of liver and the fraction
        of the liver's fresh mass that is protein.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Stokes' law.}
Mitochondria: radius \qty{0.5}{\micro m}, density excess
\qty{100}{kg/m^3}. Nuclei: radius \qty{4}{\micro m}, same excess.
Ribosomes: radius \qty{12}{nm}, density excess \qty{600}{kg/m^3}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the sedimentation speed of a mitochondrion at
        $\num{10000}\,g$ and the time to travel \qty{5}{cm}. Is
        \qty{20}{min} enough?
  \item Compute the distance a mitochondrion travels during the first
        spin ($1000\,g$, \qty{10}{min}). What fraction of the
        mitochondria, uniformly distributed at the start, would be
        pelleted in the first step?
  \item Compare with the \qty{15}{\%} of succinate dehydrogenase found
        in pellet 1. What else can drag mitochondria into pellet 1?
  \item Compute the time for a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{nucleus} to travel \qty{5}{cm} at
        $1000\,g$.
  \item Compute the time for a ribosome to travel \qty{5}{cm} at
        $\num{100000}\,g$. Would a ribosome be pelleted at
        $\num{10000}\,g$ in \qty{20}{min}?
  \item Explain why the sequence of spins is in increasing force and
        why each step must be long enough but not too long.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Specific activity and purification.}
The specific activity of an enzyme in a fraction is its activity per
milligram of protein. The purification factor of a fraction for an
enzyme is its specific activity divided by that in the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{homogenate}; the
yield is the fraction's activity divided by the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{homogenate}'s.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the specific activity of succinate dehydrogenase in the
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{homogenate} and in each fraction.
  \item Compute the purification factor and the yield of succinate
        dehydrogenase in pellet 2.
  \item Compute the purification factor of succinate dehydrogenase in
        pellet 3 and interpret its value.
  \item Compute the specific activity of lactate dehydrogenase in the
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{homogenate} and in the final supernatant, and the corresponding
        purification factor and yield.
  \item Why is the purification factor of the cytosolic enzyme lower
        than that of the mitochondrial enzyme although its yield is
        higher?
  \item Pellet 2 contains \qty{12}{U} of lactate dehydrogenase. Is the
        enzyme partly mitochondrial? Propose a better explanation and a
        way to test it.
  \item What fraction of the liver's protein is mitochondrial, if all
        the succinate dehydrogenase is mitochondrial and pellet 2 is
        \qty{60}{\%} pure? (Hint: use the specific activity of pure
        mitochondria, deduced from pellet 2, and the total activity.)
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Checking the fractions.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Pellet 2 is examined by electron microscopy: which instrument
        and preparation, and what should be seen?
  \item How would you check pellet 1 for unbroken \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, and what would
        you do if you found many?
  \item Pellet 2 is resuspended and layered on a sucrose density
        gradient, then spun until each particle stops where its density
        equals the medium's. Mitochondria band at \qty{1.18}{g/mL},
        lysosomes at \qty{1.22}{g/mL}, peroxisomes at \qty{1.24}{g/mL}.
        Explain why this separation works when \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:fractionation}{differential
        centrifugation} could not achieve it.
  \item Acid phosphatase activity in pellet 2 rises sharply when a
        detergent is added to the assay. Explain, and say what this
        tells about the state of the lysosomes in the pellet.
  \item A student homogenises in pure water instead of isotonic
        sucrose. Predict the effect on pellet 2 and on the distribution
        of succinate dehydrogenase and acid phosphatase.
  \item Summarise the result: the purification factor and the yield of
        the mitochondrial fraction, and what limits each.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
