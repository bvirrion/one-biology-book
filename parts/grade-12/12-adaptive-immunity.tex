\chapter{Adaptive Immunity and Vaccination}\label{ch:g12:adaptive-immunity}

In 1796 a country doctor scratched matter from a milkmaid's cowpox
blister into the arm of a boy, waited six weeks, and then inoculated
him with smallpox. The boy did not fall ill. Nothing in the doctor's
world explained why; the body, it seemed, had been taught. Two
centuries later smallpox is extinct, and the teaching is understood
down to the molecules: a set of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that carry, between them, a
receptor for almost any molecule that could ever exist, that
multiply when their molecule appears, and that remember. This chapter
describes that system, its two arms, its memory, the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}{vaccines} built on
it --- and the virus that destroys it.

\section{Antigens and lymphocytes}

\begin{definition}[Antigen, lymphocyte]\label{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}
An \emph{antigen}\index{antigen} is any molecule that the adaptive
\omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:immunity}{immune system} can recognise: typically a \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} or sugar of a
microbe, a toxin, a foreign \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s surface --- or, in error, one of
the body's own. The recognising \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} are the
\emph{lymphocytes}\index{lymphocyte}, white \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} made in the bone
marrow and gathered in the lymph nodes, the spleen and the lymphoid
tissue of the gut. Each lymphocyte carries thousands of copies of one
\emph{receptor}, and every receptor binds one antigen only. The
\emph{B lymphocytes} mature in the marrow and will make \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies};
the \emph{T lymphocytes} mature in the thymus and act by contact and
by signals.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[A repertoire made in advance]\label{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:repertoire}
The receptor of each \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{lymphocyte} is assembled, while the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} matures,
by a random rearrangement of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} segments, different in every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}.
The body thus holds some $10^{8}$ different receptors before ever
meeting an \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} --- enough that nearly any molecule finds a few
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that bind it. \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{Lymphocytes} whose receptor binds the body's own
molecules are eliminated during maturation; the rest wait, a few
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} for each possible \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen}, until their \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} arrives.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Clonal selection}

\begin{proposition}[Selection and expansion]\label{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:selection}
When an \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} enters, only the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{lymphocytes} whose receptor fits it
respond: they are \emph{selected}, and they multiply by \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:cycle}{mitosis} into
a clone of thousands of identical \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} over several days ---
\emph{clonal selection}\index{clonal selection}. The clone then
differentiates: most \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} become \emph{effector \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}} that fight the
\omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} for a week or two and die; a minority become long-lived
\emph{memory cells}\index{memory cell}, which persist for years and,
if the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} returns, respond faster and in far greater numbers. The
response is specific because only the fitting clones expand; it is
slow the first time because they start from a handful of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}; it is
fast the second time because the \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:selection}{memory cells} are already many.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
\omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{Antibodies} against a given \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} appear in the blood about a week
after a first injection, rise for two weeks and decline; a second
injection months later produces, within two or three days, \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies}
at ten to a hundred times the first peak, which persist for years.
Injecting a second, unrelated \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} at the same time as the second
dose gives, for that \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen}, a slow first-type response: the memory
is specific. \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{Lymphocytes} taken from an immunised animal and
transferred to a naive one transfer the memory; those from a
non-immunised animal do not.
\end{proof}

\section{Two arms: antibodies and killer cells}

\begin{proposition}[B lymphocytes and antibodies]\label{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}
A selected B clone differentiates into \emph{plasma \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}}, factories
that secrete thousands of \emph{antibodies}\index{antibody} per second:
soluble copies of the clone's receptor, Y-shaped \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} whose two
arms each bind the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen}. \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{Antibodies} do not kill; they \emph{mark}.
Bound to a virus or a toxin they block it from entering \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} or
acting; bound to a bacterium they coat it, and the coat is what the
\omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}{phagocytes} of \cref{ch:g12:innate-immunity} grip best. \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{Antigens}
cross-linked by many \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies} clump into \emph{immune complexes},
which are cleared by \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}{phagocytosis}. This is the arm against intruders
in the blood and fluids.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{proposition}[T lymphocytes]\label{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:tcells}
T \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{lymphocytes} recognise \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} only as fragments displayed on the
surface of another \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} (\cref{ch:g12:innate-immunity}). Two kinds:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Helper T cells}\index{helper T cell} are activated by
        the \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:handover}{dendritic cells} that bring the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} to the lymph node;
        once selected and multiplied, they secrete cytokines that
        authorise and amplify the response of the B \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} and of the
        other T \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. They are the coordinators; without them, almost
        no adaptive response occurs.
  \item \emph{Cytotoxic T \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}} recognise fragments of a virus (or
        of a \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}) displayed by an infected \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, and kill
        that \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} by contact, before the virus inside it can multiply.
        This is the arm against intruders hidden inside \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, where
        \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies} cannot reach.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[A virus, met twice]\label{ex:g12:adaptive-immunity:virus}
First influenza: the virus multiplies for three days before the
adaptive response is ready; by day 7 cytotoxic T \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} are killing
infected \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} in the airways and \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies} are neutralising free
virus; by day 10 the person is recovering, with \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:selection}{memory cells} of both
kinds. The same strain a year later: \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies} already in the blood
block most of the virus at entry, \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:selection}{memory cells} expand within two days,
and the infection is over before it is noticed. A different strain
whose surface \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} have mutated escapes the \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies} --- which
is why the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}{vaccine} is remade every year.
\end{example}

\section{Vaccination}

\begin{definition}[Vaccine]\label{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}
A \emph{vaccine}\index{vaccine} is a preparation that provokes a
primary adaptive response, and hence memory, against a pathogen
without causing its disease: the pathogen weakened (measles,
tuberculosis), killed (polio by injection), reduced to one of its
\omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} or an inactivated toxin (tetanus, hepatitis B), or, most
recently, to the instructions for the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} to make that \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}. An
\emph{adjuvant} added to the preparation provokes the small
inflammation without which the \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:handover}{dendritic cells} would not carry the
\omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} to the lymph node. Booster doses turn the primary response
into a secondary one and extend the memory for decades.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Protecting the group]\label{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:herd}
A vaccinated person is protected; a vaccinated \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} protects
its unvaccinated members too. An infected person passes a disease, on
average, to $R_0$ others in a \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} with no immunity (about 15
for measles, 2 to 3 for influenza); if a fraction $v$ is immune, only
$R_0(1 - v)$ of those contacts can be infected, and the disease
recedes when that number falls below 1 --- that is, when
\[
v > 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}.
\]
For measles, 93\% of the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} must be immune to protect the
infants too young for the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}{vaccine} and the people who cannot receive
it; for influenza, about 60\%. This \emph{herd immunity}\index{herd immunity}
is why vaccination is a collective act.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{When the coordinator is destroyed}

\begin{proposition}[HIV and AIDS]\label{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:hiv}
The human immunodeficiency virus, HIV, infects the \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:tcells}{helper T cells},
entering through the very receptor that marks them, and multiplies
in them. For years the \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:immunity}{immune system} kills infected \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} and makes
\omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies} --- the person is \emph{seropositive}, and infectious ---
while the virus, mutating constantly, escapes each response and slowly
depletes the helper \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. When their count falls below about a fifth
of normal, the adaptive response can no longer be coordinated:
\emph{AIDS}\index{AIDS}, in which infections that a healthy body
clears without noticing --- a fungus of the mouth, a mild parasite of
the lungs --- become fatal, and rare \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers} appear. Antiviral drugs
block the virus's \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes}, restore the helper \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} and, taken for
life, hold the disease off and stop transmission; there is no \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}{vaccine}
yet, and no cure.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Reading an immune response]\label{met:g12:adaptive-immunity:read}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} and where it is: free in the fluids
        (\omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies} act) or inside \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} (cytotoxic T \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} act).
  \item Read the timing: a week to a peak means a primary response, a
        few days means memory.
  \item Read the specificity: a response to one \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} leaves the
        response to another untouched.
  \item Locate the \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:tcells}{helper T cells}: without them nothing happens, which
        is what \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:hiv}{AIDS} demonstrates.
  \item For a \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}{vaccine}, name the form of the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen}, the adjuvant, the
        number of doses, and the fraction of the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} needed.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Selection, once more]\label{rem:g12:adaptive-immunity:selection}
The adaptive \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:immunity}{immune system} is Darwinian: a \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} with
random receptors, of which the environment --- the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:antigen}{antigen} --- selects
a few to multiply, and whose descendants inherit the winning
receptor. It runs the process of \cref{ch:g12:selection-drift-speciation}
inside one body in a week, and, through memory, keeps its results for
a lifetime. It is also the reason the \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:immunity}{immune system} can be taught,
which is what a \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}{vaccine} does, and why a virus that mutates faster
than the system can select --- HIV, influenza --- is so hard to beat.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Ninety-Three Per Cent}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a vaccination campaign reckoned:
the responses to two doses, the arithmetic of herd immunity, a
measles outbreak modelled, and the virus that undoes the system}]
\label{pb:g12:adaptive-immunity:1}
Measles has $R_0 = 15$. A town of 50\,000 people has 90\% of its
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} immune by vaccination or past infection; 1500 are infants
under one year, too young for the \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}{vaccine}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- One person's response.}
A child receives the first dose at 12 months and the second at 18.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Describe the \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibody} curve after the first dose: delay, peak,
        decline.
  \item Describe it after the second dose and explain the difference
        with \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:selection}{clonal selection}.
  \item The \omterm{def:g12:adaptive-immunity:vaccine}{vaccine} virus is weakened but alive. Why is a living virus
        used, and which arm of the response does it train that a
        killed one would not?
  \item Ten years later the child meets measles. What happens in the
        first three days, and why does she not fall ill?
  \item Her unvaccinated cousin meets the same virus. Describe his
        first ten days, and what he keeps afterwards.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The threshold.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the fraction of the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} that must be immune for
        measles to stop spreading.
  \item With 90\% immune, how many people can one infected person
        infect on average? Is the town protected?
  \item How many people in the town are not immune, and how many of
        them are the infants?
  \item If coverage rose to 95\%, how many susceptible people would
        remain, and what would one case produce on average?
  \item Explain in one sentence why the infants' safety depends on the
        adults' decisions.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- An outbreak.}
A traveller brings measles into the town at 90\% immunity.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Take each case to infect $15 \times 0.10 = 1.5$ others, in
        successive generations of 12 days. How many cases in the
        third generation? In the sixth?
  \item How many cases in all after six generations?
  \item Of the 5000 susceptible people, 30\% are infants. Estimate the
        number of infants among the cases after six generations, if
        cases fall evenly on the susceptible.
  \item Each generation of cases leaves immune survivors: explain why
        the outbreak slows on its own, and roughly when.
  \item Repeat question 11 for a town at 95\% immunity. Compare the
        two towns.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The system undone.}
A patient infected with HIV, untreated, loses \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:tcells}{helper T cells} from
1000 to 200 per microlitre in 8 years.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the average loss per year, and per month.
  \item At 200 per microlitre, \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:hiv}{AIDS} begins. Explain, with the
        organisation figure, why both \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:antibodies}{antibodies} and killer \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} are
        affected although only helper \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} are infected.
  \item The patient's measles \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:selection}{memory cells} are intact. Would a measles
        exposure now be dangerous? Explain.
  \item Antiviral treatment started at year 8 raises the count back to
        600 within two years. What does this show about the \omterm{prop:g12:adaptive-immunity:selection}{memory
        cells} and the repertoire, and why must the treatment continue?
  \item State the result: the coverage that protects the town's
        infants, the number of cases one traveller seeds in six
        generations at 90\%, and the one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} whose loss brings the
        whole adaptive system down.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
