\chapter{Plant Adaptations and Phenotypic Plasticity}\label{ch:b2:plant-plasticity}

Two leaves from the same oak: the one from the top of the crown is
small, thick and leathery, the one from the shaded interior three
times as large and thin as paper. A seedling of the same oak under a
closed canopy grows tall and spindly; its sibling in a clearing stays
short and stout. Neither difference is genetic. A plant cannot walk
to a better place, so it builds itself to fit the place it has: it
senses light, gravity, touch, water, salt, cold, and it bends,
thickens, elongates, closes its pores or hardens its cells
accordingly. This chapter is about that plasticity --- how a plant
reads its surroundings and what it does about them --- and about
the heritable \omterm{def:b2:plant-plasticity:plasticity}{adaptations} by which lineages have fitted themselves
to deserts, shade, salt and frost.

\section{Plasticity and its limits}

\begin{definition}[Phenotypic plasticity and the norm of reaction]\label{def:b2:plant-plasticity:plasticity}
\index{phenotypic plasticity}\index{norm of reaction}\index{acclimation}\index{adaptation}
\emph{Phenotypic plasticity} is the capacity of one \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype} to
produce different \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{phenotypes} in different environments. The
\emph{norm of reaction} of a \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype} is the curve of its \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{phenotype}
against an environmental variable --- leaf thickness against light,
stem length against temperature; \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotypes} differ in their norms,
and the differences are heritable even when the plasticity itself
is not. \emph{Acclimation} is the reversible, physiological form:
a leaf that thickens its cuticle in a dry week, a cell that makes
antifreeze in autumn. \emph{Adaptation}, in the evolutionary sense,
is the heritable fit of a lineage to its habitat, selected over
generations; a cactus is adapted to drought, a bean acclimates to a
dry spell. Plants are more plastic than animals because they are
modular and indeterminate (\cref{ch:b2:plant-meristems}): the next
leaf can be built differently from the last, and the sessile life
makes plasticity the only way to move.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Sun leaves and shade leaves]\label{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:sunshade}
\index{sun leaf}\index{shade leaf}
A leaf developing in full sun is small, thick, with two or three
layers of palisade cells, many stomata, a thick cuticle, a high
concentration of the carbon-fixing enzyme and a high maximal rate of
photosynthesis; a leaf of the same plant developing in shade is
large, thin, with one palisade layer, more chlorophyll per unit
mass, a low respiration rate and a low light-compensation point. The
\omterm{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:sunshade}{shade leaf} is built to harvest every photon at minimal cost, the
\omterm{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:sunshade}{sun leaf} to use a flood of them without overheating or wilting. The
decision is made while the leaf is still a primordium, from the
light --- and in particular its colour --- that reaches the bud;
a mature leaf cannot rebuild itself, which is why a plant moved from
shade to sun scorches until it has made new leaves.
\end{proposition}

\section{Reading the light}

\begin{theorem}[Phytochrome as a light-quality meter]\label{thm:b2:plant-plasticity:phytochrome}
\index{phytochrome}\index{red:far-red ratio}\index{shade avoidance}
Phytochrome exists in two forms: $P_r$, which absorbs red light
(\qty{660}{nm}) and is converted to $P_{fr}$, and $P_{fr}$, which
absorbs far-red (\qty{730}{nm}) and is converted back --- and it is
$P_{fr}$ that acts. In continuous light the two conversions balance
at a \emph{photoequilibrium} whose position depends only on the
ratio of red to far-red in the light:
\[
\varphi = \frac{P_{fr}}{P_{r} + P_{fr}} = \frac{R}{R + a\,FR},
\]
where $R$ and $FR$ are the photon flux densities in the two bands
and $a$ a constant of the pigment ($a \approx 0.77$). Sunlight has
$R{:}FR \approx 1.15$, giving $\varphi \approx 0.6$; light filtered
through leaves, which absorb red and transmit far-red, has $R{:}FR
\approx 0.2$ and $\varphi \approx 0.2$. A low $\varphi$ tells a
plant it is under or beside other plants before it is actually
shaded --- far-red reflected from a neighbour is detected on the
side facing it --- and it responds with the \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:plant-plasticity:phytochrome}{shade avoidance}
syndrome}: faster stem elongation, longer petioles, leaves held
upward, less branching, earlier flowering. A seedling in a closed
canopy doubles its rate of elongation.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $k_1 R$ be the rate constant for $P_r \to P_{fr}$ (proportional
to the red flux) and $k_2\,FR$ that for $P_{fr} \to P_r$. At
equilibrium $k_1 R\,P_r = k_2\,FR\,P_{fr}$, so $P_{fr}/P_r = k_1 R/
(k_2 FR)$ and $\varphi = P_{fr}/(P_r + P_{fr}) = R/(R + a\,FR)$ with
$a = k_2/k_1$. (Both forms absorb somewhat in both bands, which the
constant $a$ absorbs; the form of the result is unchanged.) With
$a = 0.77$: $1.15/(1.15 + 0.89) = 0.56$; $0.2/(0.2 + 0.77) = 0.21$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Phototropism]\label{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:phototropism}
\index{phototropism}\index{tropism}\index{Cholodny--Went hypothesis}
A \emph{tropism} is a growth movement oriented by a directional
stimulus. In \emph{phototropism} a shoot bends toward blue light:
the light is sensed at the tip by a blue-light receptor
(phototropin), the auxin descending from the tip is redirected
toward the shaded side --- its carriers move to the lateral membrane
--- and the shaded side, receiving more auxin, elongates faster
than the lit side, so the shoot curves toward the light. The
bending is differential growth, not a muscle: it occurs only in
the \omterm{prop:b2:plant-meristems:ram}{elongation zone}, takes an hour or two, and is irreversible.
Roots, in which the same auxin excess \emph{inhibits} elongation,
bend away.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Darwin (1880) showed with grass coleoptiles that the tip perceives
the light and the zone below it bends: covering the tip with an
opaque cap abolished the bending, covering the base with a tube did
not, and a transparent cap left it intact. Boysen-Jensen (1913) cut
the tip off and replaced it with a block of gelatin between: bending
persisted, so the signal was a diffusible substance; a sheet of mica
inserted on the shaded side blocked it, on the lit side did not, so
the substance travelled down the shaded side. Went (1928) collected
the substance in agar from cut tips and found that a block placed
off-centre on a decapitated coleoptile in the dark made it bend
away from the block --- by an angle proportional to the amount ---
and, collecting agar from the two halves of a unilaterally lit tip,
found about two thirds of the auxin on the shaded side and one third
on the lit side (Briggs, 1957, with better methods). The lateral
redistribution of a growth hormone is the \omterm{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:phototropism}{Cholodny--Went
hypothesis}, and it has held.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[How much a stem bends]\label{thm:b2:plant-plasticity:bend}
If the shaded side of a stem of diameter $d$ elongates by a relative
amount $\varepsilon_s$ and the lit side by $\varepsilon_l$ over a
growing zone of length $L$, the zone curves through an angle
\[
\theta = \frac{L\,(\varepsilon_s - \varepsilon_l)}{d}
\]
(in radians). A coleoptile growing at \qty{5}{\%} an hour over a
zone of \qty{10}{mm} and \qty{1}{mm} across, with auxin split
$65 : 35$ so that the two sides grow at $1.3$ and $0.7$ times the
mean, bends in three hours by $\theta = 10\times(0.195 - 0.105)/1 =
0.9$ radians, about \qty{50}{\degree}. Bending needs no new
mechanism: the ordinary growth of \cref{ch:b2:plant-meristems}, made
unequal on two sides, is enough.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The two sides of the zone become arcs of radii $\rho + d/2$ and
$\rho - d/2$ subtending the same angle $\theta$, of lengths $L(1 +
\varepsilon_s)$ and $L(1 + \varepsilon_l)$; their difference is
$\theta d$, so $\theta = L(\varepsilon_s - \varepsilon_l)/d$. Over
three hours at \qty{5}{\%} an hour the mean extension is $0.15$;
$1.3\times 0.15 = 0.195$ and $0.7\times 0.15 = 0.105$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Gravitropism]\label{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:gravitropism}
\index{gravitropism}\index{statolith}
A shoot laid on its side turns upward and a root downward within
hours. The direction of gravity is sensed by \emph{statoliths} ---
dense starch grains in specialised cells of the \omterm{prop:b2:plant-meristems:ram}{root cap} and of the
stem's starch sheath --- which settle to the lower side of the cell
in seconds and, by pressing on the membrane or the endoplasmic
reticulum, redirect the auxin carriers so that auxin accumulates
on the lower side. In the shoot the lower side grows faster and the
stem curves up; in the root the same auxin excess inhibits the lower
side, the upper side grows faster, and the root curves down. Remove
the \omterm{prop:b2:plant-meristems:ram}{root cap} and the root grows straight in any direction; starch-free
mutants sense gravity poorly; a root on a slowly rotating drum
(a clinostat), which never lets the statoliths settle, grows
without direction. The response is a second use of the
Cholodny--Went mechanism, with a different sensor.
\end{proposition}

\section{Water: closing the pores}

\begin{proposition}[Stomatal control]\label{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:stomata}
\index{stoma}\index{guard cell}\index{abscisic acid}\index{stomatal conductance}
The leaf's pores, the \emph{stomata}, are each bounded by two
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:stomata}{guard cells}} whose walls are thickened unevenly, so that when
they take up potassium and water and swell, they bow apart and open
the pore, and when they lose them they close it. They open in the
morning under blue light and low internal $\mathrm{CO_2}$, and
close in the dark; they close within minutes when \emph{\omterm{def:b2:plant-flowering:dormancy}{abscisic
acid}} arrives --- made in roots that sense drying soil and carried
up in the xylem, or made in the leaf itself as it loses turgor ---
and when the air is very dry. The \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:stomata}{stomatal conductance}} $g_s$
sets both the water lost, $E = g_s\,(w_i - w_a)$ (the difference in
water vapour mole fraction between the leaf interior, saturated, and
the air), and the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ taken in, $A = g_s\,(c_a -
c_i)/1.6$ (the factor $1.6$ being the ratio of the diffusivities of
water vapour and $\mathrm{CO_2}$). The leaf trades water for carbon
through one valve, and its \emph{water-use efficiency} is
\[
\frac{A}{E} = \frac{c_a - c_i}{1.6\,(w_i - w_a)},
\]
a few thousandths of a mole of carbon per mole of water: a typical
C$_3$ leaf spends \qtyrange{400}{600}{g} of water per gram of carbon
fixed, a C$_4$ leaf half that, a \omterm{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:xerophytes}{CAM plant} a fifth.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The cost of a day's carbon]\label{ex:b2:plant-plasticity:wue}
At midday a sunflower leaf with $g_s = \qty{0.3}{mol.m^{-2}.s^{-1}}$,
$w_i - w_a = 0.02$ and $c_a - c_i = \qty{120}{\micro mol/mol}$
transpires $E = \qty{6}{mmol.m^{-2}.s^{-1}}$ and fixes $A =
0.3\times 120/1.6 = \qty{22}{\micro mol.m^{-2}.s^{-1}}$: $A/E =
\qty{3.7e-3}{}$, i.e.\ 270 molecules of water for each carbon atom,
\qty{400}{g} of water per gram of carbon. A hectare of such leaves
loses \qty{40}{t} of water on a summer day to fix \qty{100}{kg} of
carbon. When the soil dries, \omterm{def:b2:plant-flowering:dormancy}{abscisic acid} closes the stomata to a
tenth of this conductance: $E$ falls tenfold, $A$ less than that
(since $c_i$ falls too), and the plant survives on a starvation
diet rather than dying of thirst.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Adaptations to drought]\label{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:xerophytes}
\index{xerophyte}\index{CAM plant}\index{succulent}
Plants of dry places (\emph{xerophytes}) combine heritable
structures and strategies. Reduce the loss: thick cuticle, stomata
sunk in pits or under hairs (which thicken the still air layer),
leaves reduced to spines (cactus) or shed in the dry season, small
thick leaves that roll. Store: succulent stems and leaves with
water-holding mucilage. Reach: roots \qty{50}{m} deep (mesquite) or
spreading just under the surface to catch a shower. Shift: \omterm{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:xerophytes}{CAM
plants} open their stomata at night, when $w_i - w_a$ is small, fix
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ into malic acid, and refix it behind closed stomata
by day --- the same carbon at a fifth of the water. Escape: desert
annuals live as \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} for years and complete a \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{life cycle} in the
weeks after rain. Tolerate: resurrection plants lose \qty{95}{\%} of
their water and revive. Each strategy has a price --- CAM is slow,
succulence is heavy, spines cannot photosynthesise --- and the
plant that pays it is outgrown wherever water is plentiful.
\end{proposition}

\section{Cold, salt and the stress response}

\begin{proposition}[Acclimation to stress]\label{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:stress}
\index{cold acclimation}\index{halophyte}\index{heat shock protein}
A week at \qty{4}{\celsius} lowers the temperature that kills a
winter rye from \qty{-5}{\celsius} to \qty{-25}{\celsius}: the
cells have loaded sugars and proteins that lower the freezing point
and protect membranes, changed their lipids to stay fluid, and made
antifreeze proteins that stop ice crystals growing; the killing
event is not the ice, which forms harmlessly between cells, but the
dehydration of the cell as water leaves it to join the ice. A few
hours at \qty{40}{\celsius} induce \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:plant-plasticity:stress}{heat shock proteins}} that
refold denatured proteins and protect the cell at
\qty{45}{\celsius}. Salt is met by exclusion at the root,
compartmentation into the vacuole (with compatible solutes in the
cytosol to balance it), and, in true \emph{halophytes}, secretion
through salt glands. Most of these responses pass through the same
signalling: a sensor, a rise in cytosolic calcium, \omterm{def:b2:plant-flowering:dormancy}{abscisic acid},
and a set of \omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factors} that switch on hundreds of
protective genes --- a stress programme as general as the immune
response of an animal, and, like it, costly: the acclimated plant
grows slowly, and a plant that acclimates when it need not loses to
one that does not.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
\omterm{def:b2:plant-plasticity:plasticity}{Cold-acclimation} experiments measure the temperature at which half
the cells of a leaf die (the LT$_{50}$) before and after a period at
low temperature; the shift, of ten to twenty degrees in a week, is
reversed by a week of warmth. Mutants unable to switch on the cold
programme (the CBF \omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factors}) acclimate poorly, and
plants expressing those factors constitutively survive frost
without \omterm{def:b2:plant-plasticity:plasticity}{acclimation} --- but grow as dwarfs.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Cues that lie]\label{ex:b2:plant-plasticity:lie}
Plasticity is only as good as the cue. A seedling under a neighbour's
far-red shade elongates --- correctly, if the neighbour is a seedling
it can overtop; fatally, if it is a tree, since the reserves spent
on stem are lost to root and leaf. Crops planted densely shade each
other, elongate, and lodge; breeders have selected wheats and rices
that ignore the far-red signal. A warm week in February brings out
leaves that a March frost kills; the plants that survive are those
that also count the cold (\cref{ch:b2:plant-flowering}). Every
plastic response is a bet on the cue's honesty, and the \omterm{def:b2:plant-plasticity:plasticity}{norm of
reaction} is shaped by how often, in the lineage's past, the cue told
the truth.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: A Seedling Under the Canopy}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a seedling's light climate,
elongation, water budget and tropisms computed from the physics of
its sensors, ending on its phytochrome state, its height after a
fortnight, and its daily water use}]
\label{pb:b2:plant-plasticity:1}
A birch seedling germinates under a canopy that transmits
\qty{3}{\%} of the sunlight and lowers $R{:}FR$ from $1.15$ to $0.2$
($a = 0.77$). In full sun it would elongate at \qty{1.5}{cm} a day;
under the canopy its elongation rate is multiplied by $1 +
2(0.6 - \varphi)$. Its stem is \qty{2}{mm} across with a
\qty{15}{mm} growing zone. Leaves: \qty{20}{cm^2} in all; $g_s =
\qty{0.2}{mol.m^{-2}.s^{-1}}$; $w_i - w_a = 0.015$ in the shade,
$0.03$ in a gap; $c_a - c_i = \qty{80}{\micro mol/mol}$; the day
lasts \qty{14}{h}. Statoliths: radius \qty{2.5}{\micro m}, density
\qty{1500}{kg/m^3}, in cytoplasm of \qty{1100}{kg/m^3} and
\qty{0.01}{Pa.s}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The light.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute $\varphi$ in the open and under the canopy.
  \item What does the seedling conclude from $\varphi$, and what
        would it conclude if the canopy transmitted \qty{3}{\%} of
        the light without changing its colour (a shade cloth)?
  \item Compute the elongation rate under the canopy.
  \item How tall is the seedling after 14 days there, against a
        sibling in the open?
  \item A gap opens on one side, raising $R{:}FR$ on that side to
        $0.8$. What two responses follow, and by which two
        photoreceptors?
  \item Why are leaves developed under the canopy larger and
        thinner, and what happens to them if the canopy is felled?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Bending toward the gap.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Light from the gap splits the auxin $65 : 35$ across the
        stem. With the canopy elongation rate of question 3, compute
        the relative extension of each side of the growing zone in
        one hour.
  \item Compute the angle of bending after one hour.
  \item After how long does the stem point straight at the gap, if
        the gap is at \qty{60}{\degree} from vertical?
  \item The stem is now leaning. Compute the settling speed of a
        statolith and the time to cross a \qty{12}{\micro m} cell.
  \item The shoot's gravitropism and its phototropism now disagree.
        Which wins in a shade-avoiding plant, and why is that
        adaptive?
  \item A root of the same seedling is tilted \qty{45}{\degree} by a
        stone. Predict its response and explain why it is opposite
        to the shoot's although the mechanism is the same.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Water.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the transpiration rate per square metre in the shade,
        and the water lost by the seedling's leaves in a day.
  \item Compute the carbon fixed per square metre per second and per
        day, and the water-use efficiency in grams of water per gram
        of carbon.
  \item What is the seedling's whole daily carbon gain in the shade,
        in milligrams?
  \item Recompute the water lost and the efficiency in the gap.
  \item The soil dries and \omterm{def:b2:plant-flowering:dormancy}{abscisic acid} reduces $g_s$ to
        \qty{0.02}{mol.m^{-2}.s^{-1}}. Recompute $E$ and $A$
        (assume $c_a - c_i$ rises to \qty{200}{\micro mol/mol}).
        Which falls more, and why?
  \item The seedling has \qty{5}{g} of leaf tissue that is \qty{85}{\%}
        water and can lose a fifth of it before wilting. How long
        does it last in the gap with the stomata open if the roots
        supply nothing?
  \item Explain why a CAM strategy would not suit a birch seedling
        under a canopy.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The bet.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The seedling holds \qty{100}{mg} of reserves and spends
        \qty{2}{mg} per centimetre of stem beyond what its leaves
        supply in the shade. How much stem can it build on reserves
        alone?
  \item The canopy is a nettle bed \qty{40}{cm} above; then a wood
        \qty{8}{m} above. Say in each case whether elongation reaches
        light before the reserves run out, and what the better
        response would have been in the wood.
  \item A birch is a pioneer of open ground; a beech seedling can
        wait for decades in deep shade. Compare their expected norms
        of reaction to $\varphi$.
  \item A plant breeder wants a dense-planted crop that does not
        elongate. Which sensor or response would you disable, and
        what side effect must you check?
  \item Explain in two sentences why a plant that cannot sense its
        neighbours and a plant that always believes them are both
        worse off than one that sometimes does.
  \item State the result: $\varphi$ in sun and shade, the seedling's
        height after 14 days, and its daily water use in the shade
        and in the gap.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
