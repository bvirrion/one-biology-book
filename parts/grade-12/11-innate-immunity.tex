\chapter{Innate Immunity}\label{ch:g12:innate-immunity}

A splinter goes into a fingertip and is pulled out within the minute.
By evening the spot is red, warm, swollen and throbbing; by the next
day a bead of yellow pus has formed under the skin; by the third day it
is gone. Nothing was decided, nothing learnt: the same four signs
appear in every wound, in every person, in every mammal, and did so
long before anyone knew that a splinter carries bacteria. They are the
visible surface of the body's first line of defence --- a set of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
and molecules that recognise an intruder within minutes and attack it
without ever having met it. This chapter describes that line; the next
describes the slower, learning defence that it summons.

\section{Two immunities}

\begin{definition}[Innate and adaptive immunity]\label{def:g12:innate-immunity:immunity}
The \emph{immune system}\index{immune system} is the set of organs,
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} and molecules that defend the body against infection and
damage. It has two parts. \emph{Innate immunity}\index{innate immunity}
is present from birth, acts within minutes to hours, recognises broad
classes of intruders --- bacteria, viruses, fungi, damaged \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} --- by
features they share, and responds the same way every time.
\emph{Adaptive immunity}\index{adaptive immunity} takes days to act,
recognises each intruder individually, and remembers it
(\cref{ch:g12:adaptive-immunity}). The innate system is shared by all
animals; the adaptive one exists only in \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates}, and depends on
the innate one to be triggered.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Barriers first]\label{prop:g12:innate-immunity:barriers}
Most intruders never enter. The skin, dry, acid and shedding its
surface continuously, stops almost everything; the linings of the
airways, gut and reproductive tract are wet but coated with mucus that
traps microbes and is swept away or renewed; tears, saliva and mucus
carry an \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} that splits bacterial walls; the stomach's acid and the
gut's own bacteria, which occupy the space, leave little room for
newcomers. The immune response of this chapter begins when a barrier
is breached.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{The inflammatory response}

\begin{proposition}[The four signs and what makes them]\label{prop:g12:innate-immunity:inflammation}
A breach --- a wound, an infection, a burn --- provokes within minutes
the \emph{inflammatory response}\index{inflammation}: redness, heat,
swelling and pain. Each has a cause:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Sentinel \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}} resident in the tissue --- macrophages,
        \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:handover}{dendritic cells}, mast \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} --- carry receptors that recognise
        molecules common to whole classes of microbes (fragments of
        bacterial walls, viral \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{nucleic acids}) and molecules released by
        damaged \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. On recognition they release
        \emph{mediators}\index{inflammatory mediator}: histamine,
        prostaglandins, and signalling \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} called cytokines.
  \item The \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:inflammation}{mediators} widen the local blood vessels and make their
        walls leaky: more blood (redness, heat), and plasma seeping
        into the tissue (swelling). They also sensitise the nerve
        endings (pain), and make the vessel walls sticky for passing
        white \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}.
  \item \emph{\omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}{Phagocytes}} --- neutrophils from the blood within hours,
        monocytes that become macrophages within a day --- squeeze
        through the vessel walls, crawl up the gradient of \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:inflammation}{mediators}
        to the site, and engulf and digest the microbes and debris.
        Dead \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}{phagocytes}, bacteria and fluid are the pus.
\end{itemize}
The response is local, fast, and the same for a splinter as for a
bacterium: it needs no prior contact with the intruder.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Injecting a fragment of bacterial wall alone into the skin, without
any living bacterium, produces the full response within an hour: the
signal is a molecular pattern, not the microbe. Blocking histamine
(with the antihistamines of hay fever) abolishes the early redness and
swelling; blocking the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} that makes prostaglandins (with aspirin
or ibuprofen) reduces the swelling, the pain and the fever. Watching
a wound under the microscope shows neutrophils rolling along the
vessel wall, stopping, crossing it and swarming to the site within two
hours; mice whose neutrophils have been depleted die of infections a
normal mouse clears overnight.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Phagocytosis]\label{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}
\emph{Phagocytosis}\index{phagocytosis} is the engulfing of a
particle --- a bacterium, a dead \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, a speck of dust --- by a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}
that wraps its membrane around it, draws it inside in a vesicle, and
digests it with \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes} and reactive chemicals. The
\emph{phagocytes}\index{phagocyte} of the innate response are the
neutrophils, short-lived and numerous, and the macrophages, long-lived
and resident. A macrophage that has digested a microbe keeps
fragments of its \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} and displays them on its surface --- the
step that will connect this response to the next chapter's.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The timetable of a splinter]\label{ex:g12:innate-immunity:timetable}
Minute 0: bacteria from the splinter in the tissue. Minutes 1--10:
mast \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} and macrophages recognise their wall fragments and release
histamine and cytokines; vessels widen. Hour 1: redness, warmth, the
first neutrophils crossing the vessel walls. Hours 2--12: neutrophils
swarm, engulf bacteria, die; swelling and pain at their height. Day 1:
monocytes arrive and become macrophages, clearing debris; pus visible.
Day 2--3: bacteria gone, \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:inflammation}{mediators} no longer released, vessels return
to normal, repair begins. If the bacteria multiply faster than the
\omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}{phagocytes} clear them, the response widens, fever appears, and the
adaptive response --- already begun in the lymph node --- takes over.
\end{example}

\section{Calming the response}

\begin{proposition}[Anti-inflammatory drugs]\label{prop:g12:innate-immunity:drugs}
The \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammatory response} is useful and costly: its swelling and pain
are the price of clearing the intruder, and when it is excessive,
misdirected (against harmless pollen, or the body's own \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:joint}{joints}) or
prolonged, it does damage of its own. \emph{Anti-inflammatory
drugs}\index{anti-inflammatory drug} act on its \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:inflammation}{mediators}: aspirin,
ibuprofen and their relatives block the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} that makes
prostaglandins, reducing pain, swelling and fever; corticosteroids,
derived from a \omterm{def:g11:hormones-and-reproduction:hormone}{hormone} of the adrenal gland, switch off the production
of most \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:inflammation}{mediators} and are used for severe or chronic inflammation. All
of them reduce the signs; none of them removes the cause, and by
damping the response they can slow the clearing of a real infection.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Fever]\label{ex:g12:innate-immunity:fever}
Cytokines released at a large or persistent site of inflammation reach
the brain, where they raise the body's temperature set point: fever.
A degree or two of extra heat speeds the \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}{phagocytes} and slows many
bacteria, at the cost of energy and discomfort; above \qty{40}{\celsius}
the cost outweighs the benefit, which is where the antipyretic
(prostaglandin-blocking) drugs are used. Fever is a regulated
response, not a failure of regulation.
\end{example}

\section{From innate to adaptive}

\begin{proposition}[The hand-over]\label{prop:g12:innate-immunity:handover}
Among the sentinel \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, the \emph{dendritic cells}\index{dendritic cell}
have a second job. Having engulfed the intruder, they leave the tissue
through the lymph vessels, carrying its \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} fragments displayed on
their surface, and travel to the nearest \emph{lymph node}. There
they present the fragments to the lymphocytes of the adaptive system,
and the cytokines they carry tell those lymphocytes what kind of
intruder it was. The innate response thus not only fights the first
days; it identifies the enemy and delivers the description. Without
that hand-over, no adaptive response begins --- which is why a vaccine
contains, besides the antigen, substances that provoke a small
inflammation.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Reading an inflammation]\label{met:g12:innate-immunity:read}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Name the breach and the likely intruder (or the damage without
        intruder: a sprain inflames too).
  \item Attribute each sign to its mediator and vessel change:
        redness and heat to widened vessels, swelling to leaky ones,
        pain to sensitised nerves.
  \item Identify the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} present at each stage: sentinels first,
        neutrophils by hours, macrophages by a day; pus as their
        remains.
  \item Judge whether the response is proportionate: local and
        resolving in days (working as intended), spreading with fever
        (infection winning, adaptive response needed), or directed at
        something harmless or chronic (a case for anti-inflammatory
        treatment).
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Old, fast and blunt]\label{rem:g12:innate-immunity:old}
\omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:immunity}{Innate immunity} is the immunity of a sponge, an insect and an oak as
much as of a human: pattern receptors and \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}{phagocytes} are older than
the \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates} by hundreds of millions of years. It is fast because
it is prepared in advance for classes of enemy, and blunt for the
same reason: it cannot tell one bacterium from another, and it cannot
improve. The next chapter's system can do both --- but only once this
one has sounded the alarm.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Three Days of a Splinter}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a wound followed hour by hour:
the cells counted, the mediators traced, the drugs tested, and the
hand-over to the lymph node timed}]
\label{pb:g12:innate-immunity:1}
A splinter carries about $\num{10000}$ bacteria into a fingertip; they
divide every 40 minutes. The tissue holds 200 resident macrophages
within a millimetre of the wound; neutrophils begin to arrive from
the blood after 1 hour at a rate of $\num{50000}$ per hour, each
able to engulf about 20 bacteria before dying.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The race.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many bacteria would there be after 2 hours, then after 4,
        if nothing killed them?
  \item The 200 macrophages each engulf about 5 bacteria an hour. How
        many do they remove in the first hour, and does that stop the
        growth?
  \item From hour 1, neutrophils arrive. How many bacteria can the
        first hour's neutrophils remove in all?
  \item Compare with the bacterial \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} at hour 2. Who is
        winning?
  \item By what hour, roughly, is the wound cleared? What is left at
        the site?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The signs.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Attribute each of redness, heat, swelling and pain to a
        mediator and to a change in vessels or nerves.
  \item Explain why the swelling helps the neutrophils, and why it
        hurts.
  \item The fingertip throbs with each heartbeat. Explain from the
        widened vessels and the pressure in the swollen tissue.
  \item A day later a bead of pus has formed. List its contents.
  \item On day 3 the signs are gone. What stopped the release of the
        \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:inflammation}{mediators}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Drugs.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item An antihistamine is taken at hour 1. Which signs does it
        reduce, and which not?
  \item Ibuprofen is taken at hour 6. Which mediator does it block,
        and which signs does it reduce?
  \item A corticosteroid cream is applied. What happens to the
        recruitment of neutrophils, and what risk follows for the
        infection?
  \item Explain why none of the three drugs shortens the infection,
        and which one could lengthen it.
  \item The patient also has a fever of \qty{38.5}{\celsius}. Which
        molecules caused it, and what is it for?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The report.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{prop:g12:innate-immunity:handover}{Dendritic cells} that engulfed bacteria at hour 2 reach the
        elbow's lymph node at hour 20, displaying bacterial fragments.
        What do they carry besides the fragments, and to whom do they
        report?
  \item From the two-responses figure, how many days will pass before
        antibodies against these bacteria are abundant? Will they be
        needed for this splinter?
  \item The same bacteria enter through a second splinter a month
        later. Which of the two responses is different the second
        time, and which is identical?
  \item A person born without functional neutrophils survives with
        daily \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotics}. Explain which part of the defence the
        \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotics} replace and which they cannot.
  \item State the result: the hour at which the \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:phagocytosis}{phagocytes} overtake
        the bacteria, the mediator behind each of the four signs, and
        the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} that carries the report to the lymph node.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
