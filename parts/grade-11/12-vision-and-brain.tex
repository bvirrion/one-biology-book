\chapter{Vision and the Brain}\label{ch:g11:vision-and-brain}

After a stroke, a patient sees the world perfectly --- in black, white
and grey. Another sees colours and shapes but no movement: a car is
here, then there, with nothing in between, and pouring tea is
impossible because the stream never seems to move. A third cannot
recognise faces, including her own in a mirror, though she describes
every feature. Their eyes are intact. What each has lost is one
piece of the brain in which seeing is put together. This chapter
follows the optic nerve into the brain, maps what the \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{visual cortex}
does with the retina's signals, and shows that the map is not fixed:
it is built by experience, altered by learning, and disturbed by
drugs.

\section{From the retina to the cortex}

\begin{proposition}[The visual pathway]\label{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}
The million fibres of each optic nerve run back to the \emph{optic
chiasm}, where the fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross to
the other side while those from the temporal half stay on their own
side. Beyond the chiasm, each \emph{optic tract} therefore carries the
signals of the left halves of both retinas (right tract) or the right
halves (left tract) --- that is, everything seen in one half of the
visual field. The tracts relay in the \emph{thalamus} and reach the
\emph{primary visual cortex}\index{visual cortex}, at the back of
each hemisphere: the left hemisphere receives the right half of the
visual field, the right hemisphere the left half.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Cutting one optic nerve blinds one eye; damage to one optic tract, or
to one hemisphere's \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{visual cortex}, leaves both eyes working but blinds
both of them to the same half of the world --- the half opposite the
lesion. Stimulating a point of the \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{visual cortex} of an awake patient
during surgery makes them see a flash at a fixed position of the
field; neighbouring points give neighbouring flashes: the cortex holds
a map of the retina, the centre of the field occupying the largest
part.
\end{proof}

\section{Many areas, one perception}

\begin{proposition}[Specialised visual areas]\label{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:areas}
The \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{primary visual cortex} passes its analysis to a dozen further
\emph{visual areas}, each specialised: one extracts colour, one
movement, others shape, depth, faces, written words. Their outputs
are combined --- with memory, with the other senses --- into the
single, coherent perception we experience. The perception is a
construction: the brain fills the blind spot, completes hidden
contours, holds colours constant under changing light, and can be
fooled by illusions that exploit its rules.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Localised lesions dissociate the components: a patient with damage to
the colour area sees a world in grey with normal acuity; damage to the
movement area leaves colour and shape intact but abolishes the
perception of motion; damage to a region of the temporal lobe abolishes
the recognition of faces alone. Functional imaging of healthy
volunteers shows the same areas lighting up, one for coloured
patterns, another for moving dots, another for faces, while the
primary cortex responds to all. Electrodes in animals find, in each
area, neurons responding to that area's feature and to little else.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Three patients, three areas]\label{ex:g11:vision-and-brain:patients}
The patient who sees in grey has a lesion of the colour area on both
sides; her retina's \omterm{def:g11:the-eye:photoreceptors}{cones} work, and her primary cortex receives their
signals, but the stage that turns cone ratios into colour is gone.
The patient who sees no movement has lost the movement area: she sees
successive snapshots. The patient who cannot recognise faces has a
lesion of the face area of the temporal lobe; she recognises people
by voice or by a hat. In each case what is lost is precise, and what
remains shows that the other areas work on their own.
\end{example}

\section{A cortex built by experience}

\begin{proposition}[Plasticity]\label{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:plasticity}
The connections of the \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{visual cortex} are not fixed at birth. During a
\emph{critical period} of early life they are shaped by what the eyes
see; in the adult they continue to change, more slowly, with learning
and with use. This \emph{cerebral plasticity}\index{cerebral plasticity}
is a property of neurons: connections that are used are strengthened
and multiplied, connections that are not are weakened and pruned.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Hubel and Wiesel (1960s) recorded from the \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{visual cortex} of cats and
monkeys. In a normal animal most neurons respond to both eyes. In an
animal whose one eye was kept closed during its first months, almost
every neuron responds only to the open eye: the closed eye's
connections have been taken over. Closing the eye of an adult for the
same time changes nothing. In humans, a child whose one eye is
strongly out of focus or misaligned and not corrected before the age
of about seven keeps a permanently weak eye --- amblyopia --- however
good its optics later. In adults, learning to read Braille enlarges
the cortical area devoted to the reading finger; learning to juggle
enlarges the areas processing motion, and they shrink again if
practice stops.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Why the squint must be treated early]\label{ex:g11:vision-and-brain:amblyopia}
A child of three with one eye turned inward sees double, and the brain
suppresses the weaker image. If the eye is not straightened, or the
good eye not patched a few hours a day to force the weak one to work,
the cortex reorganises around the good eye during the critical period
and the weak eye, though optically perfect, stays blind to fine
detail for life. Treated at three, the child recovers full vision;
treated at twelve, hardly at all.
\end{example}

\section{Drugs and the seeing brain}

\begin{proposition}[Neurons, messengers, and molecules that mimic them]\label{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:drugs}
Neurons communicate at \emph{synapses} by releasing chemical
messengers, \emph{neurotransmitters}, onto the receptors of the next
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}; the final year describes the mechanism. Several
neurotransmitters, among them \emph{serotonin}, regulate the flow of
signals through the visual areas. Molecules whose shape resembles a
neurotransmitter can bind its receptors and disturb that flow:
lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) binds serotonin receptors in the
\omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{visual cortex} and produces hallucinations --- colours, shapes and
movements seen with no corresponding light. Such drugs can leave
lasting changes: episodes recurring months later, and in some people
persistent visual disturbances.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[What a hallucination shows]\label{ex:g11:vision-and-brain:hallucination}
Under LSD the retina is unchanged and the primary cortex still maps
what the eyes see; the disturbance is in the later areas and in how
they are combined. Patterns appear that look like the cortex's own
building blocks --- grids, spirals, tunnels --- because the drug sets
the machinery of perception running without its input. The
hallucination is a demonstration, by breakdown, that seeing is made by
the brain and that a molecule can change it.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Locating a visual deficit]\label{met:g11:vision-and-brain:locate}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item One eye blind, the other normal: the eye or its optic nerve,
        before the chiasm.
  \item Both eyes blind to the same half of the field: the opposite
        optic tract, thalamus or primary cortex.
  \item Both eyes blind to the outer halves of the field: the chiasm,
        where the crossing fibres are cut.
  \item Vision intact but one attribute lost (colour, motion, faces):
        the corresponding specialised area.
  \item Perception disturbed with normal eyes and cortex, transient: a
        drug or a metabolic cause.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[What this year has shown]\label{rem:g11:vision-and-brain:year}
The eye of \cref{ch:g11:the-eye} was a matter of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} and \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins}:
\omterm{def:g11:the-eye:photoreceptors}{opsins}, their duplications, a colour-blindness inherited on the X.
The brain of this chapter is a matter of connections, shaped by use.
Between them lies the whole subject of the year --- from the sequence
of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} to a trait, and from the trait to what a person can do ---
and the lesson that at every step the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} proposes and the
environment, from the temperature of a cat's ears to the light a
kitten's eye receives, disposes.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Four Patients and a Kitten}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- visual deficits located along the
pathway, a cortex mapped by stimulation, a kitten's deprivation
counted, and a child's squint treated in time}]
\label{pb:g11:vision-and-brain:1}
Four patients are examined. A: blind in the left eye, right eye
normal. B: both eyes blind to the left half of the field. C: both eyes
blind to the outer half of their field (left eye to the left, right
eye to the right). D: normal fields, normal acuity, but cannot
perceive movement.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Locating.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Locate A's lesion. Which structure, and before or after the
        chiasm?
  \item Locate B's lesion, and say why it must lie after the chiasm.
  \item Locate C's lesion precisely, explaining which fibres are cut.
  \item Locate D's lesion. What does the preservation of fields and
        acuity tell you about the primary cortex?
  \item For each patient, say whether the retina is intact, and how
        you would check.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The map.}
During surgery, stimulating points of a patient's right \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{visual cortex}
produces flashes. Stimulating the tip of the occipital lobe gives a
flash at the centre of the field; points further forward give flashes
further out in the left field; \qty{1}{cm} of cortex at the tip covers
about $2^\circ$ of the field, while \qty{1}{cm} further forward covers
$20^\circ$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Why are all the flashes in the left field?
  \item How much cortex represents the central $2^\circ$ compared with
        a $2^\circ$ band at $40^\circ$ from centre? Compute the ratio.
  \item Relate this ratio to the retina's fovea and periphery.
  \item A small lesion at the tip of the right occipital lobe: what
        does the patient lose? A lesion of the same size further
        forward?
  \item Why can the patient with the small central lesion still read,
        slowly, by moving the eyes?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The kitten.}
From the Hubel--Wiesel figure.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item In the normal kitten, what percentage of neurons respond to
        both eyes to some degree?
  \item In the deprived kitten, what percentage respond to the closed
        (left) eye at all?
  \item The closed eye's retina and optic nerve are intact. Where has
        its input gone, and what has taken the space?
  \item The same closure in an adult cat changes nothing. What does
        this establish, and what is its human counterpart?
  \item A kitten has both eyes closed for three months. Predict the
        histogram and the animal's vision afterwards.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The child.}
A child of four has a left eye turned inward; its optics are normal.
Untreated, the cortex takes over the right eye's input.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Explain, with the kitten, what will happen to the left eye's
        vision if nothing is done, and why glasses alone will not
        prevent it.
  \item The treatment is to patch the \emph{right} eye several hours a
        day for months. Explain why the good eye is covered.
  \item Why must this be done before about seven, and why does the
        same treatment at fifteen fail?
  \item An adult who lost an eye at thirty does not lose the cortex
        devoted to it in the same way. Reconcile this with the kitten.
  \item State the result: the four lesions located from front to back
        of the pathway, and the one property of the cortex --- named
        --- that makes the child's treatment work only in time.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
