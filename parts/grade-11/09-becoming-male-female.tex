\chapter{Becoming Male or Female}\label{ch:g11:becoming-male-female}

At seven weeks after fertilisation, a human embryo has a pair of
gonads that are neither testes nor ovaries, and two complete sets of
ducts side by side --- one that could become the male tract, one that
could become the female one. Nothing visible distinguishes an embryo
that will be a boy from one that will be a girl. Over the following
weeks one set of ducts grows and the other withers, the gonads take one
form or the other, and by the fourth month the anatomy is decided. The
decision is taken by a single \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, and carried out by two hormones.
This chapter follows that sequence, from the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} received at
fertilisation to the changes of \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:puberty}{puberty}.

\section{Sex chromosomes}

\begin{definition}[Sex chromosomes]\label{def:g11:becoming-male-female:chromosomes}
Of the 23 pairs of human \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, 22 are alike in the two sexes;
the 23rd pair is made of the \emph{sex chromosomes}\index{sex chromosomes}:
two X \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} in a female (XX), one X and one Y in a male (XY).
The Y is small, carries few \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, and is transmitted only from father
to son; every egg carries an X, and a sperm carries either an X or a
Y, so the father's sperm decides the chromosomal sex of the child, with
equal chances.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The undifferentiated stage]\label{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:undifferentiated}
Until the seventh week, embryos of both chromosomal sexes are
identical in their reproductive anatomy: a pair of undifferentiated
gonads, each beside two ducts --- the \emph{Wolffian duct}, the
precursor of the male tract (epididymis, vas deferens), and the
\emph{Müllerian duct}, the precursor of the female tract (oviduct,
uterus). Sexual development is the transformation of this common
plan into one of two forms; it is the differentiation of the gonad
that starts it.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{The gene that decides}

\begin{proposition}[SRY and the gonad]\label{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}
The Y \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} carries a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, \emph{SRY}\index{SRY gene}, expressed
in the undifferentiated gonad in the seventh week. Its \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}
switches on the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} that turn the gonad into a \emph{testis}. In
its absence --- in an XX embryo --- the gonad becomes an
\emph{ovary} some weeks later. The presence of \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY}, not the number of
X \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, decides the gonad.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Rare XY individuals whose \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY gene} carries a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} develop
ovaries and a female body; rare XX individuals who carry a fragment
of the Y bearing \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY}, transferred onto an X, develop testes and a
male body. Mice: an XX embryo into which the mouse \emph{Sry} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}
has been inserted develops as a male. One \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} of the Y is
sufficient, and necessary, to make a testis.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Hormones of the foetal testis]\label{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:hormones}
Once formed, the testis secretes two hormones. \emph{Testosterone}\index{testosterone}
maintains the Wolffian ducts and makes them develop into the male
internal tract, and shapes the external genitals in the male form.
\emph{Anti-Müllerian hormone} (AMH) makes the Müllerian ducts
regress. An ovary secretes neither at this stage: without
testosterone the Wolffian ducts regress, and without AMH the Müllerian
ducts become the oviducts, the uterus and the upper vagina. The female
tract is thus the form the ducts take in the absence of testicular
hormones.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Jost (1947) removed the gonads of rabbit foetuses in the womb before
differentiation: every foetus, XX or XY, developed a female tract.
Grafting a testis beside the gonads of a castrated foetus produced a
male tract; implanting a crystal of testosterone alone kept the
Wolffian ducts but did not remove the Müllerian ones, which persisted
beside them. So the testis makes two products --- one that builds the
male ducts (testosterone) and one, later identified as AMH, that
removes the female ones --- and the ovary is not needed for the
female tract to form.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The cascade in order]\label{ex:g11:becoming-male-female:cascade}
Fertilisation by a Y-bearing sperm; week 7, \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY} expressed, the gonad
becomes a testis; weeks 8 to 12, testosterone and AMH: Wolffian ducts
kept, Müllerian ducts gone, external genitals shaped male; then
quiescence until \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:puberty}{puberty}. Fertilisation by an X-bearing sperm: no
\omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY}; weeks 10 to 12, the gonad becomes an ovary; no testicular
hormones: Müllerian ducts develop, Wolffian ducts regress, external
genitals take the female form. Each step depends on the one before,
and a failure at any step changes everything downstream.
\end{example}

\section{Puberty}

\begin{proposition}[Puberty]\label{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:puberty}
The reproductive organs, formed before birth, remain inactive
through childhood. At \emph{puberty}\index{puberty} --- around eleven
to thirteen years, earlier in girls --- a centre in the brain begins to
release, in pulses, a hormone that makes the pituitary gland secrete
two hormones, LH and FSH, into the blood. These act on the gonads:
the testis begins to make sperm and to secrete testosterone in
adult amounts; the ovary begins its cycles and secretes oestrogens.
The sex hormones then produce the \emph{secondary sexual
characteristics}: growth of the reproductive organs, body hair,
voice, breasts, the distribution of muscle and fat, the growth spurt
and then the closure of the growth plates of the bones. \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:puberty}{Puberty} is
complete when the individual can reproduce.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Reading the curves]\label{ex:g11:becoming-male-female:curves}
Oestrogens rise from about 9 in girls, testosterone from about 11 in
boys; each reaches half its adult level within two years of starting
and its plateau two or three years later. The growth spurt follows the
rise by about a year, and the bones stop growing when the hormone
reaches adult level --- which is why girls, starting earlier, also stop
growing earlier and end shorter on average.
\end{example}

\section{Sex, in its several senses}

\begin{proposition}[Distinguishing the levels]\label{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:levels}
"Male" and "female" apply at several levels that usually agree but
are distinct:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{chromosomal} sex: XX or XY, fixed at fertilisation;
  \item \emph{gonadal} sex: ovary or testis, decided by \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY};
  \item \emph{anatomical} sex: the tracts and external genitals,
        shaped by the hormones of the foetal gonad, and the secondary
        characteristics, shaped at \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:puberty}{puberty};
  \item \emph{gender identity}, the sense a person has of being a man,
        a woman or neither, and \emph{sexual orientation}, whom a
        person is attracted to --- both facts about the person that
        are not deduced from the previous levels and are not chosen.
\end{itemize}
The biology of this chapter describes the first three; it neither
determines nor judges the last.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[When the levels disagree]\label{ex:g11:becoming-male-female:disagree}
About one person in a few thousand is born with a combination the
cascade does not usually produce: an XY person whose \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} lack the
receptor for testosterone develops a female body, with testes that
never descended and no uterus; an XX person with a testis-forming
translocation of \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY} develops a male body; an XY person with a
non-working \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY} develops as female. These variations show, better
than the ordinary case, that each level is the outcome of a specific
step --- a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, a hormone, a receptor --- and that the levels are
separate facts.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Reasoning about a case]\label{met:g11:becoming-male-female:case}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{Chromosomes}: is \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY} present and working?
  \item Gonad: testis if \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY} acts, ovary otherwise.
  \item Hormones of the foetal gonad: testosterone and AMH, or neither;
        and can the tissues respond (receptors)?
  \item Ducts: Wolffian kept and Müllerian removed if both hormones
        act; Müllerian developed if neither acts; both if testosterone
        acts without AMH.
  \item \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:puberty}{Puberty}: which sex hormone the gonad secretes decides the
        secondary characteristics --- again through receptors.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[One gene, and everything after it]\label{rem:g11:becoming-male-female:onegene}
The whole difference between the two anatomies traces back to whether
one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} on one \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} is present in the seventh week. What
follows is a cascade of ordinary \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} biology --- \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} expression,
hormones, receptors --- of the kind every chapter of this year has
described. The plan the cascade starts from is common to both sexes,
and the female form is the one it builds when the testis does not
intervene.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Jost's Rabbits}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the experiments that found the
two hormones of the testis, reconstructed step by step, then applied to
three human cases and to the timing of puberty}]
\label{pb:g11:becoming-male-female:1}
In the rabbit, the gonads differentiate around day 19 of gestation and
the ducts around day 22. Jost operated on foetuses in the womb on day
19 and examined them at birth (day 30).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The experiments.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Foetuses of both chromosomal sexes, gonads removed on day 19:
        all are born with oviducts and a uterus and without male
        ducts. What does this show about the ovary's role, and about
        the testis's?
  \item Why was it essential to operate before day 22?
  \item Foetuses castrated on day 19, with a testis grafted into the
        abdomen: male ducts, no female ducts. What does the graft
        supply, and how does it reach the ducts?
  \item Foetuses castrated on day 19, with a crystal of testosterone
        implanted: male ducts present, female ducts also present.
        What does testosterone do, and what does it not do?
  \item Deduce that the testis must make a second substance, and state
        its effect. How could its existence later be confirmed?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- One gonad removed.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A single testis is removed from an XY foetus on day 19. Predict
        the ducts on each side of the body, knowing that the hormones
        act mostly on the nearer side.
  \item A testis is grafted beside one ovary of an XX foetus. Predict
        each side.
  \item A crystal of testosterone is implanted beside one ovary of an
        XX foetus. Predict each side.
  \item Why do these one-sided experiments strengthen the conclusion
        of Part I?
  \item Explain why an XX foetus with a testis graft would
        nevertheless be sterile as an adult.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Three human cases.}
Apply \cref{met:g11:becoming-male-female:case} to each.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item An XX person carries, on one X \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}, a fragment of Y
        containing \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY}.
  \item An XY person carries a non-working \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY}.
  \item An XY person with normal testes has \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that cannot respond
        to AMH.
  \item In which of the three cases is the person likely to be
        fertile? Justify.
  \item What do the three cases show about the relation between
        chromosomal sex and anatomical sex?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:puberty}{Puberty} in numbers.}
From the hormone figure, take the growth spurt to begin one year after
the hormone reaches 20\% of its adult level and the bones to stop
growing when it reaches 100\%.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Estimate the age at which the growth spurt begins in girls and
        in boys, and the age at which growth stops.
  \item How many years of growth does each sex have between the start
        of the spurt and its end?
  \item A boy grows \qty{8}{cm} a year during the spurt and a girl
        \qty{7}{cm}. Estimate the height gained during the spurt by
        each, and comment on the average difference in adult height
        (about \qty{13}{cm}).
  \item A girl whose oestrogen rise begins at 7 will grow fast early
        and stop early. Explain why doctors sometimes delay a very
        early \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:puberty}{puberty} with drugs.
  \item State the result: the two hormones Jost's rabbits revealed,
        the effect of each, and the one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} that switches on the
        organ that makes them.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
