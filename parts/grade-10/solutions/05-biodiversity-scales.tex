\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g10:biodiversity-scales} --- Biodiversity at Every Scale}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:1}
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{Ecosystems} (a pond, a beech wood); \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} (the frogs, newts and reeds
of the pond); \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} (the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of the ABO \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} in a human
population).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:2}
A group of similar organisms that breed together and give fertile
offspring. Lion and tiger give sterile hybrids, so they are two
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:3}
$\num{1000000}/6400 \approx 156$: about 150 times more.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:4}
The fraction of all the copies of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} in a population that are a
given \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}. B $= 1 - 0.66 - 0.27 = 0.07$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:5}
Five; the largest was the end-Permian extinction, 252 million years
ago, which removed about 95\% of marine \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:6}
The second: equal richness, but a far more even relative abundance.
Richness alone does not distinguish them; the relative abundances do.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:7}
The field is the same; the classmate sampled ten times more. \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{Species}
found increase with effort until the curve flattens, so counts made
with different effort are not comparable --- the student's 8 is an
undersample, not a poorer field.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:8}
The few survivors carried only a fraction of the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}' \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles};
their descendants, however numerous, share that reduced set. \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{Genetic
diversity} is lost in the bottleneck and is not restored by numbers:
the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} has nothing in reserve against a new disease or a changed
climate (the cheetah's case).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:9}
About 560 families before, 300 after: $260/560 \approx 45\%$ of
families lost. A family disappears only when all its \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} do, so
many families survived through one or two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} while losing the
rest; the fraction of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} lost (about 95\%) is far larger than
the fraction of families.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:10}
Every population carries all three \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, and its members differ
among themselves in which they carry; the differences between
populations are only shifts in frequency. Most of the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}' \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic
diversity} therefore exists inside each population: two neighbours
typically differ at as many positions as two people from far apart.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:11}
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{Ecosystem}: the pond itself, with its community. \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{Species}: the newt or
dragonfly that lived only there locally. \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{Genes}: the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} carried by
the pond's frog population, which may have differed from those of
other ponds.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:12}
With a lifetime of about $\num{2e6}$ years, $\num{2e6}$ \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} give
about one extinction per year. A thousand times more is about 1000
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} per year, $\num{100000}$ per century --- 5\% of known \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
in a hundred years.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:13}
The extinction emptied ways of life --- large herbivores, large
predators, fliers, swimmers --- that the dinosaurs had occupied.
Surviving mammal lineages, freed of those competitors and with their
own \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic diversity} to draw on, adapted to the vacant roles and
split into new \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}: the loss opened the opportunities that the
new diversity filled.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:14}
Cultivated wheat has lost most of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of its ancestors. The
wild relatives keep them: \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} for resistance to a disease, to
drought, to salt, that no current variety carries. Crossing them into
wheat restores the option; once a wild relative is extinct, its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}
are gone for good.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:biodiversity-scales:15}
Extinction is natural, but at about one \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} per year; the present
rate is hundreds to a thousand times faster, comparable to a \omterm{prop:g10:biodiversity-scales:changes}{mass
extinction}, and recovery from those took millions of years --- longer
than our \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} has existed. Meanwhile the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{ecosystems} being lost
supply food, pollination, soil, water purification and the genetic
reserves of our crops. The precedent shows the loss is real and the
recovery not available on any human time scale.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g10:biodiversity-scales:1}
\textbf{1.} 31 and 9 \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}: ratio $31/9 \approx 3.4$.

\textbf{2.} Farm A: no \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} above 22\% against one at 84\% on
Farm B. Evenness says how the individuals are shared among the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
found; a rich but uneven community is dominated by one \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} and the
rest are rare.

\textbf{3.} The gains shrink (7, 4, 2): the curve is flattening, so
the sample is close to complete; a few more quadrats would add at most
one or two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}.

\textbf{4.} Farm B's curve reached 9 by the 15th quadrat and added
nothing after: a complete sampling curve flattens to a plateau, which
Farm B reached and Farm A nearly did.

\textbf{5.} Many plants are visible or identifiable only in flower,
and insects and birds vary with the season; a different month would
change the counts for reasons unrelated to the hedges.

\textbf{6.} Insects $46/14 \approx 3.3$; birds $17/5 = 3.4$. All three
groups give a factor of about 3: the same story.

\textbf{7.} Hedges supply the plants (leaves, flowers, dead wood,
shelter) that insects feed on and breed in; fewer plant \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} mean
fewer insect \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}; insects and the hedge's berries and nest sites
feed and house the birds, so bird diversity falls in turn.

\textbf{8.} No: yield measures the crop only. A hedge may shelter the
field from wind, hold the soil, house the pollinators and the
predators of crop pests, and its effects appear in bad years or over
decades, not in one season's figure.

\textbf{9.} A hedgerow nester such as a blackbird or a hedge sparrow
(or a hedge snail, a hawthorn shield bug): it needs the shrubs for
nesting, food or shelter that Farm B no longer offers.

\textbf{10.} \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{Ecosystems}: the hedge itself, a habitat, was removed, and
the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} and \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} went with it; the losses at the other scales are
consequences.

\textbf{11.} Farm A: y copies $2 \times 100 + 200 = 400$, b copies
$200 + 2 \times 100 = 400$, out of 800: y frequency 0.5. Farm B: y
$8 + 32 = 40$, b $32 + 128 = 160$, out of 200: y frequency 0.2.

\textbf{12.} Farm A: both \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} equally frequent and 50\% of snails
carry both; Farm B: 32\% carry both and b dominates. Farm A is more
diverse.

\textbf{13.} On the bare bank the yellow snails are eaten more often;
generation after generation, fewer y copies were passed on, and the
frequency of y fell from around 0.5 to 0.2.

\textbf{14.} The advantage would reverse: yellow snails would survive
better in the shaded hedge and the frequency of y would rise again
over the generations --- provided the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is still present.

\textbf{15.} Farm B's: in a population of a few hundred, a bad year or
a few chance deaths can remove every copy of the rarer \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}; among
tens of thousands the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is carried by thousands of snails and
chance cannot erase it.

\textbf{16.} Farm A: $31 + 46 + 17 = 94$; Farm B: $9 + 14 + 5 = 28$;
ratio $94/28 \approx 3.4$.

\textbf{17.} Three per cent of the land holds the habitat of about two
thirds of the farm's \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}.

\textbf{18.} Plants $24/31 \approx 77\%$; insects $35/46 \approx 76\%$;
birds $12/17 \approx 71\%$.

\textbf{19.} \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{Genetic diversity}: the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} return from neighbouring
populations, but the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} lost from the local populations (Farm B's
snails, for instance) come back only if immigrants happen to carry
them; a lost \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is not recreated by replanting.

\textbf{20.} Hedgerows multiplied the farm's \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} richness by about
3.4 in every group counted; a replanted hedge brings back the
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{ecosystem} and, in time, most \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, but not by itself the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}
that local populations lost.
\end{solution}
