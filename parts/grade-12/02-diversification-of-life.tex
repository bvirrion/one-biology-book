\chapter{Diversification of Living Things}\label{ch:g12:diversification-of-life}

Bread wheat has 42 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, in six sets of seven: it is the sum of
three wild grasses that crossed, twice, in the fields of the first
farmers. A python has three hundred vertebrae and a mouse thirty, yet
they build their backbones with the same \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}. A lichen on a rock is
two organisms, a fungus and an alga, that have lived as one for so long
that neither can be found alone. And a young chimpanzee learns to crack
nuts with a stone by watching its mother, in a forest where the
chimpanzees a hundred kilometres away have never learnt. \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{Mutation} and
the shuffle of the last chapter are not the only ways living things
become different; this chapter surveys the others.

\section{More than mutation}

\begin{proposition}[Sources of diversification]\label{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:sources}
Point \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} and the sexual shuffle create new \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} and new
combinations of them. Beyond these, the diversity of living things has
been produced by processes that change the genome on a larger scale
--- duplicating \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, importing \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} from other species, adding whole
sets of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} --- by changes in the way existing \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} are used
during development, by associations between species, and, in animals,
by behaviour transmitted without any \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} at all.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{New genes from old}

\begin{proposition}[Gene duplication and divergence]\label{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:duplication}
An unequal \omterm{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:crossingover}{crossing-over} (\cref{ch:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling}) or
a copying error can leave a \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} with two copies of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}. One
copy continues its original job; the other, freed of that constraint,
accumulates \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} and may acquire a new function --- or be lost.
Repeated over evolutionary time, this produces \emph{gene families}\index{gene family}:
sets of related \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} descended from one ancestor, whose degrees of
similarity record the order of the duplications.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The three \omterm{def:g11:the-eye:photoreceptors}{opsins} and rhodopsin of \cref{ch:g11:the-eye} are one such
family; the globins are another: the several \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} for the chains of
haemoglobin --- one used in the embryo, one in the foetus, two in the
adult --- and the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} of myoglobin, the oxygen store of muscle, lie
in clusters on two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, are 40\% to 80\% identical in
sequence, and their tree matches the order in which the \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrate}
groups appeared. Genomes carry hundreds of such families, and a
sizeable fraction of every genome consists of duplicated segments.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Horizontal gene transfer]\label{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:horizontal}
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{Genes} can also enter a genome from another species. Bacteria exchange
plasmids freely (\cref{ch:g11:antibiotic-resistance}); viruses carry
fragments of their hosts' \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} from one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} to another and leave copies
of their own \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} behind; and comparison of genomes shows that even
animals and plants have acquired \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} this way. About 8\% of the human
genome is of viral origin, and at least one of those \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, once a
virus's tool for fusing \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, now builds the placenta.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{proposition}[Hybridisation and polyploidy]\label{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:polyploidy}
Two related species can sometimes cross; the hybrid carries one
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} set from each and is usually sterile, since its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}
have no partners to pair with at \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis}. If, by an error of division,
the hybrid doubles its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, every \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} gains a partner,
\omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} works, and a new fertile species --- a \emph{polyploid}\index{polyploidy}
--- exists at once, isolated from both parents. Half of all flowering
plant species have polyploidy in their history; bread wheat, cotton,
tobacco and the potato are among them.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Wheat: the wild einkorn has $2n = 14$; emmer, $2n = 28$, contains the
einkorn set and the set of another wild grass, \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} by
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}; bread wheat, $2n = 42$, adds the set of a third. The three
sets can be identified in the bread wheat \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{karyotype} and matched to the
living wild species, and the crosses can be repeated in the
laboratory. A cordgrass that appeared on the coast of Europe in the
nineteenth century, fertile and vigorous, has the doubled \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}
set of a sterile hybrid of two species that had never met before ships
brought one across the Atlantic.
\end{proof}

\section{Same genes, different uses}

\begin{proposition}[Developmental genes and the timing of their expression]\label{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:development}
The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} that lay out an animal's \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{body plan} during development ---
which end is the head, where the limbs go, how many segments the trunk
has --- are shared, with little change in sequence, across the animals:
the same family of \emph{developmental genes}\index{developmental
gene} organises a fly, a mouse and a human. What differs between
species is not so much these \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} as \emph{where, when and how
strongly} they are switched on. A change in the regulation of a
\omterm{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:development}{developmental gene} can alter the shape of a body part or the number of
repeated parts without changing any \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} that define the regions of the trunk are expressed in the
same order from head to tail in a mouse and a python; in the python the
region that makes ribbed vertebrae extends along almost the whole
body, and the region that would make limbs never receives the signal
to do so: three hundred vertebrae and no legs, from the same \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}
expressed over different lengths. Among Darwin's finches, the depth of
the beak follows the level at which one growth signal is expressed in
the embryonic beak: more signal, deeper beak. Raising that signal
artificially in a chicken embryo produces a deep, finch-like beak.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Beaks by the dose]\label{ex:g12:diversification-of-life:beaks}
Thirteen species of finch on one group of islands have beaks from
needle-thin to nutcracker-deep, and eat accordingly. Their beak \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}
are the same; the embryos of the deep-beaked species express one
growth signal earlier and more strongly in the tissue that becomes the
beak. A single change in the timing or amount of one signal, not a new
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, separates an insect-eater from a seed-crusher --- which is why
such differences can arise in a few thousand generations.
\end{example}

\section{Living together: symbiosis}

\begin{definition}[Symbiosis]\label{def:g12:diversification-of-life:symbiosis}
A \emph{symbiosis}\index{symbiosis} is a lasting, close association of
two species from which both benefit. A lichen is a fungus housing algal
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that feed it by \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} while it shelters and waters them;
a coral is an animal housing algae in its tissues; the roots of most
plants are wrapped in fungi that supply minerals in exchange for sugar;
the guts of animals hold bacteria that digest what the animal cannot.
The association produces organisms, structures and ways of life that
neither partner could achieve alone.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The symbiosis inside every cell]\label{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:endosymbiosis}
The \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} of every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:prokaryote}{eukaryotic cell}, and the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{chloroplasts} of
plant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, descend from free-living bacteria that were engulfed by
an ancestral \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} some two billion years ago and stayed. The \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:prokaryote}{eukaryotic
cell} itself is the outcome of a \omterm{def:g12:diversification-of-life:symbiosis}{symbiosis}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{Mitochondria} and \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{chloroplasts} have their own \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, a small circle like a
bacterium's, and their own \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{ribosomes}, of the bacterial kind, sensitive
to the \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotics} that block bacterial but not eukaryotic \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{ribosomes};
they multiply by dividing in two, and cannot be made anew by the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell};
they are wrapped in two membranes, the inner one resembling a
bacterial membrane; and their \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} are closest, in sequence, to those
of specific groups of living bacteria --- purple bacteria for
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria}, cyanobacteria for \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{chloroplasts}.
\end{proof}

\section{Diversity without genes: behaviour}

\begin{proposition}[Transmitted behaviour]\label{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:behaviour}
In many animals, part of what an individual does is learnt from
others --- by imitation, teaching or copying --- and passed on in turn:
a \emph{culture}\index{culture (animal)}, transmitted without \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}.
Populations of the same species can therefore differ in behaviour as
they differ in \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, and a behaviour can spread through a population
in a generation rather than over the many that a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} would need.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Chimpanzee communities in different forests use different tools ---
stones to crack nuts here, sticks to fish for termites there, leaves as
sponges elsewhere --- and the differences do not follow genetic
relatedness or the availability of materials; a young chimpanzee
acquires its community's set by watching its elders. Songbirds raised
in isolation sing a crude song; raised hearing adults, they sing the
local dialect, which changes across a range like a human accent. A
troop of macaques on an island learnt to wash sweet potatoes in the sea
from one young female within a decade, and its descendants still do.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Naming the source of a difference]\label{met:g12:diversification-of-life:name}
Faced with a difference between two organisms or populations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Is it in the sequence of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} (\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}, \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele})? In the
        number of copies of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} (duplication)? In the presence of a
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} from elsewhere (horizontal transfer)? In the number of
        \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} sets (polyploidy)?
  \item Is it in when, where or how much a shared \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is expressed
        during development (regulation)?
  \item Is it the result of an association with another species
        (\omterm{def:g12:diversification-of-life:symbiosis}{symbiosis})?
  \item Is it learnt (behaviour transmitted by culture) --- inherited,
        but not genetically?
\end{enumerate}
More than one answer is often right; the questions are the inventory
of this chapter.
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Diversification and selection]\label{rem:g12:diversification-of-life:selection}
Every process here produces variation; none of them decides what
survives. A \omterm{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:polyploidy}{polyploid} wheat, a python's expression pattern, a lichen,
a nut-cracking tradition each persisted because it worked where it
arose. The sorting of variation --- selection, and chance --- is the
subject of the next chapter; this one has laid out how much there is
to sort, and how many ways there are of making it.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Three Grasses in a Loaf}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the history of bread wheat
reconstructed from chromosomes, a python's genes read beside a
mouse's, and the bacterium inside every cell put on trial}]
\label{pb:g12:diversification-of-life:1}
Einkorn (AA), goat grass 1 (BB) and goat grass 2 (DD) each have
$2n = 14$. Emmer is AABB and bread wheat AABBDD.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} of wheat.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Give the number of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} in emmer and in bread wheat.
  \item The hybrid of einkorn and goat grass 1 has 14 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, 7
        of each kind. How many pairs of homologues form at its
        \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis}? What are its gametes like?
  \item Explain why a doubling of its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} restores fertility:
        count the pairs.
  \item The hybrid of emmer and goat grass 2 has 21 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}. Which
        of them can pair, and why is it sterile?
  \item After doubling, bread wheat has 42. How many pairs form at its
        \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis}, and how many \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} does a pollen grain carry?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Reading the history.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Bread wheat carries three copies of most \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, one on each
        set. Explain why a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} destroying one copy usually has no
        visible effect, and what this allows over time.
  \item Emmer was cultivated 10\,000 years ago and bread wheat appears
        in archaeological sites 8\,000 years ago. Where and when did
        the second hybridisation probably occur? What had to be
        growing side by side?
  \item Laboratory crosses of emmer with goat grass 2, followed by
        chemical doubling, give fertile plants resembling bread wheat.
        Which conclusion of this history does that confirm?
  \item Bread wheat cannot cross with einkorn to give fertile
        offspring. Is it a separate species? Justify with the
        definition of \cref{ch:g10:biodiversity-scales}.
  \item A crop breeder wants to introduce a disease-resistance \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}
        from goat grass 2 into bread wheat. Explain why this is easier
        than from an unrelated plant.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The python's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The regional \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} of the trunk are 98\% identical between
        mouse and python. What does that say about the source of the
        difference in their bodies?
  \item In the mouse embryo the "ribbed trunk" \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is active over 13
        segments; in the python over about 300. Name the kind of
        change involved.
  \item The python embryo forms tiny buds where hindlimbs would be,
        which then stop growing. What does this suggest about the
        limb \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} and the signal that would activate them?
  \item Mice engineered to express the "ribbed trunk" \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} over the
        lumbar region grow extra ribs there. What does this experiment
        add to the comparison?
  \item Compare the two kinds of change: bread wheat's and the
        python's. Which changed the genome's size, which its use?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The bacterium inside.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item List four features of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} that fit a bacterial
        origin, and for each say what you would expect if \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria}
        had instead been built by the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} from scratch.
  \item Mitochondrial \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} carries only 37 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, while the
        \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} needs about 1500 \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins}. Where are the other
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, and what happened to them over two billion years?
  \item Every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} of a person comes from the egg, none from
        the sperm. What follows for the inheritance of mitochondrial
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, and for tracing maternal ancestry?
  \item A lichen and a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} are both symbioses. In what way
        has the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion}'s gone further?
  \item State the result: the three species in a loaf and the number
        of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} each contributed; the one word that names what
        differs between the python's and the mouse's use of their
        shared \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}; and the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{organelle} that is a former bacterium.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
