\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:conservation-biology} --- Conservation Biology}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:1}
\omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{Demographic stochasticity}: chance in the births, deaths and sexes of a
few individuals --- the kakapo at fifty-one, with more males than
females. \omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{Environmental stochasticity}: a bad year that hits every
individual at once --- a drought, a disease, a hard winter striking a
population of a few hundred. \omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{Allee effect}: per-capita growth falling
at low density --- the passenger pigeon, whose colonies could not breed
once they had thinned, and whose remaining predators were no longer
swamped.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:2}
The size of an ideal population --- constant, randomly mating, equal
sexes, Poisson family sizes --- that would lose heterozygosity at the
same rate as the real one. Unequal numbers of breeding males and
females; fluctuations in size, which weight the smallest generations;
variance in family size above Poisson, with a few individuals
producing most of the young (also non-random mating and overlapping
generations).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:3}
$S = cA^{z}$. Fraction kept $= (A'/A)^{z}$: $0.5^{0.25} = 0.84$;
$0.1^{0.25} = 0.56$; $0.01^{0.25} = 0.32$ --- a hundredth of the area
still keeps a third of the species, eventually.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:4}
$N_{e} \ge 50$ keeps the rise of inbreeding below $1/100$ per
generation, the rate at which animal breeders find depression
tolerable: it guards against short-term loss of fitness. $N_{e} \ge
500$ balances the loss of variation by drift against its supply by
mutation, so the population keeps the quantitative variation it needs
to adapt: it guards the long term. Real populations need five to ten
times these numbers in the census.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:5}
$N_{e} = 4\times 8\times 120/128 = 30$; heterozygosity falls by $1/60 =
\qty{1.7}{\%}$ a generation; a quarter lost when $(1 - 1/60)^{t} =
0.75$, $t = \ln 0.75/\ln(59/60) = 17$ generations.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:6}
$1/N_{e} = \tfrac{1}{5}(4/1000 + 1/20) = 0.0108$, $N_{e} = 93$: one
generation at twenty has made five generations behave like ninety-three.
Heterozygosity retained $(1 - 1/186)^{5} = 0.973$, against $(1 -
1/2000)^{5} = 0.9975$ for a constant thousand.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:7}
$F = 1 - (1 - 1/20)^{5} = 0.23$; fitness down $5\times 2.3 = \qty{11}{\%}$.
Every offspring of a Texas female and a Florida male has $F = 0$; if the
eight newcomers are two fifths of the breeding females, two fifths of
the next generation are outbred and the mean $F$ falls to about
$0.6\times 0.23 = 0.14$ in one generation --- which is what the
recovery showed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:8}
$r = \ln(100/31)/8 = \qty{0.15}{yr^{-1}}$. Continued to 2020:
$100\,\mathrm{e}^{0.15\times 17} \approx 1200$. The park held about a
hundred: territories filled, elk declined, packs killed one another's
members, and mange and distemper spread --- density dependence set in
once the empty range was occupied.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:9}
(a) A \qty{60}{\%} decline in ten years exceeds \qty{50}{\%}: Endangered.
(b) $180 < 250$ mature individuals: Critically Endangered. (c) Range
\qty{3000}{km^2}, below \qty{5000}{}: Endangered. (d) \num{30000}
individuals and a \qty{10}{\%} decline: neither threshold met --- not
threatened (at most Near Threatened, to be watched).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:10}
All $N$ pairs fail with probability $(1/2)^{N}$: $0.25$, $0.031$,
$0.001$, $10^{-6}$. Demographic risk falls exponentially with $N$ and
is negligible beyond a few dozen individuals; above that, the risk that
remains is environmental, which does not fall so fast, because it
strikes all individuals together.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:11}
One fragment of a tenth: $0.1^{0.25} = 0.56$ of the original species.
Ten fragments hold between $0.56$ (if all hold the same species) and,
in principle, more than the whole (if each held different species ---
impossible beyond the regional pool). Where they fall depends on
turnover between fragments: different habitats in different fragments
push the total up; species that need large areas, interior habitat, or
that are lost from every fragment by the same \omterm{prop:b3:conservation-biology:area}{edge effects} push it
down; and each fragment's extinction debt is paid separately, so the
long-run total is usually below the whole's.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:conservation-biology:12}
Drift removes heterozygosity at rate $1/2N_{e}$ per generation;
immigrants replace a fraction $m$ of the gene pool with alleles drawn
from elsewhere. With $mN_{e} \approx 1$, $m \approx 1/N_{e}$, twice the
rate of loss, so variation is replenished faster than it decays and the
population stays close to the source in allele frequency ($F_{ST} = 1/(1
+ 4N_{e}m) \approx 0.2$). The cost: locally favoured alleles are diluted
by $m$ per generation, so local adaptation survives only for traits
whose selection coefficient exceeds about $1/N_{e}$; weakly selected
local differences are swamped.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:conservation-biology:1}
\textbf{1.} $N_{e} = 4\times 20\times 40/60 = 53$, against a census of 60.
\textbf{2.} $1/N_{e} = \tfrac{1}{5}(1/500 + 1/100 + 1/60 + 1/51 + 1/60) =
0.0130$, $N_{e} = 77$; the bottleneck generations of 51 and 60
dominate, the 500 hardly counts.
\textbf{3.} $F = 1 - (1 - 1/154)^{5} = 0.032$; linear $5/154 = 0.032$.
\textbf{4.} $6\times 0.32 = \qty{1.9}{\%}$.
\textbf{5.} $(1 - 1/154)^{5} = 0.968$: \qty{96.8}{\%} retained.
\textbf{6.} Ten generations at $N_{e} = 53$: increment $1 - (1 -
1/107)^{10} = 0.090$, total $F = 1 - 0.968\times 0.910 = 0.12$; fitness
loss $6\times 1.2 = \qty{7}{\%}$; a century.
\textbf{7.} Native--native pairs are $(60/70)^{2} = 0.73$ of matings and
their offspring keep $F \approx 0.03$ (plus a small increment); all
others have $F = 0$: mean $F \approx 0.73\times 0.035 = 0.026$, a fall
of a quarter in one generation.
\textbf{8.} $F$ is identity by descent within an individual, and any
outbred offspring resets it to zero; heterozygosity is a property of
allele frequencies, and the migrants' alleles spread only as they are
passed on. In a population still small, drift will remove them again
within tens of generations, so rescue must be repeated or the
population grown.
\textbf{9.} $N_{e} = N$ for equal sexes: 500 adults. From 70 at $r =
0.05$: $\ln(500/70)/0.05 = 39$ years.
\textbf{10.} The ideal population has Poisson family sizes, variance
equal to the mean of two; $N_{e} = 4N/(2 + V_{k})$, so making every pair
contribute equally ($V_{k} = 0$) gives $N_{e} = 2N$: no lineage is
lost by the luck of the nest.
\textbf{11.} Collect and store his semen for artificial insemination,
and use it on several unrelated females across years --- the cost is
that his alleles could come to dominate a small pool, so his share must
be capped near $1/N_{e}$; or cryopreserve tissue for later cloning or
gamete production, at the cost of delay and technical risk. Doing
nothing loses the lineage's alleles for good.
\textbf{12.} \qty{160}{} million dollars for some 430 birds: about
\num{370000} per bird. Expensive per parrot, cheap against a species
--- and less than a few kilometres of motorway, which is the comparison
budgets actually make.
\textbf{13.} $(300/2000)^{0.25} = 0.62$: about 112 species of 180 kept,
an extinction debt of 68.
\textbf{14.} Each fragment $(100/2000)^{0.25}\times 180 = 85$ species.
Entirely different: up to 255, impossible above the pool of 180;
identical: 85. What decides is how different the fragments' habitats
are and how many species can persist in \qty{100}{km^2}; realistically
between 85 and 112.
\textbf{15.} $(320/2000)^{0.25}\times 180 = 114$, against 85 for three
isolated identical fragments: the corridor lets recolonisation and
gene flow make three small populations behave as one larger one.
\textbf{16.} $N_{e} = 50$ with equal sexes is 25 pairs: \qty{1250}{km^2}.
The whole \qty{300}{km^2} holds six pairs, $N_{e} \approx 12$; a
fragment, two. Neither suffices: the predator is lost whatever the
species count, and protecting the area it needs protects everything
smaller --- an umbrella species.
\textbf{17.} $\ln(200/10)/0.03 = 100$ years: the debt is paid over a
century, long after the forest was cut.
\textbf{18.} Edges strip interior habitat from every fragment, so the
usable area is less than the mapped one and the loss is greater than
the law predicts; a matrix of secondary growth or hedgerows that some
species can use adds effective area and makes the loss smaller.
\textbf{19.} $r = \ln(100/31)/8 = \qty{0.146}{yr^{-1}}$; doubling time
$\ln 2/0.146 = 4.7$ years.
\textbf{20.} $N(8) = 120/[1 + (120/31 - 1)\mathrm{e}^{-1.17}] = 120/(1 +
2.87\times 0.31) = 63$. The observed 100 is closer to the exponential
124 than to the logistic 63: in the first years the wolves met no
density dependence --- empty territories and abundant elk.
\textbf{21.} $\ln(4000/\num{17000})/20 = -0.072$: \qty{7}{\%} a year. A
hundred wolves taking \num{2000} elk a year from a herd averaging
\num{8000} is a quarter a year --- more than enough to drive the decline
on its own, though hunting outside the park, drought and grizzly
predation on calves shared it.
\textbf{22.} Sixty mature individuals, below 250: Critically Endangered.
The predator at $N_{e} \approx 12$ (a few dozen animals): Critically
Endangered as well.
\textbf{23.} From $3\times 10^{9}$ to $1$ in 44 years: $r = -\ln(3\times
10^{9})/44 = -0.50$ per year, halving every seventeen months. No
harvest removes half of billions every year; the fall was slower at
first and far faster at the end, because a colonial breeder below a
threshold density stops reproducing while its predators are no longer
swamped --- the \omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{Allee effect} turned a decline into a collapse.
\textbf{24.} In 1850 the population was in the billions, its measured
growth rate positive and its variance small, and a viability analysis
projects the dynamics it is given: it would have put the risk at zero.
It would have missed the external forcing --- railways and the telegraph
that let hunters follow and empty every roost, forest clearance --- and
the Allee threshold in a colonial breeder, neither of which is in the
demography of an abundant species; a PVA extrapolates, and the causes
of extinction are usually new.
\textbf{25.} $N_{e} \approx 53$ today; $F \approx 0.12$ after ten more
generations at 60 (\qty{7}{\%} of fitness); the shrunken forest keeps
about \qty{62}{\%} of its species.
\end{solution}
