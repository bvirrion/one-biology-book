\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b1:genomes} --- Genomes of Cells and Viruses}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:genomes:1}
Bacterium: one circular \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} (plus \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{plasmids}) in a \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{nucleoid}
without a membrane, compacted by supercoiling and small basic
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins}, about \qty{90}{\%} coding. Eukaryote: several linear
\omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} in a nucleus, wound on \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{histones} into \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{chromatin}, a few
percent coding.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:genomes:4}
A \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genome} (\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} or \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA}) in a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} \omterm{def:b1:genomes:virus}{capsid}, sometimes enveloped, that
reproduces only inside a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} with the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s machinery. Outside a
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} it has no metabolism, no growth, no response --- an inert
particle; it is ``alive'' only in carrying heritable information that
evolves.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:genomes:7}
\emph{E. coli} 930 \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes} per Mb; yeast 500; fly 100; human 6; lungfish
0.15. \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{Gene} density falls by four orders of magnitude while \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} number
rises fivefold: the extra \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} is non-genic.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:genomes:8}

10^6/20 = \num{3e6}$ generations: one new insertion every three

\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:genomes:9}
The egg contributes the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cytoplasm} and its \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:mitochondrion}{mitochondria}; the sperm's
few \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:mitochondrion}{mitochondria} are excluded or destroyed after fertilisation. The
mitochondrial \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} therefore passes unmixed from mother to child, and
its accumulated mutations trace maternal lineages back through time.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:genomes:10}
Fed culture, two hours: lysogeny gives $2^6 = 64$ copies (one per
host division); lysis gives 100 phages in \qty{40}{min}, and their
progeny $100^3 = 10^6$ in two hours if hosts abound --- lysis wins by
far. Starving culture: lysogeny gives one copy, safe inside a living
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}; lysis gives 100 phages with no \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} to infect, which decay. The
switch reads the host's state because the best strategy depends on
whether new hosts will be available.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:genomes:11}
$\num{1.3e11}\times 0.34\,\mathrm{nm} = \qty{44}{m}$ (haploid);
$\num{6.5e8}$ \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{nucleosomes}; \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{histones} $\num{6.5e8}\times 108\,000\times
1.66\times 10^{-24} = \qty{117}{pg}$. Origins: $\num{1.3e6}$, each
replicating \qty{50}{kb} on each side at \qty{50}{bp/s}: \qty{1000}{s}
--- the time is not the problem, given enough origins; the cost is the
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} (\qty{130}{pg} of \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} per division), the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{histones}, the
time to make them, and a nucleus and \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} many times larger, which is
why such \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} divide slowly.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:genomes:12}
A design would contain what is needed and no more; a record contains
what happened. Pseudogenes are the corpses of duplicated \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes} that
died by mutation; transposable elements are parasites that copied
themselves for tens of millions of years and were never removed; \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}
families show duplication followed by divergence; and the C-value
paradox shows that the amount of \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} reflects a lineage's history of
accumulation and loss rather than its needs. Selection keeps the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes}
working and lets the rest drift: what we read in a \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genome} is
mostly history.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b1:genomes:1}
\textbf{1.} $\num{6.4e9}\times 0.34\,\mathrm{nm} = \qty{2.18}{m}$.
\textbf{2.} $\num{6.4e9}/10 = \num{6.4e8}$ turns.
\textbf{3.} $\num{2.5e8}\times 0.34 = \qty{8.5}{cm}$; \qty{1.7}{cm}.
\textbf{4.} $\frac{4}{3}\pi\times 3^3 = \qty{113}{\micro m^3}$.
\textbf{5.} $\pi\times(1\,\mathrm{nm})^2\times 2.18\,\mathrm{m} =
\qty{6.8e-18}{m^3} = \qty{6.8}{\micro m^3}$: \qty{6}{\%} of the nucleus.
\textbf{6.} $\num{6.4e9}\times 650\times 1.66\times 10^{-24} =
\qty{6.9e-12}{g} = \qty{6.9}{pg}$.
\textbf{7.} Nuclear mass $113\times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{mL}\times 1.1 =
\qty{124}{pg}$; \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} \qty{5.6}{\%} of it, \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} \qty{20}{\%}.
\textbf{8.} $\num{6.4e9}/200 = \num{3.2e7}$.
\textbf{9.} $\num{3.2e7}\times 108\,000\times 1.66\times 10^{-24} =
\qty{5.7}{pg}$: about equal to the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}.
\textbf{10.} $68/11 = 6.2$.
\textbf{11.} Six \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{nucleosomes}, \qty{1200}{bp} $= \qty{408}{nm}$ of
helix, in \qty{11}{nm}: factor $37$.
\textbf{12.} $2.18/37 = \qty{5.9}{cm}$ of fibre --- ten thousand times
the nuclear diameter, folded within it.
\textbf{13.} All $\num{3.2e7}$, plus the same number of new ones on the
second copy: $\num{6.4e7}$ in \qty{28800}{s}, about \num{2200} per
second.
\textbf{14.} $75\,000\times 0.34/37 = \qty{690}{nm}$ of fibre per
loop; $\num{6.4e9}/75\,000 = \num{85000}$ loops.
\textbf{15.} $\qty{8.5}{cm}/\qty{10}{\micro m} = 8500$.
\textbf{16.} $\pi\times 0.35^2\times 10 = \qty{3.85}{\micro m^3}$; \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}
$\pi\times 10^{-6}\times 0.085\,\mathrm{m} = \qty{0.27}{\micro m^3}$:
\qty{7}{\%} --- the rest is \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{histone} and scaffold \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} and water.
\textbf{17.} Total \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} $\num{6.4e9}$ pairs at the same density
($\num{2.5e8}$ pairs in \qty{3.85}{\micro m^3}, per chromatid; two
chromatids per \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome}): $2\times 6.4\times 10^9/2.5\times 10^8\times
3.85 = \qty{197}{\micro m^3}$, larger than the interphase nucleus
because the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} are now rods with space between them.
\textbf{18.} Interphase \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{chromatin} must be read and copied: polymerases
and their factors need access to the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}, so most of it stays as the
open \qty{30}{nm} fibre and loops; metaphase \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{chromatin} is inert and can
be packed for transport.
\textbf{19.} $\num{6.4e9}$ sites at $10^3$ per second: $\num{6.4e6}$
seconds, 74 days. \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{Proteins} do not search at random: they bind \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}
non-specifically and slide along it, and many copies search in
parallel, so a site is found in seconds.
\textbf{20.} $\num{20000}\times 27\,\mathrm{kb} = \qty{540}{Mb}$,
\qty{17}{\%} of \qty{3.2}{Gb}; coding $\num{20000}\times 1.5 =
\qty{30}{Mb}$, \qty{0.9}{\%} (about 1.5\% with the shorter \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes} counted
more carefully).
\textbf{21.} $113\times 2\times 250/6400 = \qty{8.8}{\micro m^3}$ (two
copies); $\num{5e8}/8.8 = \qty{5.7e7}{bp/\micro m^3}$, the same as the
whole nucleus.
\textbf{22.} $\num{2.6e11}/\num{5.7e7} = \qty{4600}{\micro m^3}$:
diameter \qty{21}{\micro m}, forty times the human nucleus in volume.
\textbf{23.} $\num{3.2e7}$ octamers, $\num{3.2e10}$ residues --- a tenth
of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s daily \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} synthesis, spent on packaging.
\textbf{24.} A bacterium's \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} is a thousand times shorter and
supercoiling in loops suffices to fit it into a micrometre; a
eukaryote must fold a length ten thousand times its nucleus and needs
a hierarchy of packing, of which the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{nucleosome} is the first level.
\textbf{25.} About \num{8500} (\qty{8.5}{cm} into \qty{10}{\micro m}),
built as $\times 6$ (\omterm{def:b1:genomes:nucleosome}{nucleosomes}), $\times 6$ more (\qty{30}{nm} fibre,
$\times 37$ in all), $\times 27$ more (loops, $\times 1000$ in all), and
$\times 8$ more (metaphase condensation).
\end{solution}
