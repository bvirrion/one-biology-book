\chapter{Chemical Messengers and Signal Transduction}\label{ch:b2:cell-signalling}

A single molecule of adrenaline, arriving at the surface of a liver
cell, never enters it. It binds a protein on the outside, changes
the protein's shape, and within a second the cell has made ten
thousand molecules of a \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{second messenger}, activated a hundred
thousand enzyme molecules, and begun releasing a million molecules
of glucose --- a chain of amplifications a million-fold long, started
by a signal that stayed outside the door. This chapter is about the
messengers cells send one another and how a cell converts a
message at its surface into a change in its chemistry, its genes or
its shape: the receptors, the \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{second messengers}, the cascades of
kinases, and the two designs --- the fast, wired signalling of nerves
and the slow, broadcast signalling of \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormones} --- whose contrast
runs through the rest of this book.

\section{Messengers}

\begin{definition}[Kinds of chemical signalling]\label{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}
\index{hormone}\index{endocrine signalling}\index{paracrine signalling}\index{neurotransmitter}
A cell signals to another with a secreted molecule, a
\emph{messenger} or \emph{ligand}, that binds a \emph{receptor} on or
in the target. By range: \emph{endocrine} signalling, in which a
\emph{hormone} is released into the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} by a gland and reaches
every cell of the body, acting only on those with the receptor,
over minutes to hours (insulin, cortisol, adrenaline, thyroxine);
\emph{paracrine} signalling, in which the messenger diffuses to
neighbouring cells within a millimetre and is destroyed on the way
(growth factors, histamine, prostaglandins, the morphogens of
\cref{ch:b2:vertebrate-development}); \emph{autocrine}, the cell
signalling to itself; and \emph{synaptic}, in which a neuron
releases a \emph{neurotransmitter} across a gap of \qty{20}{nm} onto
one target cell, in a millisecond (\cref{ch:b2:neurons-synapses}).
By chemistry: \emph{water-soluble} messengers --- amino-acid
derivatives such as adrenaline, peptides and proteins such as
insulin --- cannot cross the membrane and act on surface receptors;
\emph{lipid-soluble} messengers --- the steroids (cortisol,
oestradiol, testosterone), thyroxine, retinoic acid, nitric oxide ---
cross it and act on receptors inside. The endocrine and nervous
systems are the two ways a body of $10^{13}$ cells coordinates
itself: the one broadcasts slowly to everyone, the other wires
quickly to someone.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Receptor occupancy]\label{thm:b2:cell-signalling:occupancy}
\index{dissociation constant}\index{receptor occupancy}
A receptor $R$ binds its ligand $L$ reversibly, $R + L
\rightleftharpoons RL$, with a \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:cell-signalling:occupancy}{dissociation constant}} $K_d =
[R][L]/[RL]$. At equilibrium the fraction of receptors occupied is
\[
\theta = \frac{[RL]}{[R]_{\text{tot}}} = \frac{[L]}{K_d + [L]},
\]
the same hyperbola as an enzyme's saturation. $K_d$ is the
concentration at which half the receptors are occupied and measures
the \emph{affinity}: the lower, the tighter. \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{Hormone} receptors have
$K_d$ in the nanomolar to picomolar range, matched to the
concentrations at which \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormones} circulate ($10^{-12}$ to
$10^{-9}$ mol/L): at $[L] = K_d/10$ a tenth are occupied, at
$10K_d$ nine tenths. A cell cannot respond to a \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} it does not
have receptors for, and it tunes its sensitivity by changing the
number of receptors, or --- as in \cref{ch:b2:reproductive-hormones}
--- by withdrawing them when the \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} is constantly present.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
With $[R] = [R]_{\text{tot}} - [RL]$, $K_d = ([R]_{\text{tot}} -
[RL])[L]/[RL]$, so $[RL](K_d + [L]) = [R]_{\text{tot}}[L]$, which is
the formula. It is the Michaelis--Menten expression of the Year 1
volume without the catalytic step.
\end{proof}

\section{Receptors at the surface}

\begin{proposition}[G-protein-coupled receptors and cyclic AMP]\label{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}
\index{G protein}\index{G-protein-coupled receptor}\index{cyclic AMP}\index{second messenger}\index{protein kinase A}
The largest family of receptors --- some \num{800} genes in a human,
for \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormones}, \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{neurotransmitters}, odours, light --- are proteins that
cross the membrane seven times. A ligand binding outside changes
their shape inside, where they activate a \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G protein}}: a
three-subunit switch that swaps its bound GDP for GTP, splits, and
in its GTP-bound form regulates an effector enzyme for a few seconds
before hydrolysing the GTP and switching itself off. The classic
effector is \emph{adenylyl cyclase}, which makes \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{cyclic AMP}}
from ATP: a small, diffusible \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{second messenger}} whose
concentration rises within seconds from \qty{0.1}{\micro mol/L} to
several micromolar and which activates \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{protein kinase A}}, an
enzyme that phosphorylates --- and so switches on or off --- dozens
of target proteins. The signal is ended by phosphodiesterase, which
destroys cAMP, and by phosphatases, which remove the phosphates.
Adrenaline on a liver cell acts this way: receptor $\to$ \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G protein}
$\to$ cyclase $\to$ cAMP $\to$ kinase A $\to$ phosphorylase kinase
$\to$ glycogen phosphorylase $\to$ glucose. Glucagon uses the same
route; other \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G proteins} inhibit the cyclase, or activate
phospholipase C, which releases two other \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{second messengers},
\emph{inositol trisphosphate} --- which opens calcium stores --- and
diacylglycerol.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Sutherland (1957) showed that adrenaline added to a liver
homogenate released glucose only if membranes were present, that
the membranes produced a heat-stable factor which alone activated
phosphorylase in the membrane-free fraction, and that the factor was
\omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{cyclic AMP} --- the first \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{second messenger}, and the first
demonstration that a \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} acts at the cell surface through an
intracellular intermediary. Rodbell and Gilman (1970s) found that
the cyclase needed GTP and isolated the \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G protein} that carries the
signal between receptor and enzyme; cholera toxin, which locks the
\omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G protein} in its on state, causes the gut to secrete litres of fluid
a day --- the disease is a signalling defect.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Amplification in a cascade]\label{thm:b2:cell-signalling:amplification}
\index{amplification (signalling)}
If each of $n$ stages of a cascade activates $a$ molecules of the
next during the lifetime of its own activation, one molecule of
messenger produces $a^{n}$ molecules of the final product. With
four amplifying stages of a hundred each --- a receptor activating a
hundred \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G proteins}, a cyclase making a hundred cAMP, a kinase
phosphorylating a hundred enzymes, an enzyme releasing a hundred
glucose molecules --- $a^{n} = 10^{8}$; in practice a million or so,
in a few seconds, from a \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} at $10^{-9}$ mol/L. Amplification
is why \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormones} can be effective at concentrations a millionfold
below those of the metabolites they control, and why the cascade
must be switched off at every stage: an amplifier that cannot be
turned off is a disease (cholera, and the many cancers in which a
signalling protein is locked on).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Stage 1 produces $a$ active molecules; each of these produces $a$
at stage 2, giving $a^{2}$; by induction, $a^{n}$ after $n$ stages.
The gain of each stage is the number of substrate turnovers an
activated catalyst achieves before it is inactivated: its catalytic
rate times its active lifetime.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Receptor tyrosine kinases and calcium]\label{prop:b2:cell-signalling:rtk}
\index{receptor tyrosine kinase}\index{insulin receptor}\index{calcium signalling}\index{calmodulin}
A second family of surface receptors --- for insulin, for the growth
factors that drive cell division, for the inductive signals of
development --- are \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:rtk}{receptor tyrosine kinases}}: single-pass
proteins that, when their ligand brings two of them together,
phosphorylate each other on tyrosines and so create docking sites
for a set of adaptor proteins. From these run several cascades at
once: a chain of three kinases (the MAP kinase cascade) that ends in
the nucleus and switches on the genes of growth; a lipid kinase
whose product recruits the kinase that mediates insulin's metabolic
effects --- moving glucose transporters to the membrane, switching
on glycogen synthesis; and others. A third universal \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{second
messenger} is \emph{calcium}: held in the cytosol at
\qty{0.1}{\micro mol/L}, ten thousand times below the outside, it
floods in through channels opened by a signal or a voltage, or out
of the endoplasmic reticulum in answer to inositol trisphosphate,
rising to micromolar in milliseconds; bound to \emph{calmodulin} it
activates kinases and pumps, and bound to troponin it triggers
contraction (\cref{ch:b2:muscle-movement}). Pumps return it to the
stores within a second. Whatever the receptor, the cell's interior
speaks a few languages --- cAMP, calcium, phosphorylation --- and the
specificity lies in which proteins each cell type has for them to
act on.
\end{proposition}

\section{Receptors inside}

\begin{proposition}[Nuclear receptors]\label{prop:b2:cell-signalling:nuclear}
\index{nuclear receptor}\index{steroid hormone}
Steroids, thyroxine, retinoic acid and vitamin D diffuse through
the membrane and bind receptors in the cytosol or nucleus that are
themselves \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factors}}: the hormone--receptor
complex binds specific DNA sequences and switches its target genes
on or off. The response is slow --- minutes to hours to make the
mRNA and the protein --- and lasting: cortisol induces the enzymes
of gluconeogenesis for hours, oestradiol builds the uterine lining
over days, testosterone makes muscle over weeks, thyroxine sets the
metabolic rate of every cell. Some steroids also have fast actions
through surface receptors, and some surface pathways also reach the
nucleus (the MAP kinase cascade), so the distinction is not
absolute; but the rule stands that a lipid-soluble messenger
changes what a cell \emph{is} by changing its genes, while a
water-soluble one changes what it \emph{does} by changing its
enzymes.
\end{proposition}

\section{A worked case: insulin, glucagon and the glucose set point}

\begin{proposition}[Two hormones and a set point]\label{prop:b2:cell-signalling:glucose}
\index{insulin}\index{glucagon}\index{blood glucose}
The \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}'s glucose is held near \qty{5}{mmol/L} (\qty{0.9}{g/L}) by
two antagonistic \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormones} of the pancreatic islets. After a meal
the rising glucose is sensed by the $\beta$ cells --- which
metabolise it, close a potassium channel as their ATP rises,
depolarise, admit calcium and release \emph{insulin} --- and insulin,
through its tyrosine-kinase receptor, makes muscle and fat take up
glucose (by moving transporters to their membranes), the liver
store it as glycogen and fat, and all tissues stop burning fat.
Between meals the falling glucose lets the $\alpha$ cells release
\emph{glucagon}, which, through cAMP in the liver, breaks glycogen
down and makes new glucose from amino acids; adrenaline does the
same faster in emergencies, and cortisol and growth \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} over
hours. Each \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} is released in proportion to the deviation it
corrects: a negative feedback with two effectors, one for each
direction, and a set point defended to within a fifth. The brain,
which burns \qty{120}{g} of glucose a day and stores none, is the
organ the loop protects; type 1 diabetes is the loss of the
$\beta$ cells, and its untreated course --- glucose at
\qty{20}{mmol/L}, fat burnt for want of insulin, acid in the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}
--- is what the loop looks like with one arm removed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Speed, reach and cost of the two systems]\label{ex:b2:cell-signalling:compare}
Adrenaline in the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} reaches every cell in about a minute
(the circulation time) and acts for minutes; a nerve impulse
reaches its target in milliseconds and acts for milliseconds. A
\omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} costs almost nothing per cell reached --- a nanomole spread
over the body --- but cannot address one cell; a nerve addresses one
cell exactly but must build and maintain a wire to it. The body uses
both: the sympathetic nerves of \cref{ch:b2:blood-pressure} act on
the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} and the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} in a second, and the adrenal medulla,
itself a modified sympathetic ganglion, backs them up by pouring the
same molecule into the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} for the whole body. And at the synapse
the two designs meet, for a \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{neurotransmitter} is a messenger acting
on a receptor by the same mechanisms --- a channel that opens, a \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G
protein} that switches --- only across twenty nanometres instead of a
metre.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:cell-signalling:1}
Classify as endocrine, paracrine, autocrine or synaptic, and as
water- or lipid-soluble: insulin, cortisol, acetylcholine at a
synapse, histamine at a wound, oestradiol, adrenaline from the
adrenal, nitric oxide from the endothelium.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:cell-signalling:2}
List the steps from adrenaline at the liver cell membrane to glucose
in the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}, and name the off-switch at each step.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:cell-signalling:3}
Compare surface and \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:nuclear}{nuclear receptors}: what binds them, where they

\section{Problem: A Hormone from Blood to Gene}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- adrenaline followed from the
adrenal gland to a liver cell's glucose, with its concentration,
receptor occupancy, cascade gain and timing computed, then cortisol
followed to a gene, and the glucose loop analysed, ending on the
occupancy, the amplification and the time each hormone takes}]
\label{pb:b2:cell-signalling:1}
Data: the adrenal medulla releases \qty{1}{nmol} of adrenaline into
\qty{5}{L} of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} in a fright; adrenaline's $K_d$ at the liver
receptor is \qty{1}{nmol/L}; a liver cell (\qty{5000}{\micro m^3})
carries $2\times 10^{4}$ receptors. Cascade gains per second of
activation: receptor $\to$ \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G protein} 20, \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G protein} $\to$ cAMP 100
(the cyclase's turnover in the \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G protein}'s 3-second lifetime),
cAMP $\to$ kinase A 1 (stoichiometric), kinase A $\to$ phosphorylase
kinase 50, $\to$ phosphorylase 50, $\to$ glucose 1000. The liver
holds \qty{100}{g} of glycogen. Cortisol: transcription at
\qty{50}{} nucleotides a second on a gene of \qty{4000}{}
nucleotides, translation at \qty{5}{} residues a second on a
\num{600}-residue enzyme, \num{20} ribosomes per mRNA, \num{100}
mRNAs made per hour, an effect needing $10^{5}$ enzyme molecules.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} arrives.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma} concentration of adrenaline after the
        release, ignoring its removal.
  \item Compute the \omterm{thm:b2:cell-signalling:occupancy}{receptor occupancy} and the number of occupied
        receptors per liver cell.
  \item Adrenaline is removed with a half-life of \qty{2}{min}. What
        is its concentration and occupancy after \qty{6}{min}?
  \item How long does the adrenaline take to reach the liver from
        the adrenal, given a circulation time of about a minute? Why
        does the sympathetic nerve to the liver act faster?
  \item A cell with $2\times 10^{5}$ receptors and the same $K_d$:
        what changes?
  \item Why is $K_d$ matched to the circulating concentration ---
        what would a receptor with $K_d = \qty{1}{\micro mol/L}$ do
        with a nanomolar \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The cascade.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the overall gain from one occupied receptor to
        glucose molecules per second.
  \item Compute the cAMP molecules the cell makes in the first
        second, and the concentration they reach in its volume.
  \item Compute the glucose output of one liver cell per second at
        the occupancy of question 2, and per minute.
  \item The liver has $2\times 10^{11}$ cells. Compute the output
        the gains predict, in grams per minute, and compare with the
        \qty{5}{g/min} actually observed. What limits the real
        output?
  \item How long would the \qty{100}{g} of glycogen last at this
        rate? Why does it not run out in a fright?
  \item Name the off-switch at each amplifying stage and say which
        acts fastest.
  \item A phosphodiesterase inhibitor (caffeine) is present. Predict
        the effect on the cascade and on the glucose output.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Cortisol to a gene.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the time to transcribe one mRNA.
  \item Compute the time to translate one enzyme molecule, and the
        number of enzyme molecules per mRNA per hour (each mRNA
        living an hour, ribosomes starting one after another).
  \item Compute the enzyme molecules made per hour from the
        \num{100} mRNAs, and the time to reach $10^{5}$.
  \item Add the time for cortisol to enter, bind, and reach the DNA
        (minutes) and explain the observed \qty{4}{h}.
  \item Why would the body not use a cascade like adrenaline's for
        cortisol's job, and why not use cortisol's mechanism for a
        fright?
  \item Cortisol also raises the number of adrenaline receptors in
        liver cells. Explain how a slow \omterm{def:b2:cell-signalling:kinds}{hormone} can amplify a fast
        one, and what this does to the fast one's occupancy curve.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The loop.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item After a meal glucose rises from 5 to \qty{8}{mmol/L}.
        Describe the $\beta$ cell's response from glucose to
        insulin release, naming the coupling.
  \item Insulin acts through a tyrosine kinase, glucagon through
        cAMP. Explain why a single cell can obey both and how the
        liver's glycogen enzymes are set by their ratio.
  \item A patient has no $\beta$ cells. Predict fasting and
        post-meal glucose, and explain why fat is burnt and acid
        appears.
  \item Injected insulin has no feedback. Explain the danger of an
        unusually long run with a usual dose.
  \item Compare the glucose loop with the \omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreflex} of
        \cref{ch:b2:blood-pressure}: sensor, effectors, gain, speed.
  \item State the result: adrenaline's occupancy after release, the
        cascade gain, the liver's glucose output, and the time
        cortisol takes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
