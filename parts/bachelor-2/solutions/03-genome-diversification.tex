\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b2:genome-diversification} --- Mutations and Genome Diversification}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:1}
GAG: silent (still glutamate), a transition (A$\to$G). GTA: missense
(valine), a transversion (A$\to$T). TAA: nonsense (stop), a
transversion (G$\to$T). Inserting a base: a \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{frameshift}, which
rewrites every codon after it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:3}
\omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:hgt}{Transformation}: uptake of free DNA from lysed cells, needs a
competent recipient; transfers chromosomal fragments (capsule genes
in pneumococci). Conjugation: cell-to-cell contact through a pilus,
needs a conjugative \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{plasmid} in the donor; transfers \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{plasmids}
(resistance genes) and, from an Hfr, chromosomal genes. \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:hgt}{Transduction}:
a bacteriophage that packaged host DNA; transfers a random fragment
(generalised) or the genes next to the prophage site (specialised).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:4}
\omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:polyploidy}{Autopolyploid}: extra sets from one species, by a diploid gamete
(tetraploid potato, many autotetraploid clovers). \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:polyploidy}{Allopolyploid}: the
sets of two species combined by hybridisation and then doubled
(bread wheat, cotton, tobacco). The diploid hybrid AB has no two
chromosomes alike, so none can pair at meiosis I; the chromosomes
segregate at random and the gametes are unbalanced and inviable.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:5}
$2\times 3.2\times 10^{9}\times 1.2\times 10^{-8} = 77$ new \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations}.
In coding sequence: $77\times 0.015 = 1.2$; changing an amino acid:
$1.2\times 0.7 = 0.8$ --- most children carry about one new
amino-acid substitution.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:6}
Events: $m(N - N_0) = 2\times 10^{-8}\times 10^{9} = 20$. Mutant
cells: $mN\ln(N/N_0) = 20\times \ln 10^{6} = 20\times 13.8 = 276$.
$p_0 = e^{-20} \approx 2\times 10^{-9}$: every culture has mutants, so
counting cultures without any measures nothing. One needs $m(N -
N_0) \approx 1$, i.e.\ $N \approx 5\times 10^{7}$, where $p_0 \approx
0.37$ and a few dozen cultures give $m$ to within a factor of two.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:7}
Uracil does not belong in DNA, so a uracil-DNA glycosylase can
remove every uracil it finds without ambiguity. Thymine is a normal
base: after 5-methylcytosine deaminates, the G$\cdot$T mismatch is
seen, but the repair machinery has no way to know which strand is
wrong and half the time ``corrects'' the G, fixing a C$\to$T
transition. Methylated CpG sites therefore mutate about ten times
faster than other sites, and over evolutionary time they have been
converted to TpG and CpA: mammalian genomes hold only about a fifth
of the CpG that base composition predicts.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:9}
The second copy of one chromosome pairs with the first copy of the
other; a crossover between them yields one chromatid carrying
copy~1, copy~2 and copy~2$'$ (three genes) and one carrying only
copy~1$'$. A person with a one-gene chromosome from each parent has
two $\alpha$ genes instead of four ($-\alpha/-\alpha$): $\alpha$
chains are made at half rate, the red cells are small and
pale, with mild or no anaemia --- $\alpha$-thalassaemia trait.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:10}
Gametes: $n = 21$ (one set each of A, B, D). Hybrids: AB, 14
chromosomes; ABD, 21. Bread wheat $\times$ emmer: AABBD, 35
chromosomes --- the A and B sets pair, the D set has no partners,
and the plant is largely sterile. Each doubling gave every
chromosome an identical homologue, so bivalents form and gametes are
balanced; crossing back to a parent produces plants with one set
unpaired, whose gametes are unbalanced, so the hexaploid is
reproductively isolated from its ancestors --- a species in one step.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:11}
Logistic growth: $R(t) = N/\bigl(1 + (N - 1)e^{-\gamma Nt}\bigr)$.
Half the population when $(N - 1)e^{-\gamma Nt} = 1$: $t = \ln(N -
1)/\gamma N = 20.7/0.5 = \qty{41}{h}$. Without the antibiotic,
plasmid-free cells grow \qty{5}{\%} faster, so the ratio of carriers
to non-carriers decays as $e^{-st}$ with $s = 0.05\ln 2 =
\qty{0.035}{h^{-1}}$ per hour (\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:division}{generation time} \qty{1}{h}): from
\qty{99}{\%} to \qty{1}{\%} takes $\ln(99^2)/0.035 = \qty{260}{h}$,
eleven days --- and continued transfer can keep the \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{plasmid}
indefinitely at a low frequency, ready for the next course of the
drug.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:genome-diversification:12}
The \omterm{thm:b2:genome-diversification:luria}{fluctuation test} showed that resistance \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations} arise before
and without the selecting agent, at random times, so \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation} is
blind to what would be useful; replica plating showed the same
without exposing the selected cells at all. Evolution is not random
because selection sorts these blind variants by their consequences,
consistently and cumulatively. The one place where randomness is
itself shaped is the rate: lineages with too many or too few errors
lose, so the \omterm{thm:b2:genome-diversification:rate}{mutation rate} is an adapted compromise --- but what
each \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation} does remains unpredictable and unchosen.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b2:genome-diversification:1}
\textbf{1.} Sum 119, mean $5.95$; mean of squares $11489/20 = 574$;
variance $574 - 35 = 540$.
\textbf{2.} Sum 324, mean $16.2$; mean of squares $5462/20 = 273$;
variance $273 - 262 = 11$.
\textbf{3.} The samples from one culture are Poisson (variance close
to the mean): they are random draws from one fixed mutant fraction.
The independent cultures have a variance ninety times the mean:
their mutants arose at different, random times during growth, and
the earlier the \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation} the larger its clone.
\textbf{4.} A jackpot: a \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation} in one of the first few divisions,
whose clone then grew with the culture to a hundred cells.
\textbf{5.} Fourteen of twenty: $p_0 = 0.70$; $m(N - N_0) = -\ln 0.70
= 0.36$; $m = 0.36/2\times 10^{8} = \qty{1.8e-9}{}$ per division.
\textbf{6.} $1.8\times 10^{-9}\times 2\times 10^{8}\times \ln(2\times
10^{6}) = 0.36\times 14.5 = 5.2$ mutant cells; observed $5.95$ ---
agreement within the noise of twenty cultures.
\textbf{7.} No sample was empty (all share the parent culture's
mutants), so $p_0 = 0$ gives no estimate; the samples measure the
mutant fraction of one culture, which depends on when its \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations}
happened to occur.
\textbf{8.} $u = m/20 = \qty{9e-11}{}$ per base pair per replication.
\textbf{9.} $U = 9\times 10^{-11}\times 4.6\times 10^{6} =
\qty{4.1e-4}{}$.
\textbf{10.} $10^{12}$ cells $\times$ $4.1\times 10^{-4}$ $= 4.1\times
10^{8}$ new \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations}.
\textbf{11.} $3\times 4.6\times 10^{6} = 1.4\times 10^{7}$ possible
changes; each arises $4.1\times 10^{8}/1.4\times 10^{7} \approx 30$
times.
\textbf{12.} Every single-base change, including every one that
confers resistance to a drug defeated by one \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation}, is present in
dozens of cells before the drug is added; the drug merely selects
them.
\textbf{13.} Two independent \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations} in the same cell occur at
$m^2 \approx (2\times 10^{-9})^2 = 4\times 10^{-18}$ per division: in
$10^{12}$ cells, $4\times 10^{-6}$ per generation --- a doubly
resistant cell essentially never pre-exists, which is why
tuberculosis is treated with several drugs at once.
\textbf{14.} With $a = N - 1$ and $E = e^{-\gamma Nt}$: $R = N/(1 +
aE)$, $\mathrm{d}R/\mathrm{d}t = \gamma N\cdot NaE/(1 + aE)^2$; and
$\gamma R(N - R) = \gamma\,\frac{N}{1 + aE}\cdot\frac{NaE}{1 + aE}$,
the same; $R(0) = N/(1 + N - 1) = 1$.
\textbf{15.} $(N - 1)E = 1$: $t = \ln(N - 1)/\gamma N = 20.7/0.5 =
\qty{41}{h}$.
\textbf{16.} $1 + aE = 1/0.99$, $aE = 0.0101$: $t = \ln(10^{9}/0.0101)
/0.5 = (20.7 + 4.6)/0.5 = \qty{51}{h}$.
\textbf{17.} $\gamma (N/2)^2 = \gamma N\cdot N/4 = 0.5\times 2.5\times
10^{8} = 1.25\times 10^{8}$ transfers per hour.
\textbf{18.} Growth rates $\ln 2 = \qty{0.693}{h^{-1}}$ and
\qty{0.762}{h^{-1}}; the ratio mutant/sensitive grows as $e^{st}$
with $s = \qty{0.069}{h^{-1}}$; from $1/N$ to 1 takes $\ln N/s =
20.7/0.069 = \qty{300}{h}$, twelve days.
\textbf{19.} Transfer is proportional to the product of carriers and
non-carriers and runs at the rate of encounters, not of divisions;
descent is limited by the mutant's growth advantage, which is small.
A \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{plasmid} crosses the population in two days, a favoured mutant in
two weeks.
\textbf{20.} $U = 4.1\times 10^{-2}$; harmful: $0.041\times 0.88\times
0.3 = 0.011$ per generation.
\textbf{21.} Mean $2.2$: $e^{-2.2} = 0.11$, one descendant in nine
unscathed.
\textbf{22.} Wild type: $1.1\times 10^{-4}$ per generation, $0.022$ in
200: $e^{-0.022} = 0.98$.
\textbf{23.} Mean $520$ mutant cells; $p_0 = e^{-36} \approx
10^{-16}$: no empty culture.
\textbf{24.} Under intense, changing selection (a hospital's rotating
antibiotics) the hundredfold supply of new variants outweighs the
load, and mutators hitch-hike with the resistances they generate. In
a stable environment there is nothing to gain and one harmful
\omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation} per hundred generations to lose, so mutator lineages are
purged.
\textbf{25.} $m = \qty{1.8e-9}{}$ per division to resistance, $u =
\qty{9e-11}{}$ per base pair per replication, $U = \qty{4.1e-4}{}$
\omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations} per genome per replication; the \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{plasmid} reaches half the
gut in \qty{41}{h} and \qty{99}{\%} of it in \qty{51}{h}.
\end{solution}
