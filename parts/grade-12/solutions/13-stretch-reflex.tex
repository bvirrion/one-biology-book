\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g12:stretch-reflex} --- The Stretch Reflex and the Nerve Message}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:1}
Receptor (muscle spindle), sensory \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, integration centre (spinal
cord grey matter, one \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse}), motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, effector (the muscle).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:2}
A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} body with the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus}, dendrites that receive signals, and one
axon (often myelinated) that carries the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}'s message to its
endings. The message travels from dendrites and \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} body along the
axon to the endings.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:3}
A brief reversal of the membrane voltage, from \qty{-70}{mV} to about
\qty{+30}{mV} and back within a millisecond, travelling along the axon
unchanged. All or none: below threshold nothing, above it always the
same full signal. Intensity is coded in the frequency of \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action
potentials}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:4}
\omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{Action potentials} reach the ending; vesicles release the
\omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{neurotransmitter} into the cleft; it binds receptors on the receiving
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, changing its voltage (towards or away from threshold); it is
then destroyed or taken back.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:5}
Acetylcholine. Curare occupies its receptors on the muscle without
activating them; botulinum toxin prevents its release from the
vesicles. Both paralyse.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:6}
6, 12 and 24 spikes in \qty{100}{ms}: 60, 120 and 240 per second. The
size and shape of the spikes do not change.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:7}
Conduction time $30 - 12 = \qty{18}{ms}$ for \qty{1.5}{m}: about
\qty{83}{m/s}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:8}
The dorsal root carries the sensory fibres: cut, the stretch message
never reaches the cord, but the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} and its fibre are intact
and can still be stimulated to contract the muscle. The ventral root
carries the motor fibres: cut, nothing can reach the muscle from the
cord at all.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:9}
If the antagonist contracted too, it would oppose the movement and
stretch itself, triggering its own \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} --- a tug of war. The
interneuron of the cord does the inhibiting, through an inhibitory
\omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse} on the antagonist's motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:10}
1 unit: nothing (below threshold). 4 and 8 units: \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials}
of the same \qty{100}{mV}, but at higher frequencies, higher for 8
than for 4.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:11}
Only the ending holds vesicles of transmitter and only the receiving
membrane holds receptors, so the chemical can act one way. Releasing
the transmitter, its diffusion across the cleft and the receptors'
response take about a millisecond.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:12}
Acetylcholine accumulates in the junctions and keeps firing the
fibres: twitching, then sustained contraction and exhaustion of the
muscles, including those of breathing --- death by asphyxia. A
receptor blocker (an atropine-like drug) reduces the excess
stimulation; too little leaves the poisoning, too much paralyses like
curare, so the dose must match.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:13}
Every sensory input excites its motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} and, normally, inhibits
the antagonists' through interneurons. With inhibition blocked, a
touch excites both sides of every \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:joint}{joint} and the excitation spreads
unopposed through the cord: all muscles contract at once, and the
spasm stretches other muscles whose \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes} add to it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:14}
Conduction time $60 - 12 = \qty{48}{ms}$ for \qty{1.5}{m}: about
\qty{31}{m/s}, a third of normal. Myelin lets the \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potential}
jump between gaps in the sheath instead of travelling continuously;
without it the fibre conducts like a thin unmyelinated one.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:stretch-reflex:15}
The cord carries messages between brain and body, but it also
processes them: its grey matter holds the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapses} of the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex arcs},
where a stretch is turned into a contraction and, through interneurons,
into the inhibition of the antagonist, without any message reaching
the brain. An animal whose cord is cut from its brain still withdraws
a paw from a pinch and still shows the knee jerk: the cord is a
centre, not only a cable.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g12:stretch-reflex:1}
\textbf{1.} $0.75 + 0.75 = \qty{1.5}{m}$.

\textbf{2.} $30 - 4 - 4 - 1 = \qty{21}{ms}$ of conduction: $1.5/0.021
\approx \qty{70}{m/s}$.

\textbf{3.} About \qty{10}{cm} more each way, \qty{0.2}{m} in all:
$0.2/70 \approx \qty{3}{ms}$ more, some \qty{33}{ms}.

\textbf{4.} The electrical signal is the fibres' activation; the
contraction itself --- the sliding of filaments and the pull on the
\omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{tendon} --- takes some tens of milliseconds to develop enough force to
move the leg.

\textbf{5.} A message from the cord to the brain and a command back
travel \qty{1.2}{m} at about \qty{70}{m/s} --- \qty{17}{ms} --- plus
several \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapses} and the brain's own processing: at least \qty{50}{ms},
and a decision far longer. The kick is over before any brain signal
can reach the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}.

\textbf{6.} At 200 per second, 2 \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials} in \qty{10}{ms}
(0.2 at rest). The rate changes; the size and shape of each spike do
not.

\textbf{7.} By a factor of 10.

\textbf{8.} At 20 per second the doses of transmitter are \qty{50}{ms}
apart and each fades before the next; the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} never reaches
threshold. At 200 per second they arrive \qty{5}{ms} apart and add
up: threshold is crossed and the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} fires.

\textbf{9.} Fewer motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} reach threshold and each fires fewer
\omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials}: fewer motor units contract, at a lower rate, and
the kick is weaker.

\textbf{10.} \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{Action potentials} are all or none: their size is fixed by
the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, so it carries no information about the stimulus. Only the
rate can vary.

\textbf{11.} Acetylcholine; after binding the receptors it is destroyed
within milliseconds by an \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} in the cleft.

\textbf{12.} The receptors of the muscle fibre: acetylcholine is
released but cannot act.

\textbf{13.} The release of acetylcholine from the vesicles. In the
laboratory, acetylcholine applied directly to the muscle would make it
contract under botulinum toxin (receptors free) but not under curare
(receptors blocked).

\textbf{14.} The sensory fibre's \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials} and, since nothing
reaches the cord, the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}'s and the electromyogram; the
muscle still contracts if its nerve is stimulated below the block.

\textbf{15.} Curare blocks reception, botulinum toxin blocks release,
the anaesthetic blocks conduction: three steps, one silent muscle.

\textbf{16.} The sensory fibre also excites an interneuron that
inhibits the hamstrings' motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}; their resting activity is
suppressed, and the antagonist relaxes below its resting tone.

\textbf{17.} The \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} needs only the cord, which is intact at that
level. Its exaggeration shows that the brain normally sends signals
that damp the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex}; without them the arc runs at full sensitivity.

\textbf{18.} The spindle, the sensory nerve or dorsal root, the cord's
grey matter at that level, the motor nerve or ventral root, the muscle
itself. Stimulating the motor nerve directly and recording the muscle
tells whether the motor side works; recording the sensory nerve during
a stretch tells whether the sensory side does.

\textbf{19.} It tests, in one tap, the whole arc --- sensory nerve,
cord, motor nerve, muscle --- and the brain's damping of it: an absent,
weak or exaggerated \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} points to a level of the nervous system
before any other sign.

\textbf{20.} About \qty{70}{m/s}; the frequency of the spindle's
\omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials}; the binding of acetylcholine to the muscle's
receptors.
\end{solution}
