\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b1:species-interactions} --- Interactions Between Species}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:1}
\omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{Competition} $-/-$ (two oaks for light); \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{predation} $+/-$ (lynx, hare);
\omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{parasitism} $+/-$ (tick, deer); \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{mutualism} $+/+$ (bee, flower);
\omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{commensalism} $+/0$ (fern on bark); amensalism $-/0$ (grass under an
oak's shade).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:2}
Two species using the same limiting resource in the same way cannot
coexist indefinitely in a stable environment. The five warblers use
the same food but in different parts of the tree and at different
times: their \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{niches} differ, so no two of them are the ``same way''.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:3}
\omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:functional}{Handling time}: the time to catch, eat and digest one prey, during
which no other is taken. Attack rate: the area searched per unit time
times the probability of capture. Maximum rate $1/h = 2$ prey per
hour.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:4}
A species whose removal changes the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community}'s structure out of
proportion to its abundance, usually by controlling a dominant
competitor. Abundance measures presence, not effect; a rare predator
that eats the would-be winner has an effect its numbers do not show.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:5}
\omterm{prop:b1:species-interactions:lotka}{Isocline} 1: $N_1 + 0.5 N_2 = 500$ (intercepts 500 on $N_1$, 1000 on
$N_2$); \omterm{prop:b1:species-interactions:lotka}{isocline} 2: $N_2 + 0.6 N_1 = 400$ (400 on $N_2$, 667 on $N_1$).
They cross with each species limited more by itself ($\alpha = 0.5 <
K_1/K_2 = 1.25$; $\beta = 0.6 < K_2/K_1 = 0.8$): stable coexistence
at $N_1 = 429$, $N_2 = 143$. With $\alpha = 2$: \omterm{prop:b1:species-interactions:lotka}{isocline} 1 intercepts
500 and 250, both inside \omterm{prop:b1:species-interactions:lotka}{isocline} 2's (667 and 400): species 2
always wins.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:6}
$f = aN/(1 + ahN)$: at 10, $2/(1 + 0.5) = 1.33$ per hour; at 100,
$20/(1 + 5) = 3.33$ per hour (maximum $1/h = 4$). Half saturation at
$N = 1/(ah) = 20$ per square metre.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:7}
With a type III response the fraction of prey eaten rises with
density at low density: rare prey are taken proportionally less and
can recover. With a type II the fraction eaten is highest at low
density (the predator is never saturated there), so a small prey
\omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{population} is pushed further down.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:8}
\emph{P.~caudatum} used the same bacteria in the same water column
as \emph{P.~aurelia}, and the faster, more frugal \emph{P.~aurelia}
drove the food below what it needed. \emph{P.~bursaria} fed at the
bottom on different resources: the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{niche}, not the growth rate, decided
--- \emph{P.~bursaria} grows no faster than \emph{P.~caudatum}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:9}
Predators meet the pattern more often without harm, learn it less
well, and attack it more: protection falls for both mimic and model.
The mimic's fitness therefore falls as it becomes common, a negative
frequency dependence that keeps it rare relative to the model.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:10}
Without the predator the mussel's \omterm{prop:b1:species-interactions:lotka}{isocline} lies outside every
competitor's: it wins the rock. \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{Predation} lowers the mussel's
effective $K$ (its \omterm{prop:b1:species-interactions:lotka}{isocline} is pulled inward) until it crosses the
others' \omterm{prop:b1:species-interactions:lotka}{isoclines} from the inside: the competitors are now limited
more by themselves than by the mussel, and coexist with it. The
predator acts on the strongest competitor and makes room for the rest.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:11}
When phosphate limits growth, \qty{80}{\%} of the phosphate is worth
far more than \qty{15}{\%} of the sugar: \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{mutualism}. In a phosphate-rich
soil the plant would take up the phosphate itself and the fungus
costs \qty{15}{\%} for nothing: \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{parasitism}. Experiment: grow plants
with and without the fungus at a range of phosphate levels and
measure biomass; the association is mutualistic where the mycorrhizal
plants grow larger, parasitic where they grow smaller.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:species-interactions:12}
Exclosures: fenced plots along the rivers that elk cannot enter,
paired with open plots, replicated in several valleys with and
without wolf packs, measured for years before and after
reintroduction; record willow height and cover, elk density and time
spent in each plot (tracks, cameras), hunting and snow depth. The
cascade predicts willows recovering in open plots only where wolves
are, at the same rate as inside the exclosures; if willows recover
equally with and without wolves, or track snow and hunting instead,
the cascade is refuted or shared with those factors.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b1:species-interactions:1}
\textbf{1.} A: $N_A + 1.4 N_B = 200$, intercepts 200 ($N_A$) and 143
($N_B$). B: $N_B + 0.9 N_A = 130$, intercepts 130 ($N_B$) and 144
($N_A$).
\textbf{2.} The intercepts (200 against 144 on the $N_A$ axis, 143
against 130 on the $N_B$ axis) put A's \omterm{prop:b1:species-interactions:lotka}{isocline} entirely outside B's:
solving $N_A = 200 - 1.4 N_B$ with $N_B = 130 - 0.9 N_A$ gives $N_A = -69$,
so the lines meet outside the positive quadrant. A wins from any starting
point: wherever B can still grow, A grows too and outlasts it.
\textbf{3.} $T_A = 0.87$ days, $T_B = 1.26$ days: A, the faster
grower.
\textbf{4.} A: $N_A + 0.3 N_C = 200$ (200, 667); C: $N_C + 0.3 N_A =
100$ (100, 333). Each species limits itself more than the other
($0.3 < 200/100$ and $0.3 < 100/200 = 0.5$): stable coexistence.
\textbf{5.} A forest is not constant (seasons, disturbances reset the
race before it is decided) and has many resources and places, so
that species partition \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{niches} instead of sharing one.
\textbf{6.} $N_A = 200 - 0.3 N_C$, $N_C = 100 - 0.3 N_A = 100 - 60 +
0.09 N_C$, so $N_C = 44$ and $N_A = 187$.
\textbf{7.} Rising and flattening toward about 10: type II.
\textbf{8.} $(1/N, 1/f)$: $(0.2, 0.5)$, $(0.1, 0.303)$, $(0.05, 0.2)$,
$(0.025, 0.149)$, $(0.0125, 0.125)$: a straight line.
\textbf{9.} Slope $(0.5 - 0.125)/(0.2 - 0.0125) = 2.0 = 1/a$, so $a =
\qty{0.5}{m^2/d}$; intercept $0.5 - 2\times 0.2 = 0.1$ day $= h$.
\textbf{10.} Maximum $1/h = 10$ prey per day; half-saturation $N =
1/(ah) = 20$ per square metre.
\textbf{11.} Handling fraction $= f h = f/10$: more than half when $f
> 5$, i.e.\ at 40 and 80 per square metre (5.0 at 20 is exactly half).
\textbf{12.} Prey growth $0.1 N (1 - N/200)$ equals consumption
$0.5N/(1 + 0.05N)$: $0.1(1 - N/200) = 0.5/(1 + 0.05 N)$, i.e.\
$(1 - N/200)(1 + 0.05N) = 5$: $1 + 0.05N - N/200 - N^2/4000 = 5$, so
$N^2 - 180 N + 16\,000 = 0$, $N = 90 \pm \sqrt{8100 - 16\,000}$: no
real \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} --- one beetle per square metre eats more than the prey can
ever produce (maximum growth $rK/4 = 5$ per day, at $N = 100$, where
the beetle eats 8.3) and drives it to extinction. No equilibrium; the
prey persists only if the beetles are fewer or the prey has refuges.
\textbf{13.} \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:functional}{Handling time} halves for the second prey ($h' = 0.05$),
so at saturation the beetle eats up to 20 of them a day: total intake
at high density roughly doubles if it switches to the quicker prey.
\textbf{14.} $H_{15} = \ln 15 = 2.71$. Eight species: $-(0.85\ln 0.85
+ 7\times 0.0214\ln 0.0214) = 0.138 + 0.576 = 0.71$.
\textbf{15.} By a factor $2.71/0.71 = 3.8$.
\textbf{16.} The sea star's $-$ on the mussel kept the mussel's $-$ on
its competitors weak; remove the first and the mussel reaches the
density at which its \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{competition} excludes the rest.
\textbf{17.} Biomass measures how much of the energy flow a species
holds, not what its interactions do; a keystone's effect goes through
the species it controls, whose biomass is large.
\textbf{18.} Removing the sea star would have released a rare species
that could not take over the rock: the mussel-dominated \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community}
would have been little changed, and the sea star would not have been
a keystone.
\textbf{19.} Cages excluding the sea star from small patches, with
open cages as controls for the cage's own effect: if mussels take
over only in the closed cages, \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{predation} on mussels is the mechanism;
cages with the sea star present but mussels removed by hand would
test whether the sea star's other prey matter.
\textbf{20.} Gain $\qty{40}{mg}$, cost $8\times 0.5 = \qty{4}{mg}$: net
\qty{36}{mg} $=$ \qty{0.58}{kJ}.
\textbf{21.} \qty{40}{\micro g} of \qty{4}{mg}: \qty{1}{\%} of the
flower's daily photosynthate buys about three pollen transfers, the
plant's reproduction.
\textbf{22.} Each takes something from the other, but each gains more
than it loses: the sign is that of the net effect, not of the
exploitation.
\textbf{23.} Robber $+$, plant $-$ (nectar taken, no pollination): a
\omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{parasitism} of the \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:types}{mutualism}; the bee finds emptied flowers and gains
less, an indirect $-$.
\textbf{24.} Plant $\leftrightarrow$ bee $+/+$; robber $\to$ plant $-$,
robber $\to$ bee $-$; bird $\to$ robber $-$. Two minus signs: the bird
has an indirect $+$ on the plant and on the bee.
\textbf{25.} $a = \qty{0.5}{m^2/d}$, $h = \qty{0.1}{d}$ (\qty{2.4}{h}),
maximum \num{10} prey per day.
\end{solution}
