\chapter{Renal Physiology and Osmoregulation}\label{ch:b3:renal-osmoregulation}

Your kidneys filter a hundred and eighty litres of plasma a day ---
your whole blood volume every half hour --- and return all but a litre
and a half of it, having taken out the urea, adjusted the salt, the
acid and the water to within a per cent of what the body needs, and
kept every gram of glucose. A kangaroo rat in the desert never drinks
at all: it lives on the water its metabolism makes from dry seeds and
excretes a urine five times more concentrated than sea water. A
marine fish drinks constantly and pumps the salt out through its
gills; a freshwater fish never drinks and pumps salt in. The problem
they all solve is the same: to hold the composition of the fluid
around the cells constant while eating, drinking and breathing
disturb it, and to discard nitrogen without discarding too much
water. This chapter treats the mammalian kidney as the worked
example --- filtration, the arithmetic of clearance, the countercurrent
trick that concentrates urine, and the hormones that set the
dials --- and then the range of solutions across the animals.

\section{The problem}

\begin{definition}[Osmoregulation]\label{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}
\index{osmolarity}\index{osmotic pressure}\index{extracellular fluid}\index{osmoconformer}\index{osmoregulator}
The \emph{osmolarity} of a solution is its total concentration of
dissolved particles (in osmoles per litre); it sets the \emph{osmotic
pressure}, $\Pi = cRT$ for dilute solutions, which drives water across
any membrane that passes water but not solute. A mammal's body fluids
sit at about \qty{300}{mOsm/L} --- \qty{40}{\%} of body mass inside the
cells, \qty{20}{\%} outside, the \emph{extracellular fluid} of which a
quarter is plasma --- and cells swell or shrink within seconds if the
outside changes, since their membranes pass water freely. An
\emph{osmoconformer} (most marine invertebrates, sharks) keeps its
fluids at the osmolarity of the sea and regulates only their
composition; an \emph{osmoregulator} (vertebrates, insects,
freshwater animals) holds its osmolarity constant against the
environment, which costs energy in proportion to the gradient. The
second task, coupled to the first, is to dispose of the nitrogen of
protein breakdown: as \emph{ammonia} into abundant water (fish), as
\emph{urea}, soluble and non-toxic but needing water to carry it
(mammals), or as \emph{uric acid}, a paste excreted almost dry (birds,
reptiles, insects).
\end{definition}

\section{The nephron and filtration}

\begin{definition}[The nephron]\label{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}
\index{nephron}\index{glomerulus}\index{proximal tubule}\index{loop of Henle}\index{collecting duct}
Each human kidney holds about a million \emph{nephrons}. Blood enters
a \emph{glomerulus}, a tuft of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule, whose
walls --- fenestrated endothelium, basement membrane, and the slit
diaphragms between the podocytes' feet --- pass water and every solute
below about \qty{60}{kDa} but hold back the proteins and cells. The
filtrate flows through the \emph{proximal tubule}, which reabsorbs two
thirds of the water and salt and all the glucose and amino acids; down
and up the \emph{loop of Henle}, which reaches into the medulla and
builds the concentration gradient of the next section; through the
\emph{distal tubule}, where salt is adjusted; and into the
\emph{collecting duct}, where the final water content is set by
vasopressin as the duct passes through the medulla to the pelvis. The
blood that leaves the glomerulus enters a second capillary bed around
the tubules, which takes up what they reabsorb.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Filtration and clearance]\label{thm:b3:renal-osmoregulation:clearance}
\index{Starling forces}\index{glomerular filtration rate}\index{clearance}\index{inulin}\index{creatinine}
The filtrate flows across the glomerular wall at a rate set by the
\emph{\omterm{thm:b3:renal-osmoregulation:clearance}{Starling forces}}: the hydrostatic pressure of the capillary
$P_{\text{GC}}$ pushes fluid out, the hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's
space $P_{\text{BS}}$ and the oncotic pressure of the plasma proteins
$\pi_{\text{GC}}$ pull it back, and
\[
\text{GFR} = K_{f}\,\bigl(P_{\text{GC}} - P_{\text{BS}} - \pi_{\text{GC}}\bigr)
\approx K_{f}\,(55 - 15 - 30)\ \unit{mmHg} = K_{f}\times \qty{10}{mmHg},
\]
the \emph{\omterm{thm:b3:renal-osmoregulation:clearance}{glomerular filtration rate}}, about \qty{125}{mL/min} in an
adult. For any substance $x$ with plasma concentration $P_{x}$, urine
concentration $U_{x}$ and urine flow $V$, the \emph{clearance}
\[
C_{x} = \frac{U_{x}\,V}{P_{x}}
\]
is the volume of plasma cleared of $x$ per unit time. If $x$ is freely
filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted (inulin, and nearly so
creatinine), $C_{x} = \text{GFR}$; if it is completely removed from the
plasma passing the kidney (para-aminohippurate at low dose), $C_{x}$
equals the renal plasma flow; a clearance below the GFR means net
reabsorption, above it net secretion, and the filtered load $\text{GFR}
\times P_{x}$ minus the excretion $U_{x}V$ is the amount reabsorbed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Filtration is ultrafiltration through a porous wall: flow is
proportional to the net pressure across it, and the net pressure is
the outward hydrostatic minus the inward hydrostatic and oncotic
pressures (Starling), the oncotic pressure of the filtrate being
negligible since it holds no protein. Clearance: in unit time the
kidney excretes $U_{x}V$ of $x$; the plasma volume that contained this
amount is $U_{x}V/P_{x}$. For a substance that is only filtered, what
is excreted is what was filtered, $\text{GFR}\times P_{x} = U_{x}V$,
whence $C_{x} = \text{GFR}$. For one wholly extracted, all the $x$ in
the plasma flowing through, $\text{RPF}\times P_{x}$, is excreted,
whence $C_{x} = \text{RPF}$. Mass balance gives the reabsorbed amount.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Clearances in numbers]\label{ex:b3:renal-osmoregulation:numbers}
Inulin infused to a plasma level of \qty{1}{mg/mL} appears in urine at
\qty{125}{mg/mL} with a flow of \qty{1}{mL/min}: $C = 125\times 1/1 =
\qty{125}{mL/min}$, the GFR, \qty{180}{L/day}. Para-aminohippurate
gives \qty{650}{mL/min}, the plasma flow; with a haematocrit of $0.45$
the renal blood flow is \qty{1.2}{L/min}, a fifth of the cardiac
output for \qty{0.5}{\%} of the body mass, and the filtration fraction
$125/650 = 0.19$. Urea: plasma \qty{5}{mmol/L}, urine \qty{300}{mmol/L}
at \qty{1}{mL/min} --- clearance \qty{60}{mL/min}, half the GFR, so half
the filtered urea is reabsorbed. Glucose: clearance zero at normal
plasma levels, every molecule of the \qty{180}{g} filtered a day
returned. Creatinine, made by muscle at a constant rate and only
filtered, is the clinic's measure of GFR: when the GFR halves the
plasma creatinine doubles, without any collection of urine.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Measuring kidney function]\label{met:b3:renal-osmoregulation:gfr}
\index{GFR measurement}
(1) For a research value, infuse inulin to a steady plasma level,
collect timed urine, measure both, and compute $UV/P$. (2) In the
clinic, measure the plasma creatinine and estimate the GFR from it
with a formula that corrects for age, sex and body size; the estimate
is what the stages of chronic kidney disease are defined by (a GFR
below \qty{60}{mL/min} for three months). (3) Test the filter's
selectivity by measuring albumin in the urine --- normally under
\qty{30}{mg/day}; more means a leaking barrier, the earliest sign of
diabetic kidney damage. (4) Test the concentrating ability by
withholding water overnight and measuring the urine \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}, which
should exceed \qty{800}{mOsm/L}; failure to concentrate with a normal
GFR points to the \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{collecting duct} or to vasopressin.
\end{method}

\section{Concentrating the urine}

\begin{proposition}[The countercurrent multiplier]\label{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:countercurrent}
\index{countercurrent multiplication}\index{single effect}\index{vasopressin}\index{aquaporin}\index{urea recycling}
The \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{loop of Henle} builds a gradient of \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} from \qty{300}{mOsm/L}
at the cortex to \qty{1200}{mOsm/L} at the tip of the medulla, without
any cell pumping against more than a fraction of it. The thick
ascending limb pumps NaCl out of its fluid into the interstitium and
is impermeable to water, so at each level it makes the interstitium
about \qty{200}{mOsm/L} saltier than its own contents --- the
\emph{\omterm{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:countercurrent}{single effect}}. The descending limb is permeable to water and
equilibrates with the interstitium, so the fluid flowing down grows
saltier as it descends and delivers to the bend a fluid already
concentrated, which the ascending limb then pumps from; the fluid
flowing up grows more dilute than the interstitium at every level and
leaves the loop at \qty{100}{mOsm/L}, hypotonic. Countercurrent flow
converts a small transverse effect into a large longitudinal gradient:
the saltiest fluid at the bend was made from fluid that was already
salty, stage upon stage. The \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{collecting duct} then runs down through
this gradient. Without \emph{vasopressin} (antidiuretic hormone) its
wall is impermeable to water and a litre or more of dilute urine
leaves each hour; with it, \emph{aquaporin-2} channels are inserted into
the luminal membrane, water leaves down the gradient into the
medulla, and the urine reaches the \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} of the tip,
\qty{1200}{mOsm/L}. Urea, reabsorbed from the inner duct and trapped in
the medulla, supplies half the tip's \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}, and the vasa recta,
themselves in countercurrent, remove the reabsorbed water without
washing the gradient away.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Wirz, Hargitay and Kuhn (1951) froze rat kidneys and measured the
melting point of the fluid in slices from cortex to papilla: the
\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} rose steadily with depth, reaching the urine's value at the
tip --- the gradient the theory of the countercurrent multiplier (Kuhn,
a physical chemist, 1942) had required. Micropuncture of the tubules
(Gottschalk, 1959) then found the fluid at the bend of the loop
hypertonic and that leaving the ascending limb hypotonic, with the
\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{collecting duct}'s fluid matching the interstitium at each level when
vasopressin was present. The desert rodents with the longest loops had
the steepest gradients and the most concentrated urine.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The arithmetic of water]\label{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:water}
\index{obligatory water loss}\index{free-water clearance}
A body that must excrete a daily solute load $S$ (about
\qty{600}{mOsm}, mostly urea and salt) at a maximal urine \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}
$U_{\max}$ needs a minimum urine volume $V_{\min} = S/U_{\max}$: for a
human, $600/1200 = \qty{0.5}{L}$ a day, the \emph{obligatory} water
loss, to which the lungs and skin add about \qty{0.9}{L}. Drinking
sea water (about \qty{1000}{mOsm/L}, nearly all salt) supplies a litre
of water with \qty{1000}{mOsm} of salt that must be excreted; at
$U_{\max}$ that takes $1000/1200 = \qty{0.83}{L}$ of urine, but the
salt is excreted with a urea load as well, and the net gain is close
to zero or negative: a shipwrecked sailor who drinks sea water
dehydrates faster than one who does not. The \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:water}{free-water
clearance}}, $V - U_{\text{osm}}V/P_{\text{osm}}$, is the volume of
solute-free water the kidney adds to or removes from the body per unit
time: positive in a water load (dilute urine), negative in
dehydration.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Solute leaves only in urine (sweat aside), at concentration at most
$U_{\max}$, so $S = U V \le U_{\max}V$ gives $V \ge S/U_{\max}$. For sea
water: a litre brings \qty{1000}{mOsm}; excreting them at
\qty{1200}{mOsm/L} takes \qty{0.83}{L}, leaving \qty{0.17}{L}, before
counting the day's own \qty{600}{mOsm} of metabolic solute, whose
excretion consumes it. \omterm{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:water}{Free-water clearance}: the urine of volume $V$
and \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} $U_{\text{osm}}$ contains solute $U_{\text{osm}}V$ that
would occupy $U_{\text{osm}}V/P_{\text{osm}}$ at plasma \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}; the
rest of the volume is water in excess of that, removed from the body.
\end{proof}

\section{Regulation}

\begin{definition}[The controls]\label{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:controls}
\index{renin--angiotensin--aldosterone system}\index{aldosterone}\index{atrial natriuretic peptide}\index{osmoreceptor}\index{tubuloglomerular feedback}
Two variables are regulated separately: the \emph{volume} of the
\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{extracellular fluid}, which is a matter of how much sodium the body
holds, and its \emph{\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}}, which is a matter of water. Volume:
cells of the juxtaglomerular apparatus, where the distal tubule
touches its own \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{glomerulus}'s arteriole, release \emph{renin} when
arterial pressure or tubular salt falls; renin cleaves angiotensin I
from a plasma protein, converting enzyme makes \emph{angiotensin II},
which constricts arterioles, stimulates thirst, and releases
\emph{aldosterone} from the adrenal cortex, which over hours makes the
distal tubule and \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{collecting duct} reabsorb sodium (through the channel
ENaC) and secrete potassium. The stretched atria release
\emph{natriuretic peptide}, which does the opposite. \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{Osmolarity}:
\emph{osmoreceptors} in the hypothalamus sense a rise of \qty{1}{\%} and
release vasopressin from the posterior pituitary within minutes, and
drive thirst; a fall shuts it off. The kidney also regulates itself:
\emph{tubuloglomerular feedback} constricts the arteriole of a
\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{nephron} whose distal salt delivery is too high, holding each
\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{nephron}'s filtration to what its tubule can handle. And acid--base:
the \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{proximal tubule} recovers the filtered bicarbonate, the \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{collecting
duct} secretes protons against a thousandfold gradient and excretes
them buffered on phosphate and, above all, as ammonium made from
glutamine --- the body's route for disposing of the acid of a
protein-rich diet.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Two days]\label{ex:b3:renal-osmoregulation:two-days}
A day without water: the plasma \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} rises from $290$ toward
\qty{295}{mOsm/L}, vasopressin rises within minutes, the \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{collecting
ducts} open to water, the urine concentrates to \qty{1200}{mOsm/L} and
falls to half a litre; the person is thirsty; the volume lost is
mostly from the cells, whose water follows the extracellular salt.
A litre of water drunk at once: \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} falls \qty{1}{\%},
vasopressin falls to nothing within twenty minutes, the ducts close,
and over the next two hours the kidney passes a litre of urine at
\qty{50}{mOsm/L}. A haemorrhage of a litre: pressure falls, renin
and vasopressin both rise (volume overrides \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}), the
arterioles constrict, sodium and water are kept, and over a day the
plasma volume is refilled --- with fluid drawn from the interstitium and
then drunk. Diabetes insipidus, the absence of vasopressin or of its
receptor, produces \qty{15}{L} of dilute urine a day and a thirst to
match; kidney failure, the absence of filtration, leaves a body that
must be filtered by a machine three times a week, through a membrane
whose clearance is a fraction of two kidneys'.
\end{example}

\section{Other animals, other solutions}

\begin{definition}[Osmoregulation across the animals]\label{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:comparative}
\index{Malpighian tubule}\index{salt gland}\index{chloride cell}\index{metabolic water}\index{relative medullary thickness}
Freshwater fish live in a medium a tenth the \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} of their blood:
water floods in through the gills, salt leaks out, and they excrete a
copious dilute urine and take salt up actively through the gills'
\emph{chloride cells}, never drinking. Marine teleosts, whose blood is
a third the sea's \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}, lose water through the gills, drink sea
water, and pump the salt out through the same chloride cells reversed,
keeping the urine scant; sharks instead hold their blood iso-osmotic
with the sea by retaining urea (and trimethylamine oxide to stabilise
their proteins against it), and excrete excess salt through a rectal
gland. Sea birds and marine reptiles carry \emph{salt glands} above
the eyes that secrete a brine twice as salty as the sea, letting an
albatross drink from the ocean. Insects filter through
\emph{Malpighian tubules} driven by active potassium secretion rather
than blood pressure, and reabsorb water in the rectum so completely
that a desert beetle's droppings are dry. Among mammals the
concentrating power scales with the length of the loops relative to
the kidney's size (the \emph{relative medullary thickness}): a beaver
manages \qty{500}{mOsm/L}, a human \num{1200}, a camel \num{3000}, the
kangaroo rat \num{5500} --- which lets it live on the \emph{metabolic
water} released when its food is oxidised, about \qty{0.6}{g} of water
per gram of starch, with a nose that recovers the water from each
exhaled breath by countercurrent cooling.
\end{definition}

\begin{omfigure}
\includegraphics[width=0.56\linewidth]{images/book5/ai/b5-20-kangaroo-rat.jpg}

{\small A kangaroo rat, which never drinks: long loops of Henle, a urine
five times as concentrated as sea water, a cool nose that condenses the
water of each breath, and a diet whose oxidation makes all the water
it needs.}
\end{omfigure}

\begin{remark}[The kidney as a design problem]\label{rem:b3:renal-osmoregulation:design}
The mammalian kidney filters forty times the plasma volume a day only
to take nearly all of it back: an extravagant design, whose point is
that filtering everything and reabsorbing selectively is simpler than
secreting selectively every waste that might appear. Its cost is the
\qty{7}{\%} of the resting metabolism spent pumping sodium back, and
its vulnerability is the delicate filter, which fails slowly under
high pressure and high glucose. Its cleverness is the countercurrent
loop, a device also found in the swim bladders of deep fish, the legs
of wading birds and the noses of desert rodents, by which a small,
affordable local difference is stacked into a large one --- the same
idea, in each case, that a gradient can be built from flow.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:1}
Name the segments of the \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{nephron} in order and state one thing each
does.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:2}
Define clearance. A substance has plasma concentration
\qty{2}{mg/mL}, urine concentration \qty{100}{mg/mL} and the urine flow
is \qty{1.5}{mL/min}. Compute its clearance and say whether it is
reabsorbed, secreted or neither, if the GFR is \qty{120}{mL/min}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:3}
Why does a freshwater fish never drink and a marine fish drink
continuously? What do the gills do in each?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:4}
What is the \omterm{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:countercurrent}{single effect} of the \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{loop of Henle}, which limb produces
it, and why must the two limbs differ in their permeability to water?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:5}
Glomerular capillary pressure \qty{60}{mmHg}, Bowman's space
\qty{18}{mmHg}, plasma oncotic pressure \qty{28}{mmHg} at the afferent
end rising to \qty{35}{mmHg} at the efferent end. Compute the net
filtration pressure at each end and explain why filtration may stop
before the end of the capillary. What does that imply for the effect
of plasma flow on GFR?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:6}
A patient's creatinine clearance is \qty{40}{mL/min}. Plasma sodium is
\qty{140}{mmol/L}, urine sodium \qty{70}{mmol/L}, urine flow
\qty{1}{mL/min}. Compute the filtered load of sodium, the excretion,
the fraction reabsorbed, and the sodium clearance.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:7}
A person eats a diet producing \qty{900}{mOsm} of solute a day and can
concentrate to \qty{1200}{mOsm/L}. Minimum urine volume? If kidney
disease lowers the maximum to \qty{400}{mOsm/L}, how much must they
drink to stay in balance, allowing \qty{0.9}{L} of insensible loss?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:8}
Predict the urine volume and \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}, the plasma sodium and the
state of thirst in (a) diabetes insipidus, (b) inappropriate secretion
of vasopressin by a \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumour}, (c) \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:controls}{aldosterone} excess, (d) a diet with no
salt for a week.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:9}
Compute the \omterm{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:water}{free-water clearance} for urine of \qty{100}{mOsm/L} at
\qty{6}{mL/min} and for urine of \qty{1000}{mOsm/L} at
\qty{0.6}{mL/min}, with plasma at \qty{300}{mOsm/L}. Interpret each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:10}
Model the loop as $n$ stages in which the ascending fluid at each
stage is \qty{200}{mOsm/L} below the interstitium and the descending
fluid equals it, with the fluid entering at \qty{300}{mOsm/L}. Argue
that the steady-state \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} at the bend can approach $300 +
200n$, so that the gradient is limited by the loop's length and the
pump's \omterm{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:countercurrent}{single effect}, and say what sets $n$ physically.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:11}
A kangaroo rat eats \qty{5}{g} of dry seed a day, \qty{80}{\%} starch,
oxidised to CO$_{2}$ and water (\qty{0.6}{g} water per gram of starch).
It must excrete \qty{3}{mOsm} of solute a day at \qty{5500}{mOsm/L},
and loses \qty{1.5}{g} of water a day by evaporation from a nose that
recovers \qty{70}{\%} of the water in its breath. Balance its water
budget and say whether it survives without drinking.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:renal-osmoregulation:12}
Dialysis passes blood along one side of a membrane and dialysate along
the other, in countercurrent. Explain why countercurrent flow clears
more than parallel flow, estimate the clearance of a machine that
removes urea from \qty{300}{mL/min} of blood with \qty{70}{\%}
efficiency, and compare with two kidneys over a week if the machine
runs four hours three times a week.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: Eighteen Buckets a Day}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a day's filtrate followed from the
glomerulus to the bladder: the Starling pressures, the clearances of
four substances, the glucose threshold, the water budget from maximal
dilution to sea water, and a desert rodent's balance, ending on the
GFR, the obligatory urine volume and the net water a litre of sea water
yields}]
\label{pb:b3:renal-osmoregulation:1}
Data: $K_{f} = \qty{12.5}{mL/min/mmHg}$; $P_{\text{GC}} = \qty{55}{mmHg}$,
$P_{\text{BS}} = \qty{15}{mmHg}$, $\pi_{\text{GC}} = \qty{30}{mmHg}$.
Plasma: inulin \qty{0.5}{mg/mL}, creatinine \qty{0.01}{mg/mL}, urea
\qty{5}{mmol/L}, glucose \qty{5}{mmol/L}, para-aminohippurate
\qty{0.02}{mg/mL}, Na$^{+}$ \qty{140}{mmol/L}, \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity}
\qty{290}{mOsm/L}. Urine flow \qty{1}{mL/min}: inulin \qty{62.5}{mg/mL},
creatinine \qty{1.3}{mg/mL}, urea \qty{250}{mmol/L}, glucose $0$,
para-aminohippurate \qty{13}{mg/mL}, Na$^{+}$ \qty{100}{mmol/L},
\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} \qty{600}{mOsm/L}. Haematocrit $0.45$. Glucose $T_{m} =
\qty{375}{mg/min}$ (\qty{2.1}{mmol/min}). Daily solute \qty{600}{mOsm};
$U_{\max} = \qty{1200}{mOsm/L}$, $U_{\min} = \qty{50}{mOsm/L}$;
insensible loss \qty{0.9}{L/day}; sea water \qty{1000}{mOsm/L}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The filter.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the net filtration pressure and the GFR.
  \item Convert the GFR to litres per day. How many times is the plasma
        volume (\qty{3}{L}) filtered?
  \item Compute the inulin clearance and compare it with the GFR.
        Compute the creatinine clearance; why is it slightly larger?
  \item Compute the para-aminohippurate clearance (the renal plasma
        flow), the renal blood flow, and the filtration fraction.
  \item The efferent arteriole constricts, raising $P_{\text{GC}}$ to
        \qty{60}{mmHg}. New GFR? Why do drugs that block angiotensin II
        lower the GFR a little in a healthy kidney and protect a
        diabetic one?
  \item Protein appears in the urine at \qty{3}{g/day}. Which layer of
        the filter has failed, and what happens to the plasma oncotic
        pressure and to the tissues if it continues?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Handling.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Urea: filtered load, excretion, fraction reabsorbed, and
        clearance.
  \item Sodium: filtered load per day in moles and grams, excretion,
        and the fraction reabsorbed. How many kilograms of salt would
        be lost in a day if nothing were reabsorbed?
  \item Glucose: filtered load in mmol/min and in grams per day; is any
        excreted? At what plasma glucose does the filtered load reach
        $T_{m}$ (the threshold)?
  \item A diabetic has plasma glucose \qty{20}{mmol/L}. Glucose excreted
        per minute and per day in grams, and the extra urine volume if
        each \qty{300}{mOsm} of glucose carries \qty{1}{L} at the
        prevailing concentration. Explain the thirst and the weight
        loss of untreated diabetes.
  \item Reabsorbing sodium costs one ATP per three Na$^{+}$. How many
        moles of ATP a day does the kidney spend on sodium, and how
        many kilojoules (\qty{50}{kJ/mol})? Compare with a resting
        \qty{7}{MJ/day}.
  \item Explain why the kidney filters and reabsorbs rather than
        secreting each waste, in terms of what the tubule would need to
        recognise.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Water.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Osmolar clearance $U_{\text{osm}}V/P_{\text{osm}}$ from the
        data, and the \omterm{prop:b3:renal-osmoregulation:water}{free-water clearance}. Is the person conserving or
        shedding water?
  \item Minimum urine volume for the daily solute; maximum (at
        $U_{\min}$); the total daily water need at the minimum, with
        insensible loss.
  \item A litre of sea water is drunk. How much urine is needed to
        excrete its salt at $U_{\max}$, and what is the net water gain
        before the day's own solute is counted? After?
  \item Vasopressin is absent (diabetes insipidus) and the urine stays
        at $U_{\min}$. Daily urine volume for the same solute, and the
        water that must be drunk each day to keep balance.
  \item Beer at \qty{20}{mOsm/L}: how much can be drunk per day before
        the kidney, at $U_{\min}$, can no longer excrete the water?
        (Water in excess of what the solute can carry at $U_{\min}$
        accumulates.)
  \item After a night without water the plasma \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} is
        \qty{298}{mOsm/L}. By what per cent has it risen, and what does
        the hypothalamus do? Estimate the water deficit if the total
        body water is \qty{42}{L} and solute is unchanged.
  \item Explain why a marathon runner who drinks \qty{6}{L} of water
        while sweating salt can develop a dangerously low plasma sodium,
        and what the kidney would need to do to prevent it.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The desert.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A kangaroo rat excretes \qty{3}{mOsm} a day at \qty{5500}{mOsm/L}.
        Urine volume? Compare with the volume a human kidney would
        need for the same solute at its own maximum.
  \item Its food yields \qty{2.4}{g} of \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:comparative}{metabolic water} a day and it
        loses \qty{1.6}{g} by breathing and \qty{0.3}{g} in faeces.
        Balance the budget with the urine of question 20.
  \item A camel concentrates to \qty{3000}{mOsm/L} and tolerates losing
        \qty{25}{\%} of its body water. For a \qty{500}{kg} camel with
        \qty{65}{\%} water, how much can it lose, and how many days does
        that cover at a loss of \qty{5}{L/day}?
  \item A marine bird eats \qty{200}{g} of fish and drinks \qty{100}{mL}
        of sea water a day, taking in \qty{150}{mmol} of NaCl. Its \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:comparative}{salt
        gland} secretes at \qty{800}{mmol/L}. How much brine does it
        produce, and why is the gland better than the kidney (which in
        birds concentrates to only \qty{600}{mOsm/L}) for this?
  \item A freshwater fish of \qty{100}{g} gains \qty{30}{\%} of its body
        mass in water a day through its gills. How much urine must it
        make, at what \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} relative to its blood, and what would
        happen to its salt without active uptake?
  \item Summarise: the GFR (question~1), the obligatory urine volume
        (question~14), and the net water from a litre of sea water after
        the day's solute is counted (question~15).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
