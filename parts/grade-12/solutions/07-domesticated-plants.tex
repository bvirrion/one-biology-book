\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g12:domesticated-plants} --- The Domesticated Plant}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:1}
\omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{Domestication}: the transformation of a wild species into one that
depends on and serves humans. \omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{Artificial selection}: humans keeping and
sowing the individuals with the traits they want, raising the
frequency of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} behind them.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:2}
Non-shattering ears (no dispersal); immediate germination (no waiting
out a bad year); loss of toxins (eaten by everything); a single large
ear on an unbranched plant (all eggs in one basket); simultaneous
ripening (one bad week destroys the whole crop).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:3}
The uniform, vigorous offspring of two homozygous inbred lines. Its
own seed segregates into a mixture of less vigorous plants, so the
farmer buys fresh hybrid seed each year.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:4}
The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is inserted into the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} that a soil bacterium naturally
transfers into plant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}; infected \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that take the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} are
regrown into whole plants.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:5}
From about \num{1.9} to \qty{7.2}{t/ha}: nearly four times.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:6}
Plants whose grain shattered lost it before or during the harvest;
mutants with a tough stalk kept theirs and were the ones gathered,
hence the ones sown. Harvesting itself selected the tough-stalk
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}, generation after generation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:7}
One species: they interbreed and give fertile offspring. Farmers
selected the exaggeration of a different organ in each lineage ---
leaves, terminal bud, flower buds --- so they differ in the regulation
of growth, not in their ability to cross.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:8}
F1 all $Aa$. F2: $AA$ $1/4$, $Aa$ $1/2$, $aa$ $1/4$. The F1 is uniform
because every plant receives $A$ from one parent and $a$ from the
other; in the F2 \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} and fertilisation deal the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} out at
random.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:9}
Among millions of borers a few carried a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} making them tolerate
the toxin; on a field where every plant makes it, only they survived
and bred --- selection. A refuge keeps many sensitive borers alive;
their matings with the rare resistant ones give offspring carrying one
resistance \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}, which (being \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive}) die on the toxin maize, so
the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is diluted each generation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:10}
The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} concerned control development --- the branching of the
plant, the growth of the kernel's casing, the number of rows; a change
in the regulation of one such \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} alters the form of a whole organ.
Few regulatory changes, large visible effects.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:11}
They hold the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} that selection removed from the crop: resistance
to diseases and pests, tolerance of drought, salt or cold. When a new
disease or climate arrives, breeders cross those \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} back into the
crop; once a wild relative is extinct its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} are gone.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:12}
Pollen from the rapeseed fertilises the mustard; hybrids carry the
tolerance \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}; back-crossed to mustard over some generations and
selected by the herbicide sprayed on the fields, a tolerant wild
mustard spreads. Processes: hybridisation and horizontal movement of
a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} between \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}, then \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:selection}{natural selection} by the herbicide.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:13}
Biological: the plant's \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} and its capacity to fill grain
are near their limits, and the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic diversity} of the elite
varieties, from which further gains must come, is narrow. Non-
biological: fertiliser and pesticide use is no longer increasing, or
is being reduced, and climate stresses have grown. A breeder might
bring \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} from wild relatives, or edit \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} of \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} or
drought tolerance.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:14}
Crossing: the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} must exist in a plant that can be crossed with
the crop; thousands of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} are reshuffled and must be sorted out by
selection over some ten generations. \omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:universal}{Transgenesis}: the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} may come
from any organism; one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is added and the rest of the genome is
untouched; a few years.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:domesticated-plants:15}
The arithmetic is identical: individuals carrying certain \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}
reproduce more, and the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}' frequencies rise. What differs is the
agent --- a human choice in place of the environment --- and the
outcome for the plant: \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:selection}{natural selection} keeps a species able to live
on its own, while \omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{domestication} selects traits that serve the selector
and leaves the plant dependent on it. \omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{Domestication} is \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:selection}{natural
selection} with a different selector, not its opposite.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g12:domesticated-plants:1}
\textbf{1.} Teosinte $12 \times 0.08 \approx \qty{1}{g}$; maize $600
\times 0.3 = \qty{180}{g}$: nearly two hundred times.

\textbf{2.} Many kernels on a rigid cob (no dispersal); naked kernels
(unprotected from animals and rot); one large ear on a single stalk
(no spreading of risk); kernels held in husks (cannot fall to the
ground at all).

\textbf{3.} The naked \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive}: a plant shows naked kernels
only when it carries two copies, which for a rare \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} happens in a
tiny minority of plants; carriers of one copy look ordinary.

\textbf{4.} $q^2 = 0.0001$: one plant in ten thousand.

\textbf{5.} All the seed comes from $nn$ plants, whose kernels received
$n$ from the mother; pollen came mostly from the ordinary field ($N$
with frequency 0.99). The seed is nearly all $Nn$, so the frequency of
$n$ jumps to about 0.5 --- one generation of severe selection.

\textbf{6.} $600/12 = 50$ over 9000 generations: a factor of $50^{1/9000}
\approx 1.0004$ per generation, a gain of 0.04\%. Tiny steps,
compounded over thousands of generations, become a large change ---
the arithmetic of selection.

\textbf{7.} With many \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} each adding a little, selection raises the
frequency of favourable \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} at all of them and keeps finding new
combinations for a long time; a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{single-gene} trait switches as soon as
the \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is made homozygous.

\textbf{8.} One species: fertile hybrids. \omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{Domestication} changed the
frequencies of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} at a few \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} and the plant's form; it did not
create a reproductive barrier.

\textbf{9.} A disease or pest that overcomes one plant overcomes them
all; the answer is the diversity kept in old varieties and wild
relatives, in seed banks and fields.

\textbf{10.} The farmer is the plant's dispersal, its selecting
environment and its only route to the next generation: maize is under
\omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{artificial selection} at every generation and cannot leave it.

\textbf{11.} $10/5.5 - 1 \approx 82\%$ above the better parent;
$1 - 8.2/10 = 18\%$ below the F1.

\textbf{12.} Each line is homozygous for some weak \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles};
in the F1 every such \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is masked by the other line's working one.
In the F2 a quarter of the plants are homozygous for the weak \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}
at each such \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, and the average falls.

\textbf{13.} Buying: $10 - 0.4 = \qty{9.6}{t/ha}$; saving: \qty{8.2}{t/ha}.
Buying wins by \qty{1.4}{t/ha} each year, which is why a company that
holds the inbred lines can sell the hybrid seed every season.

\textbf{14.} The company keeps the lines; with them, a farmer could
make the hybrid indefinitely and the company would sell once. The
lines are the company's capital; only their product is sold.

\textbf{15.} Every field of a hybrid variety is genetically identical,
and regions grow a few hybrids: diversity is narrowed. The farmer no
longer produces seed and depends on the supplier each year.

\textbf{16.} $q^2 = 10^{-6}$: one borer in a million survives; among
the survivors, all $rr$, the frequency of $r$ is 1.

\textbf{17.} The survivors breed among themselves; their offspring are
all $rr$; within a few generations the borers of the field are
resistant --- as bacteria in a patient on an incomplete course.

\textbf{18.} A resistant survivor ($rr$) mating with a refuge borer
($RR$, the vast majority) gives $Rr$ offspring, which die on the toxin
maize. So long as sensitive borers greatly outnumber resistant ones,
almost every resistant borer's children are heterozygous and are
removed, and $r$ stays rare.

\textbf{19.} Same: both aim to prevent the multiplication of the rare
resistant individuals. Different: the full course removes them by
killing every individual before resistance can be selected; the refuge
accepts survivors and dilutes them with sensitive mates.

\textbf{20.} Nearly two hundred times more grain per ear; about five
major \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}; a plant that depends on the farmer to disperse and sow
it.
\end{solution}
