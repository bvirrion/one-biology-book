\chapter{DNA Replication}\label{ch:g11:dna-replication}

Every day your bone marrow produces two hundred billion new red blood
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, your gut lining replaces itself every few days, and each of those
new \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} receives a complete copy of the two metres of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} that the
previous chapters described as the text of the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}. Copying
three billion letters, without a template to consult, in a few hours,
with fewer errors than a professional typist makes in a page: how is it
done? The answer was guessed from the structure of the molecule itself
in 1953, and proved by one of the most elegant experiments in biology
five years later.

\section{The problem of copying}

\begin{definition}[Replication]\label{def:g11:dna-replication:replication}
\emph{DNA replication}\index{DNA replication} is the process by which a
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} makes, from one double-stranded \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecule, two molecules
identical to it and to each other, before dividing. It takes place in
a defined period of the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} cycle, the \emph{S phase}
(\cref{ch:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis}), during which the amount of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} in
the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus} doubles.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The template principle]\label{prop:g11:dna-replication:template}
Because the two strands of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecule are complementary --- A
facing T, G facing C --- each strand carries all the information needed
to rebuild the other. Replication therefore proceeds by separating the
two strands and using each as a \emph{template} on which a new
complementary strand is assembled, \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide} by \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Three conceivable outcomes]\label{ex:g11:dna-replication:models}
Given the template principle, three ways of distributing old and new
strands were proposed in the 1950s:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{semi-conservative}: each daughter molecule keeps one old
        strand and gets one new one;
  \item \emph{conservative}: the original molecule is kept whole and
        an entirely new double strand is built beside it;
  \item \emph{dispersive}: old and new segments alternate along each
        strand of both daughters.
\end{itemize}
The three cannot be told apart by looking; they can by weighing.
\end{example}

\section{The Meselson--Stahl experiment}

\begin{proposition}[Replication is semi-conservative]\label{prop:g11:dna-replication:semiconservative}
Each daughter \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecule contains one strand of the parent molecule
and one newly synthesised strand.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Meselson and Stahl (1958) grew bacteria for many generations in a
medium whose only nitrogen was the heavy isotope $^{15}$N, so that all
their \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} was heavy. They then transferred the bacteria to ordinary
$^{14}$N medium and sampled the culture after each generation
(every twenty minutes). \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} extracted from each sample was spun for
many hours in a dense salt solution, where a molecule floats at the
level matching its own density, and photographed under ultraviolet
light: heavy and light \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} form bands a measurable distance apart.
Results: before transfer, one heavy band; after one generation, a
single band exactly half-way between heavy and light --- every
molecule hybrid; after two generations, two bands of equal intensity,
one hybrid and one light; after three, the light band three times the
hybrid one. The conservative model predicts a heavy and a light band
at generation one, never a hybrid; the dispersive model predicts a
single band that drifts lighter every generation, never splitting into
two. Only the semi-conservative model gives one hybrid band, then
hybrid plus light in the proportions observed.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Predicting the fourth generation]\label{ex:g11:dna-replication:fourth}
After $n$ generations in light medium, the two original heavy strands
are still there, one in each of two hybrid molecules; every other
molecule is fully light. Out of $2^n$ molecules, 2 are hybrid and
$2^n - 2$ light: at generation 4, 2 hybrid for 14 light --- a light
band seven times the hybrid one, and the hybrid band never disappears,
only fades. The two original strands are, in effect, immortal.
\end{example}

\section{The machinery}

\begin{proposition}[Replication forks]\label{prop:g11:dna-replication:forks}
Replication starts at defined sites, the \emph{origins}, where the two
strands are pulled apart into a bubble. At each edge of the bubble a
\emph{replication fork}\index{replication fork} advances: an \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme},
\emph{DNA polymerase}\index{DNA polymerase}, moves along each opened
strand and adds, one at a time, the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} complementary to it,
chaining them into the new strand. The two forks of a bubble travel in
opposite directions until they meet the forks of neighbouring bubbles;
the bacterial \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome}, a single circle, has one origin and two
forks; a human \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome} has thousands of origins working at once.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Speed and scale]\label{ex:g11:dna-replication:speed}
A bacterial fork adds about 1000 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} per second; a human fork,
about 50. The \emph{E.~coli} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome}, $\num{4.6e6}$ base pairs,
copied by two forks from one origin, takes $\num{4.6e6}/(2 \times
1000) \approx \qty{2300}{s}$, some 40 minutes --- the length of the
bacterium's \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} cycle in rich medium. A human \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, with $\num{6.4e9}$
base pairs to copy in an S phase of about 8 hours, needs its thousands
of origins: a single fork would take over two years.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Fidelity]\label{prop:g11:dna-replication:fidelity}
\omterm{prop:g11:dna-replication:forks}{DNA polymerase} pairs the wrong \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide} about once in $10^5$; it
checks each \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide} as it adds it and removes most mismatches at
once, and other \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzymes} correct most of the remaining ones just behind
the fork. The final error rate is about one per $10^9$ \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides}: a
human \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, copying $\num{6.4e9}$ \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides}, makes on average some
half-dozen errors per division. Those that escape are
\emph{mutations}, the subject of \cref{ch:g11:mutations}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Replication calculations]\label{met:g11:dna-replication:calc}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Time}: length to copy (base pairs) divided by the total
        speed of all the forks at work (forks $\times$ \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} per
        second per fork).
  \item \emph{Origins needed}: total length divided by what one origin
        (two forks) can copy in the time available.
  \item \emph{Errors}: \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} copied $\times$ error rate; remember
        that a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} copies both strands, i.e. twice the number of base
        pairs.
  \item \emph{Isotope experiments}: track the two original strands;
        after $n$ generations they sit in 2 hybrid molecules out of
        $2^n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Why the structure and the mechanism fit]\label{rem:g11:dna-replication:fit}
Watson and Crick ended their 1953 paper by noting that the pairing
"immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism". Meselson and Stahl
showed it was the actual mechanism; the enzymology that followed
showed how. It is one of the rare cases in biology where a molecule's
shape, seen once, dictated the process that uses it.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Weighing a Molecule to Watch It Copy}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the Meselson--Stahl experiment
reconstructed: the isotopes, the three models and their predictions,
the bands that decided, and the forks that copy a chromosome in
forty minutes}]
\label{pb:g11:dna-replication:1}
Ordinary nitrogen is $^{14}$N; the heavy isotope $^{15}$N is 7\%
heavier per atom. Nitrogen is about 16\% of the mass of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}. Bacteria
grown for fourteen generations in $^{15}$N medium are transferred to
$^{14}$N medium at time zero and divide every 20 minutes.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Heavy and light.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item By what fraction is fully heavy \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} denser than light \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}?
        (Assume density follows mass.) Why is such a small difference
        enough to separate bands?
  \item Why were the bacteria grown for fourteen generations in heavy
        medium before the transfer, rather than one or two?
  \item A hybrid molecule has one heavy and one light strand. Where
        does it float relative to the heavy and light bands?
  \item Explain why the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} must be extracted from a large number of
        bacteria for the bands to be visible.
  \item How could the experimenters be sure that the transfer to light
        medium did not itself alter the bacteria's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Three predictions.}
For each model, give the bands (position and relative amount)
expected after one and after two generations.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Semi-conservative model.
  \item Conservative model.
  \item Dispersive model.
  \item After one generation a single band appears half-way between
        heavy and light. Which model is eliminated at once, and which
        two remain?
  \item After two generations there are two bands, hybrid and light,
        of equal intensity. Which model survives, and why does the
        other fail?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Counting strands.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item After $n$ generations, how many \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecules descend from one
        original molecule, and how many of them contain an original
        heavy strand?
  \item Compute the ratio light : hybrid at generations 3, 4 and 6.
  \item At which generation does the hybrid band fall below 5\% of the
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}?
  \item The experimenters heated the hybrid \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} to separate its two
        strands and centrifuged the single strands. Predict the bands.
        Which model does this test?
  \item Explain how the same experiment, done in the reverse direction
        (light to heavy), would confirm the conclusion.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The forks.}
The bacterial \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome} is a circle of $\num{4.6e6}$ base pairs with
one origin; each fork adds 1000 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} per second.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the time needed to copy the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome} with its two
        forks.
  \item With one fork only, how long would it take? Why is one origin
        with two forks the minimum for a circle?
  \item A human \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} copies $\num{6.4e9}$ base pairs in 8 hours with
        forks of 50 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} per second. What is the minimum number of
        origins that must fire, if all start at once?
  \item At one error per $10^9$ \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides}, how many errors does the
        bacterium make per replication, and the human \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}? Why is the
        human figure not proportionally worse for the organism?
  \item State the result: what the two-generation bands proved, and
        the time the two forks need to copy the bacterial \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome}
        --- compared with the bacterium's generation time.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
