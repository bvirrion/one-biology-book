\chapter{Exercise and the Body's Energy Needs}\label{ch:g10:exercise-and-energy}

A cyclist on a laboratory bicycle pedals against a brake that measures
her power: \qty{100}{W}, then \qty{150}{W}, then \qty{200}{W}. A mask
over her face sends every breath to an analyser. The numbers on the
screen climb in step with the brake: the more mechanical work she
delivers, the more oxygen she consumes and the more carbon dioxide she
breathes out. Her muscles are running the respiration of
\cref{ch:g10:cell-metabolism} at full stretch, and the analyser is
reading the balance sheet of it. This chapter follows the energy of
exercise from the food that supplies it to the oxygen that releases it.

\section{Energy in, work out}

\begin{definition}[Energy expenditure]\label{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:expenditure}
The \emph{energy expenditure}\index{energy expenditure} of the body,
in joules, is the total energy its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} release from organic
molecules over a given time. Divided by the time, it is a
\emph{metabolic power}, in watts. At rest an adult spends about
\qty{80}{W} --- some \qty{7000}{kJ} a day --- to keep the heart
beating, the brain working, the temperature at \qty{37}{\celsius} and
the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} alive; this is the \emph{basal \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{metabolism}}. Every activity
adds to it.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Where the energy goes]\label{prop:g10:exercise-and-energy:efficiency}
A working muscle converts only about a quarter of the energy it
releases into mechanical work; the other three quarters leave as heat.
Delivering a mechanical power $P$ therefore costs the body a metabolic
power of about $4P$ above rest: a cyclist at \qty{100}{W} on the pedals
is spending some \qty{400}{W} in her muscles, and her body must get rid
of \qty{300}{W} of heat --- by sweating, mostly.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Daily budgets]\label{ex:g10:exercise-and-energy:budgets}
A student sitting in class, walking to school and sleeping spends
about \qty{9000}{kJ} a day; an hour of football adds some
\qty{2500}{kJ}; a day in the mountains with a pack, \qty{15000}{kJ} in
all. A cyclist on a mountain stage of a race spends \qty{25000}{kJ},
and cannot eat enough during the day to cover it. The energy comes
from food (\cref{ch:g10:chemistry-of-life}): \qty{17}{kJ} per gram of
\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate} or \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}, \qty{38}{kJ} per gram of \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipid}.
\end{example}

\section{Oxygen consumption measures energy expenditure}

\begin{proposition}[The oxygen equivalent]\label{prop:g10:exercise-and-energy:oxygen}
Since the energy of exercise is released by \omterm{prop:g10:cell-metabolism:respiration}{cellular respiration},
which consumes oxygen, the body's oxygen consumption measures its
\omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:expenditure}{energy expenditure}: each litre of oxygen consumed corresponds to
about \qty{20}{kJ} released, whatever the mixture of glucose and
\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids} burned. At rest a person consumes about \qty{0.25}{L} of
oxygen per minute; during exercise the consumption rises in
proportion to the power delivered, up to a personal ceiling.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The respiration equation of \cref{ch:g10:cell-metabolism} gives
\qty{2870}{kJ} for \qty{6}{mol} of oxygen, i.e. \qty{478}{kJ} per mole,
and a mole of gas occupies about \qty{24}{L}: \qty{20}{kJ} per litre.
For \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids} the figure is \qty{19.6}{kJ/L}, for \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrates}
\qty{21.1}{kJ/L}, so the mixture hardly matters. Measurements in
sealed chambers, where the heat released by a subject is collected
directly, agree with the oxygen figure to within a few per cent.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Maximal oxygen consumption]\label{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:vo2max}
The \emph{maximal oxygen consumption}\index{maximal oxygen consumption}
, written $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max, is the highest rate
at which a person's body can take up and use oxygen, in litres per
minute --- or, to compare people of different sizes, in millilitres
per minute per kilogram of body mass. It sets the highest power that
can be sustained by respiration alone. Typical values: \qty{35}{mL/min}
per kilogram in a sedentary adult, \num{50} in a trained student,
\num{80} or more in a champion endurance athlete.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Reading the curve]\label{ex:g10:exercise-and-energy:reading}
In the figure, \qty{100}{W} of pedalling costs \qty{1.5}{L/min} of
oxygen: $1.5 \times 20 = \qty{30}{kJ}$ per minute, i.e. \qty{500}{W} of
metabolic power --- the \qty{100}{W} of work plus \qty{400}{W} of heat
and resting needs, in line with
\cref{prop:g10:exercise-and-energy:efficiency}. The ceiling of
\qty{3.3}{L/min} for a \qty{66}{kg} subject is \qty{50}{mL/min} per
kilogram.
\end{example}

\section{The fuels of the muscle}

\begin{proposition}[Three sources of ATP]\label{prop:g10:exercise-and-energy:fuels}
Muscle contraction is paid for in ATP, and the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} keeps only a few
seconds' worth. It is renewed by three routes, each with its own
speed and capacity:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a small store of a phosphate compound in the muscle, which
        re-forms ATP instantly but lasts about ten seconds --- the
        sprint;
  \item lactic \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation} of glucose in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cytoplasm}, fast but
        wasteful, sustaining an all-out effort for one to two minutes
        and producing lactic acid;
  \item \omterm{prop:g10:cell-metabolism:respiration}{cellular respiration} in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria}, slower to reach full
        rate but almost unlimited in duration, burning glucose from
        the blood, \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} from the muscle, and fatty acids from the
        body's fat.
\end{itemize}
The share of each depends on the intensity and duration of the
exercise.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{definition}[Glycogen]\label{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}
\emph{Glycogen}\index{glycogen} is the animal form of stored
\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate}: a branched chain of glucose units, held as granules in
the muscle \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} (about \qty{400}{g} in an adult) and in the liver
(about \qty{100}{g}). Muscle glycogen fuels the muscle that stores it;
liver glycogen is broken down into glucose released into the blood,
keeping the blood glucose near \qty{1}{g/L} for the brain and the
other tissues. Fat, stored in adipose \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, is the far larger reserve
(\cref{ch:g10:chemistry-of-life}), but it can be burned only slowly and
only with oxygen.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The marathon wall]\label{ex:g10:exercise-and-energy:wall}
Running consumes about \qty{4}{kJ} per kilogram of body mass per
kilometre; a \qty{70}{kg} runner spends \qty{280}{kJ/km}, and a
marathon costs \qty{11800}{kJ}. The \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} stores hold about
\qty{8500}{kJ}. If the runner burns \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} too fast, the stores run
out around the thirtieth kilometre; the muscles must then rely on fat,
which supplies energy at barely half the rate, and the pace collapses
--- "hitting the wall". Trained runners burn a larger share of fat
from the start, and eat \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate} during the race.
\end{example}

\section{Measuring and reasoning}

\begin{method}[An energy calculation for exercise]\label{met:g10:exercise-and-energy:calc}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item From oxygen: energy (\unit{kJ}) $=$ oxygen consumed (\unit{L})
        $\times 20$. Subtract the resting consumption if the question
        asks for the cost of the exercise alone.
  \item From mechanical power: metabolic power $\approx 4 P$; energy
        $=$ power $\times$ time (\qty{1}{W} for \qty{1}{s} is
        \qty{1}{J}; \qty{1}{kWh} $= \qty{3600}{kJ}$).
  \item From the fuel: mass burned $=$ energy $/$ energy value
        (\qty{17}{kJ/g} \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate}, \qty{38}{kJ/g} \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipid}); \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} is
        stored with three times its mass of water.
  \item Check orders of magnitude: a whole day is about
        \qty{10000}{kJ}; a hard hour, \qtyrange{2000}{3000}{kJ}; a
        marathon, \qty{12000}{kJ}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[An hour of cycling]\label{ex:g10:exercise-and-energy:hour}
One hour at \qty{150}{W} on the pedals: metabolic power about
\qty{600}{W}, energy $600 \times 3600 = \qty{2.16e6}{J} \approx
\qty{2200}{kJ}$; oxygen $2200/20 = \qty{110}{L}$, i.e. \qty{1.8}{L/min}
--- consistent with the curve. If half comes from \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate}:
$1100/17 \approx \qty{65}{g}$ of glucose or \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen}, and
$1100/38 \approx \qty{29}{g}$ of fat.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Why the oxygen must arrive]\label{rem:g10:exercise-and-energy:delivery}
Everything above assumes that oxygen reaches the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} as fast
as they can use it. That is the job of the lungs, the heart and the
blood, whose response to exercise --- faster breathing, faster and
stronger heartbeat, blood redirected to the muscles --- is the subject
of \cref{ch:g10:heart-lungs-effort}. $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max is
mostly a limit of delivery, not of the muscles' appetite.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Where the Wall Stands}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a marathon's energy budget: the
oxygen the runner breathes, the glycogen she carries, the kilometre
at which it runs out, and what training changes}]
\label{pb:g10:exercise-and-energy:1}
Nadia, \qty{60}{kg}, runs a marathon (\qty{42.2}{km}) in
\qty{3}{h}\,\qty{30}{min}. Running costs \qty{4.0}{kJ} per kilogram
per kilometre. Her muscles hold \qty{300}{g} of \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} and her liver
\qty{80}{g}; \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} and glucose give \qty{17}{kJ/g}, fat
\qty{38}{kJ/g}; one litre of oxygen releases \qty{20}{kJ}. Her
$\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max is \qty{52}{mL/min} per kilogram.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The cost of the race.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the energy cost of the marathon for Nadia.
  \item Compute her average metabolic power during the race, in
        watts.
  \item Compute the volume of oxygen she consumes over the race, then
        her average oxygen consumption in litres per minute.
  \item Express that consumption in millilitres per minute per
        kilogram, and as a percentage of her
        $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max.
  \item Her resting consumption is \qty{0.2}{L/min}. What fraction of
        her race-day consumption does the running itself account for?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The stores.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the energy held in her \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} stores.
  \item If she burned \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} only, at which kilometre would the
        stores run out?
  \item In reality she burns a mixture: 80\% \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate}, 20\% fat
        at her race pace. At which kilometre does the \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} now
        run out?
  \item What mass of fat does she burn in the race? Compare with the
        \qty{9}{kg} of fat a \qty{60}{kg} woman typically carries.
  \item Explain, from \cref{prop:g10:exercise-and-energy:fuels}, why
        she cannot simply burn fat alone once the \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} is gone and
        keep the same pace.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Beating the wall.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item During the race she drinks a sugary solution supplying
        \qty{60}{g} of \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate} per hour. How much energy does
        that add over the race, and how many kilometres of
        \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate} use does it cover?
  \item Redo question 8 with the drink. Does she now finish before
        the \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} runs out?
  \item Three days before the race she "loads" \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate}, raising
        her muscle \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} to \qty{500}{g}. Each gram is stored with
        \qty{3}{g} of water. By how much does her mass rise, and what
        is the cost of that in energy per kilometre?
  \item Training shifts her race-pace mixture to 55\% \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate},
        45\% fat. With loading and the drink, at which kilometre
        would the \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} run out? Comment.
  \item Why does the brain, which uses glucose only, make \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen}
        depletion feel like exhaustion even when the muscles still
        have fat to burn?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The numbers of a champion.}
An elite runner of \qty{55}{kg} runs the marathon in \qty{2}{h}\,
\qty{05}{min}, with a running cost of \qty{3.6}{kJ} per kilogram per
kilometre and a $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max of \qty{80}{mL/min} per
kilogram.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the champion's energy cost for the race and his
        average oxygen consumption in litres per minute.
  \item What percentage of his $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max does he
        sustain? Compare with Nadia's figure.
  \item Two things distinguish the champion: a higher ceiling and a
        lower cost per kilometre. Which of the two is
        "economy"? Compute by how much the difference in cost alone
        changes the energy of the race for a \qty{55}{kg} runner.
  \item His muscles hold \qty{500}{g} of \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} after loading. At
        70\% \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate} use, does he reach the finish on it without
        drinking?
  \item State the result: for Nadia, the kilometre where the wall
        stands without preparation, and the three measures that move
        it beyond the finish line.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
