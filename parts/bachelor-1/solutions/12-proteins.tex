\begin{solution}{exo:b1:proteins:2}
Primary: the sequence, \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{peptide bonds}. Secondary: local backbone
folding (helix, sheet), backbone \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds}. Tertiary: the whole
chain's fold; \omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:properties}{hydrophobic effect}, \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds}, salt bridges, \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:bonds}{van der
Waals} contacts, \omterm{def:b1:proteins:tertiary}{disulfide bridges}. Quaternary: the assembly of
subunits, by the same non-covalent forces.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:proteins:5}
Groups: N-terminal amino (9.5), Lys \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chain} (10.5), Asp \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chain}
(3.9), C-terminal carboxyl (2). pH 7: $+1 + 1 - 1 - 1 = 0$. pH 2:
$+1 + 1 + 0 - 0.5 = +1.5$ (the terminal carboxyl half ionised). pH
12: $0 + 0 - 1 - 1 = -2$ (Lys mostly deprotonated). Net zero between
the p$K_a$ of Asp (3.9) and of the amino group (9.5), where the charge
is $0$ throughout: p$I \approx 6.7$ (mean of 3.9 and 9.5).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:proteins:6}
Proline's \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chain} is bonded back to its own nitrogen: the nitrogen
carries no hydrogen to donate the helix's \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bond}, and $\phi$ is
locked at about $-60^\circ$, which is tolerated only at the first turn
of a helix. Glycine, with a hydrogen for \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chain}, suffers no steric
clash and can adopt $\phi$, $\psi$ values forbidden to every other
residue, including those of tight turns.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:proteins:7}
Pairings of eight cysteines: $7\times 5\times 3\times 1 = 105$; random
re-oxidation gives the native set about \qty{1}{\%} of the time. The
recovery of nearly full activity showed that the chain, not chance,
chose the pairing: the sequence encodes the fold and the disulfides
merely lock it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:proteins:9}
A tetramer of four identical \qty{50}{kDa} subunits. Run the SDS gel
without reducing agent: if bands appear at 100, 150 or \qty{200}{kDa},
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:tertiary}{disulfide bridges} link the subunits; if the \qty{50}{kDa} band alone

\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:proteins:10}
Glutamate is charged and hydrated at the surface; valine is
hydrophobic and creates a sticky patch that water excludes. In the
deoxygenated T state a complementary hydrophobic pocket is exposed on
a neighbouring molecule, so molecules stick end to end into fibres
that deform the red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}. In the lungs the R state hides the pocket
and the fibres dissolve; in the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}, where haemoglobin is
deoxygenated, they form --- so the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} sickle and block the small
vessels there.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:proteins:11}
At \qty{37}{\celsius}, $RT = \qty{2.58}{kJ/mol}$: $K = e^{-40/2.58} =
e^{-15.5} = \num{1.8e-7}$: one molecule in five million unfolded. At
\qty{45}{\celsius} with \qty{10}{kJ/mol}, $RT = 2.64$: $K = e^{-3.8} =
0.022$: \qty{2}{\%} unfolded, and rising steeply. Marginal stability
lets \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} change shape when they work (\omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{allostery}, catalysis), be
unfolded for transport across membranes, and be degraded and replaced
quickly when damaged or no longer needed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:proteins:12}
Anfinsen showed that the sequence suffices to specify the fold, and
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{allostery} shows that the shape --- and its changes --- is the function.
But the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} intervenes at every step: \omterm{prop:b1:proteins:denaturation}{chaperones} rescue chains that
would aggregate before folding, because the crowded \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol} is not a
dilute test tube; a single substitution (sickle \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}) gives a
correctly folded \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} whose surface is wrong, so "shape" must
include the surface chemistry, not the backbone alone; and many
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} need modifications, partners or ligands added after folding
before they work. The sentence states the principle; the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} supplies
the conditions.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b1:proteins:1}
\textbf{1.} $150\times 1.34 = \qty{201}{mL}$ of $\mathrm{O_2}$ per litre.
\textbf{2.} $e^{3.51} = 33.4$; $e^{4.67} = 106.7$; $e^{7.25} = 1408$.
\textbf{3.} Lungs: $1408/(33.4 + 1408) = 0.977$. \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{Tissues}:
$106.7/(33.4 + 106.7) = 0.762$.
\textbf{4.} $0.977 - 0.762 = 0.215$: $0.215\times 201 = \qty{43}{mL}$
per litre.
\textbf{5.} $250/43 = \qty{5.8}{L/min}$ --- close to the resting
cardiac output.
\textbf{6.} Myoglobin: lungs $13.3/13.67 = 0.973$; \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}
$5.3/5.67 = 0.935$; delivers $0.038\times 201 = \qty{7.7}{mL}$ per
litre, six times less: a hyperbolic carrier that loads well cannot
unload at \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} pressures.
\textbf{7.} \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{Cooperativity} places the steep part of the curve between
the two working pressures, so that a small drop in pressure releases a
large fraction of the load.
\textbf{8.} $e^{2.78} = 16.1$; $Y = 16.1/(33.4 + 16.1) = 0.325$.
\textbf{9.} $e^{4.21} = 67.4$; $Y = 16.1/(67.4 + 16.1) = 0.193$.
\textbf{10.} Without Bohr: $(0.977 - 0.325)\times 201 = \qty{131}{mL}$;
with: $(0.977 - 0.193)\times 201 = \qty{158}{mL}$ per litre; gain
\qty{27}{mL}, \qty{20}{\%}.
\textbf{11.} $2.7/3.07 = 0.88$ saturated; at \qty{0.5}{kPa},
$0.5/0.87 = 0.57$: it releases a third of its store into the
\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:mitochondrion}{mitochondria} during the contraction, and reloads between contractions.
\textbf{12.} Protons bind at sites (histidines, the N-termini) away
from the haems, and by binding stabilise the T state, lowering the
affinity at the haems: a ligand at one site changing the affinity at
another --- \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{allostery}.
\textbf{13.} $e^{5.45} = 233$; lungs $233/(33.4 + 233) = 0.875$;
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} $0.762$; delivery $0.113\times 201 = \qty{23}{mL}$: about half
of sea level.
\textbf{14.} $e^{3.88} = 48.4$: lungs $233/(48.4 + 233) = 0.828$;
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} $106.7/(48.4 + 106.7) = 0.688$; delivery $0.140\times 201 =
\qty{28}{mL}$.
\textbf{15.} The loading falls a little (0.875 to 0.828) but the
unloading falls more (0.762 to 0.688), so the difference grows: at
\qty{7}{kPa} the lungs are still on the flat top of the curve while the
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} are on its steep part.
\textbf{16.} Capacity $180\times 1.34 = \qty{241}{mL}$; $0.140\times
241 = \qty{34}{mL}$ per litre --- three quarters of sea level restored.
\textbf{17.} $6^{2.8} = e^{2.8\times 1.79} = e^{5.02} = 151$: lungs
$233/384 = 0.607$, \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} $106.7/258 = 0.414$: delivery $0.193$,
better still on paper --- but the arterial blood would be only
\qty{61}{\%} saturated, and any further fall of lung pressure or any
demand for a higher \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} pressure would leave the brain short; the
real adjustment stops near \qty{4}{kPa}.
\textbf{18.} $48.4/(33.4 + 48.4) = 0.59$.
\textbf{19.} $e^{2.68} = 14.6$; $48.4/(14.6 + 48.4) = 0.77$.
\textbf{20.} At the same pressure the fetal \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} binds more: as the
mother's blood unloads toward \qty{59}{\%} and the fetal blood loads
toward \qty{77}{\%}, oxygen flows down the pressure gradient that the
difference of affinities maintains across the placental membrane.
\textbf{21.} BPG binds the T state and lowers affinity; the $\gamma$
chains bind it weakly, so fetal haemoglobin sits nearer its intrinsic
high affinity: lower $P_{50}$.
\textbf{22.} Lungs: $1408/(14.6 + 1408) = 0.990$; \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} $106.7/(14.6
+ 106.7) = 0.880$; delivery $0.110\times 201 = \qty{22}{mL}$, half the
adult's: good for taking oxygen from the mother, poor for delivering
it once the lungs work, hence the switch.
\textbf{23.} $5^{2.8} = e^{2.8\times 1.609} = e^{4.51} = 90.6$: lungs
$1408/1499 = 0.94$, \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} $106.7/197 = 0.54$: delivery $0.40$, nearly
double the normal --- the person is mildly cyanotic at the lips but
tolerates it well; the price is a smaller reserve at altitude.
\textbf{24.} $Y = P/(3.5 + P)$: lungs $0.79$, \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} $0.60$: delivery
$0.19\times 201 = \qty{38}{mL}$ --- and in the sprinting muscle at
\qty{2.7}{kPa}, $0.44$: delivery $0.35\times 201 = \qty{70}{mL}$ against
\qty{158}{mL}. Without \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{cooperativity} the carrier loads badly and
unloads badly.
\textbf{25.} \qty{43}{mL} of $\mathrm{O_2}$ per litre at rest,
\qty{158}{mL} per litre in a sprinting muscle; the difference comes
from \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{cooperativity} (the sigmoid curve) and from the Bohr effect (the
shift of $P_{50}$ in acid).
\end{solution}
