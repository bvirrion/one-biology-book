\chapter{Plant Water and Mineral Nutrition}\label{ch:b1:plant-water-minerals}

A maize plant in July draws a litre of water a day out of soil that
feels dry to the hand, and with it the thirty milligrams of nitrogen,
as nitrate, that it needs to build a day's growth. It has no pump. The
water enters because the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} is drier than the soil, in the precise
sense of \cref{ch:b1:membranes-transport}; the nitrate enters because
the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} spend \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} to make it; and both must pass a single layer
of \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} whose walls are sealed with \omterm{def:b1:lipids:wax}{wax} before they reach the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}.
This chapter describes the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} as an absorbing \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ}, the \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water
potentials} that drive water from soil to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}, the mineral elements a
plant needs and how it takes them up, the special case of nitrogen,
and the fungi and bacteria that most \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} employ to do part of the
work.

\section{The root as an absorbing organ}

\begin{definition}[Apoplast, symplast, endodermis]\label{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:pathways}
\index{apoplast}\index{symplast}\index{Casparian strip}
Water and solutes cross the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} cortex by two routes: the
\emph{apoplast}, the continuous system of \omterm{prop:b1:carbohydrates:wall}{cell walls} and intercellular
spaces, through which they move without crossing any membrane; and the
\emph{symplast}, the continuous \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cytoplasm} of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} connected by
\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{plasmodesmata} (\cref{ch:b1:eukaryotic-cell}), entered by crossing one
plasma membrane. At the inner boundary of the cortex the
\emph{\omterm{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:stemroot}{endodermis}} (\cref{ch:b1:flowering-plant-organization}) blocks the
apoplast: a band of suberin and lignin, the \emph{Casparian strip},
impregnates its radial walls, so that everything entering the
vascular cylinder must pass through an endodermal \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s membrane. The
membrane's transporters thus decide what reaches the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}, and
prevent what has been loaded into the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} from leaking back.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Root pressure]\label{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:rootpressure}
\index{root pressure}\index{guttation}
The \omterm{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:stemroot}{endodermis} and the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} within it pump ions into the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}, which
lowers the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}'s \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} and draws water in osmotically from
the cortex; when the shoot transpires little (at night, in humid air),
the water accumulates and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} sap is pushed upward under a
positive \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:rootpressure}{root pressure}} of a few tenths of a megapascal ---
enough to force droplets out of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} tips of small plants
(\emph{guttation}) and to refill air-blocked vessels, but not enough
to lift sap to the top of a tree. By day the pull of transpiration
(\cref{ch:b1:plant-transport}) takes over and the \omterm{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:rootpressure}{root pressure}
vanishes.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
A stem cut near the ground exudes sap for hours from its stump, and
a manometer attached to the stump registers a pressure of
\qtyrange{0.1}{0.3}{MPa}; the exudate is richer in ions than the soil
solution, and its flow stops when the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} are cooled or poisoned
with an \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:inhibitors}{inhibitor} of respiration: the flow is driven by active ion
loading, not by any pumping of water.
\end{proof}

\section{Water: from soil to xylem}

\begin{proposition}[The water-potential ladder]\label{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:ladder}
\index{water potential}
Water moves from soil to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} because the \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential}
(\cref{ch:b1:membranes-transport}) falls at each step:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\hline
compartment & $\Psi$ (\unit{MPa}) \\
\hline
moist soil & $-0.03$ to $-0.3$ \\
soil at the wilting point & $-1.5$ \\
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} cortex \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} & $-0.3$ to $-0.6$ \\
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} & $-0.5$ to $-0.8$ \\
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} & $-1.0$ to $-2.0$ \\
air at \qty{50}{\%} relative humidity & $-95$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The soil's potential is set mostly by the tension with which its
pores hold water (capillarity); the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}' by their solutes and their
\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor}; the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}'s by the tension of the transpiring column; the
air's by its humidity, $\Psi = (RT/V_w)\ln(\text{RH})$, which is
enormous even in humid air. The steepest step by far is the last, from
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} to air: that is where nearly all the driving force is spent, and
why plants control it with \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Reading a water-potential profile]\label{met:b1:plant-water-minerals:profile}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item List the compartments in order and their $\Psi$; water flows
        only from higher to lower, so the sequence must decrease along
        the path or the flow reverses.
  \item Split each \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s $\Psi$ into $\Psi_s$ (solutes, negative) and
        $\Psi_p$ (\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor}, positive); a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} at $\Psi = -0.4$ with
        $\Psi_s = -0.9$ has $\Psi_p = +0.5$. \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{Xylem} water has
        $\Psi_s \approx 0$ and negative $\Psi_p$: it is under tension.
  \item The flow across a step is the difference of $\Psi$ times the
        hydraulic conductance of the barrier; the \omterm{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:stemroot}{endodermis} and the
        \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} are the two steps of low conductance where the plant
        regulates.
  \item Dry the soil and its $\Psi$ falls; when it reaches the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s,
        flow stops and the plant wilts; when the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}' $\Psi_p$
        reaches zero the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} hangs. Recovery needs the soil, not the
        plant, to change.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[A litre a day]\label{ex:b1:plant-water-minerals:litre}
A maize plant's fine \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} offer some \qty{0.2}{m^2} of surface with a
hydraulic conductance of about \qty{2e-7}{m.s^{-1}.MPa^{-1}}; between
soil at \qty{-0.2}{MPa} and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} at \qty{-0.6}{MPa} the flux is
$2\times 10^{-7}\times 0.4\times 0.2 = \qty{1.6e-8}{m^3/s}$ --- a
litre and a half a day, all of it lost from the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}. The \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} does
no work to move it: the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s transpiration sets the tension, and the
soil supplies the water as long as its potential stays above the
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}'s.
\end{example}

\section{Mineral nutrition}

\begin{definition}[Essential elements]\label{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:elements}
\index{essential element}\index{macronutrient}\index{micronutrient}
An element is \emph{essential} to a plant if the plant cannot
complete its life cycle without it and no other element can replace
it. Seventeen are: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen from air and water; the
\emph{macronutrients} taken from the soil in grams per kilogram of
dry mass --- nitrogen (as nitrate or ammonium), phosphorus (phosphate),
potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulfur (sulfate); and the
\emph{micronutrients} needed in milligrams or less --- iron, manganese,
zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine, nickel. Nitrogen builds
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} and nucleic acids; phosphorus, \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} and membranes;
potassium is the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s main cation and the osmotic agent of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata};
magnesium sits in \omterm{def:b1:photosynthesis:pigments}{chlorophyll}; iron in the cytochromes and
ferredoxin; molybdenum in \omterm{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:nitrate}{nitrate reductase} and nitrogenase.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[How the list was made]\label{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:hydroponics}
The \omterm{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:elements}{essential elements} were identified by growing plants in water
containing known salts only.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Sachs and Knop (1860s) grew plants to maturity in solutions of a few
mineral salts, with no soil at all, proving that soil supplies
nothing but minerals and support; leaving out one salt at a time gave
a characteristic sickness for each element, and adding it back cured
it. The \omterm{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:elements}{micronutrients} were found later, as chemists learned to
purify the salts: a plant deprived of molybdenum, needed at one part
in ten million, fails to reduce nitrate. The technique, hydroponics,
now feeds greenhouses.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Reading a deficiency]\label{met:b1:plant-water-minerals:deficiency}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Ask where the symptom appears. Elements the plant can move in
        the phloem (N, P, K, Mg) are withdrawn from old \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} to feed
        young ones: the old \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} show the deficiency first. Elements
        it cannot move (Ca, Fe, B) are stranded where they were laid
        down: the young \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} and growing tips show it.
  \item Ask what the element does: no nitrogen, little \omterm{def:b1:photosynthesis:pigments}{chlorophyll} and
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} --- pale, stunted; no magnesium or iron, \omterm{def:b1:photosynthesis:pigments}{chlorophyll}
        fails --- yellow \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} with green veins; no potassium, the
        \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} margins scorch; no calcium, the growing tips die.
  \item Confirm by supplying the suspected element alone and watching
        the new growth.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[Active uptake of ions]\label{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:uptake}
\index{proton pump}
The soil solution holds ions at micromoles to a few millimoles per
litre; the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} hold potassium at \qty{100}{mmol/L}, phosphate
at \qty{10}{}, nitrate at several. Uptake is therefore mostly uphill
and paid for with \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP}, but indirectly: a \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:uptake}{proton pump}} in the
plasma membrane (\cref{ch:b1:membranes-transport}) exports
$\mathrm{H^+}$, making the outside acid and the inside negative by
\qtyrange{-120}{-200}{mV}; cations such as $\mathrm{K^+}$ then enter
through \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels} down the electrical gradient, and anions
($\mathrm{NO_3^-}$, $\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-}$, $\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$) by
\omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporters} that ride protons back in. Transporters are selective:
potassium is taken up a hundred times more readily than sodium of the
same charge and nearly the same size, and the plant holds sodium out
even in salty soil. \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{Roots} use a third of the plant's respiration on
this transport.
\end{proposition}

\section{Nitrogen}

\begin{proposition}[Nitrate to amino acid]\label{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:nitrate}
\index{nitrate reductase}\index{nitrogen assimilation}
Most plants take nitrogen as nitrate. In the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol} \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:nitrate}{nitrate
reductase}}, a molybdenum \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme}, reduces it to nitrite with electrons
from NADH; in the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plastid}{plastid} \emph{nitrite reductase} reduces nitrite to
ammonium with electrons from ferredoxin; and ammonium is fixed at
once into glutamine and \omterm{def:b1:biosyntheses-integration:nitrogen}{glutamate} (\cref{ch:b1:biosyntheses-integration})
--- ammonium itself is toxic and never accumulates. The whole reduction
costs eight electrons per nitrogen, a tenth of a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s photosynthetic
electron flow on a nitrate-rich soil, and a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} does much of it in
the light, straight from the chloroplast's ferredoxin. Ammonium, where
the soil offers it, is taken up directly and skips the cost.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[Nitrogen fixation and nodules]\label{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:nodules}
\index{nitrogen fixation}\index{root nodule}\index{Rhizobium}
No eukaryote can use the $\mathrm{N_2}$ of the air. Some bacteria can:
\emph{nitrogenase} breaks the triple bond of $\mathrm{N_2}$ and
reduces it to two ammonia, at a cost of sixteen \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} and eight
electrons per molecule, and only where oxygen is excluded. Legumes
(peas, beans, clover) house such bacteria (\emph{Rhizobium}) in
\emph{\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} nodules}: the plant builds a chamber, feeds the bacteria
sugar, and keeps the oxygen low with a red \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}, leghaemoglobin,
that delivers it to the bacteria's respiration at a concentration too
low to harm the \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme}; the bacteria give back ammonium, which the
plant turns into \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids}. A field of clover fixes a hundred
kilograms of nitrogen per hectare a year, for which the plant pays a
tenth of its photosynthesis. The nitrogen cycle as a whole belongs to
the Year 2 volume.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The price of nitrogen]\label{ex:b1:plant-water-minerals:priceN}
A maize plant needs about \qty{30}{mg} of nitrogen a day; from
nitrate at \qty{2}{mmol/L} it gets that in the litre of water it
transpires, and spends a few hundred millimoles of electrons reducing
it. A clover plant fixing the same amount pays \qty{16}{} \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} per
$\mathrm{N_2}$ plus the reductant --- roughly \qty{6}{g} of sugar per
gram of nitrogen, some \qty{0.2}{g} a day, a tenth of its production
--- and can grow on soil with no nitrate at all. Farmers have rotated
legumes with cereals for two thousand years to move that nitrogen from
one field to the next.
\end{example}

\section{Partners in the soil}

\begin{definition}[Mycorrhiza]\label{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:mycorrhiza}
\index{mycorrhiza}
A \emph{mycorrhiza} is an association between a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} and a fungus,
found in nine plants out of ten. In the commonest form the fungal
hyphae grow into the cortex \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} and branch inside them into
\emph{arbuscules}, the \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface}, while outside the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} a
network of hyphae a few micrometres across explores the soil for
metres. The fungus delivers phosphate and other poorly mobile ions,
and water, that the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}'s own hairs could not reach; the plant
delivers sugar, a tenth or more of its photosynthesis. Trees of
temperate forests carry a second form, in which the fungus sheathes
the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} tips and threads between the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}; its fruiting bodies are
the mushrooms of the forest floor.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Seedlings grown in sterilised soil grow poorly and take up little
phosphate; inoculated with spores of the fungus, they grow several
times larger. Radioactive phosphate placed in soil beyond the reach of
the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} appears in the plant only if hyphae connect the two, and
labelled carbon fed to the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} appears in the hyphae within a day.
Cutting the hyphae with a fine mesh that lets solutes but not fungi
pass abolishes the gain.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Why phosphate needs a fungus]\label{ex:b1:plant-water-minerals:phosphate}
Phosphate binds to soil particles and diffuses a millimetre in a day;
a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hair} takes up all the phosphate within reach in hours and then
waits. Hyphae a tenth as thick as a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hair} reach ten times farther
per gram of \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue}, and a metre of hypha costs the plant a thousandth
of what a metre of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} does. For nitrate, which moves freely with the
soil water, the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}'s own hairs suffice, and plants on nitrate-rich
soils reduce their fungus; for phosphate, the fungus is the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}'s
extension.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Water-Potential Ladder of a Maize Root}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- water and nitrate from a July soil
into a maize plant: potentials at each step, the flux through the
roots, the nitrogen it carries and the cost of reducing it, ending on
the daily water flux into the root}]
\label{pb:b1:plant-water-minerals:1}
A maize plant in July: \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{fine-root} surface \qty{0.2}{m^2}, hydraulic
conductance of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} \qty{2e-7}{m.s^{-1}.MPa^{-1}}; \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} area
\qty{0.5}{m^2}. Soil \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} \qty{-0.2}{MPa}; \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} cortex \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}
with $\Psi_s = \qty{-0.9}{MPa}$ and $\Psi_p = \qty{0.5}{MPa}$; \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} \qty{-0.6}{MPa}; \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} \qty{-1.3}{MPa}; air at \qty{50}{\%}
relative humidity ($RT/V_w = \qty{137}{MPa}$). Soil nitrate
\qty{2}{mmol/L}; the plant needs \qty{30}{mg} of nitrogen a day.
Nitrate reduction to ammonium uses 8 electrons per nitrogen;
photosynthesis moves 4 electrons per $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} fixes
\qty{4}{\micro mol} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ per square metre per second for
\qty{12}{h}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The ladder.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} cortex \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
  \item Write the sequence soil, cortex, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}, and verify that
        water flows inward and upward at each step.
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} of the air.
  \item Compute the drop from soil to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} and the drop from \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} to
        air; what fraction of the total is the last step?
  \item The soil dries to \qty{-0.9}{MPa}. What happens to the flow
        into the cortex \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, and to their \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor}?
  \item The soil dries to \qty{-1.5}{MPa}. What happens to the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves},
        and why does watering restore them?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The flux.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the water flux into the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} (conductance $\times$
        surface $\times$ the difference between soil and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}).
  \item Convert it to litres per day.
  \item All of it is lost by transpiration. Compute the transpiration
        per square metre of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} per hour, in grams.
  \item Express the transpiration per square metre of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} per second
        in millimoles of water (\qty{18}{g/mol}).
  \item The \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} close at midday and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s $\Psi$ rises to
        \qty{-0.9}{MPa} while the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}'s rises to \qty{-0.4}{MPa}.
        Recompute the flux. What has the plant done, and why?
  \item Why does the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} take up water without spending energy,
        while it must spend energy to take up nitrate?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Nitrogen.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the nitrate carried into the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} by the water flux
        of question 8, in millimoles and in milligrams of nitrogen.
  \item Does it cover the plant's need? What happens to the balance?
  \item The \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} accumulates nitrate to \qty{5}{mmol/L} in its \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
        Compute the \omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:gibbs}{free energy} of moving one mole from
        \qty{2}{mmol/L} outside to \qty{5}{mmol/L} inside against a
        \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:potential}{membrane potential} of \qty{-150}{mV} ($RT = \qty{2.48}{kJ/mol}$;
        an anion entering a negative \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}).
  \item The uptake is a symport with two protons. Compute the \omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:gibbs}{free
        energy} two protons release entering a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} at \qty{-150}{mV}
        from pH 5.5 to pH 7.5, and check it suffices.
  \item Compute the electrons needed per day to reduce the
        \qty{30}{mg} of nitrogen to ammonium.
  \item Compute the electrons the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} moves through its \omterm{def:b1:photosynthesis:zscheme}{photosystems}
        per day, and the fraction diverted to nitrate.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Alternatives.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item If the soil offered ammonium instead of nitrate, what would
        the plant save, and what problem would ammonium uptake create
        for its pH balance?
  \item A clover fixes the same \qty{30}{mg} of nitrogen a day. With
        \qty{16}{} \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} and \qty{8}{} electrons per $\mathrm{N_2}$,
        compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} spent by nitrogenase per day.
  \item At \qty{6}{g} of sugar per gram of nitrogen fixed overall,
        compute the clover's daily cost in sugar and compare it with a
        production of \qty{3}{g} of sugar per day. Is the bargain
        worth it in a soil of \qty{2}{mmol/L} nitrate? In a soil with
        none?
  \item Phosphate is at \qty{2}{\micro mol/L} in the soil solution.
        Compute the phosphate the water flux of question 8 brings in,
        and compare with a need of \qty{4}{mg} of phosphorus a day
        (\qty{31}{g/mol}). What must the plant rely on?
  \item Explain in two sentences how the \omterm{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:pathways}{Casparian strip} lets the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}
        keep nitrate at \qty{5}{mmol/L} in its \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} while the soil is
        at \qty{2}{}.
  \item A mutant lacks the \omterm{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:pathways}{Casparian strip}. Predict the composition of
        its \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} sap and its behaviour in salty soil.
  \item State the result: the water flux into the maize \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} per day,
        the nitrogen it delivers, and the fraction of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s
        electrons spent reducing it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
