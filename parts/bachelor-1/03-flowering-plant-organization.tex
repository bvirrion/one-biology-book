\chapter{Functional Organization of a Flowering Plant}\label{ch:b1:flowering-plant-organization}

An oak two centuries old stands in the same square metre of ground it
germinated in. It has never eaten, never moved, never held its
temperature; yet it has built forty tonnes of wood, and every summer it
spreads five hundred square metres of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} into the air and a greater
surface of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} into the soil. A flowering plant is the other answer to
the problem of \cref{ch:b1:organism-environment}: a fixed autotroph
that meets its environment not by internalising its exchanges but by
growing its \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surfaces} outward, indefinitely, wherever light and
water are. This chapter describes how such a body is organised --- its
\omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs}, its tissues, the surfaces it deploys, and the growth that
builds them.

\section{The vegetative body}

\begin{definition}[Root, stem, leaf]\label{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}
The vegetative body of a flowering plant is made of three kinds of
\omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ}. The \emph{root}\index{root} anchors the plant and absorbs water
and mineral ions from the soil; it grows from its tip, branches, and
bears \emph{root hairs}\index{root hair} just behind the tip. The
\emph{stem}\index{stem} carries the leaves toward the light and
conducts between roots and leaves; it is built of repeated units, each a
\emph{node}\index{node} bearing a leaf and an axillary bud, and an
\emph{internode}\index{internode} between two nodes. The
\emph{leaf}\index{leaf} is the \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} of photosynthesis: a flat blade of
large surface and small thickness, held by a petiole. Roots form the
root system; stems and leaves, the shoot system.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[A modular, open body]\label{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:modular}
The plant body is \emph{modular}\index{modular growth}: the shoot is a
series of identical units (node, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}, bud, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{internode}) added one after
another by an apical bud, and each axillary bud can start a new series.
Growth is \emph{indeterminate}\index{indeterminate growth}: there is no
adult size, and the plant keeps adding modules, and thus surface, for as
long as it lives. The number and placement of the modules depend on the
site --- light, water, damage --- so the same species takes different
shapes in different places. A mammal's body, by contrast, is closed:
its \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} are fixed in number and it stops growing at a set size.
\end{proposition}

\section{The tissues of the primary body}

\begin{definition}[Dermal, ground and vascular tissues]\label{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}
Every \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} of the plant is built from three tissue systems. The
\emph{dermal tissue}\index{dermal tissue} is the
\emph{epidermis}\index{epidermis}, a single layer of cells covering the
\omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ}, coated in the air with a waxy \emph{cuticle}\index{cuticle} and
pierced by \emph{stomata}\index{stoma}, and extended in the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} by
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hairs}. The \emph{ground tissue}\index{ground tissue} fills the
\omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ}: \emph{parenchyma}\index{parenchyma} (living, thin-walled cells
for photosynthesis and storage), \emph{collenchyma}\index{collenchyma}
(living cells with unevenly thickened walls, the flexible support of
young stems) and \emph{sclerenchyma}\index{sclerenchyma} (cells with
thick lignified walls, dead at maturity, the rigid support: fibres and
stone cells). The \emph{vascular tissue}\index{vascular tissue} conducts:
\emph{xylem}\index{xylem} carries water and minerals upward in the
hollow, lignified, dead \emph{tracheids}\index{tracheid} and
\emph{vessels}\index{vessel} (\cref{ch:b1:plant-transport});
\emph{phloem}\index{phloem} carries sugars in living
\emph{sieve tubes}\index{sieve tube} kept alive by their companion
cells.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Stem and root differ in the placement of the vascular tissues]\label{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:stemroot}
In the young stem the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{vascular tissues} form bundles arranged in a ring
(dicots) or scattered through the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{ground tissue} (monocots), each bundle
with \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} toward the centre and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{phloem} toward the outside; there is
no \omterm{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:stemroot}{endodermis}, and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{ground tissue} is divided into a central pith and
an outer cortex. In the young \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} there is a single central cylinder:
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} forms a star (two to five arms in dicots, many in monocots),
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{phloem} lies between the arms, and the cylinder is bounded by the
\emph{endodermis}\index{endodermis}, a ring of cells whose radial walls
carry a waterproof band (\cref{ch:b1:plant-water-minerals}). The
placement fits the function: a stem resists bending, and a ring of
bundles near its surface is stiffest for its mass; a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} resists
pulling, and a central cylinder is the arrangement of a cable.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Identifying a plant section]\label{met:b1:flowering-plant-organization:section}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A single central vascular cylinder with an \omterm{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:stemroot}{endodermis}: a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}.
        Bundles in a ring or scattered, with a pith: a stem. A flat
        \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} with two epidermises and a spongy layer: a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}.
  \item \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{Root} with few \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} arms (2--5): dicot; many arms around a
        pith: monocot. Stem with bundles in one ring: dicot; scattered
        bundles: monocot.
  \item \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{Xylem} is recognised by its large, thick-walled, empty cells
        (red with the usual stains); \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{phloem} by small living cells with
        \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{sieve tubes} and companion cells (green or blue).
  \item In a stem section, the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} points inward and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{phloem}
        outward. In a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{phloem} alternate on the same
        radius.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Where the exchanges happen]\label{ex:b1:flowering-plant-organization:where}
Carbon dioxide enters a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} through its \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata}, crosses the air
spaces of the spongy layer and dissolves in the wet walls of the
palisade cells; water arrives in the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} of the veins and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves},
as vapour, through the same \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata}. Water and ions enter the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}
through the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hairs} and cross the cortex to the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} at the centre.
Both \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} put their \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface} on the outside and their
conducting tissue in the middle.
\end{example}

\section{The fixed life: surfaces}

\begin{definition}[Leaf area index]\label{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:lai}
The \emph{leaf area index}\index{leaf area index} $L$ of a plant cover
is the total one-sided \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} area per unit area of ground. A meadow in
June has $L \approx 3$, a deciduous forest \qtyrange{4}{6}{}, a
tropical rain forest up to \qty{8}{}: several layers of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} are
stacked above every square metre of soil.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Light interception by a canopy]\label{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:beer}
The fraction of the incident light that reaches the ground under a
canopy of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:lai}{leaf area index} $L$ falls off exponentially,
\[
\frac{I(L)}{I_0} = e^{-kL},
\]
with an extinction coefficient $k$ between $0.3$ (erect \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}) and
$0.8$ (horizontal \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}), typically $0.5$. A canopy of $L = 5$ with
$k = 0.5$ intercepts $1 - e^{-2.5} = \qty{92}{\%}$ of the light.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Descending through the canopy, each thin layer of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} area $\dd L$
intercepts a fraction $k\,\dd L$ of the light reaching it, $k$ being
the fraction of the horizontal area shaded by unit \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} area (which
depends on \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} orientation). Hence $\dd I = -kI\,\dd L$, whose
solution is the exponential.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The root surface exceeds the leaf surface]\label{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:rootsurface}
A single rye plant grown four months in a box of \qty{0.05}{m^3} of
soil produced \qty{620}{km} of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}, of surface \qty{240}{m^2}, and
$\num{1.4e10}$ \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hairs} of a further \qty{400}{m^2}: a surface some
hundred times that of its \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}, spread through the soil at a density
of ten kilometres of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} per litre. \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{Root hairs}, each a tubular
extension of one epidermal cell \qtyrange{5}{20}{\micro m} wide and up
to a millimetre long, are where most of the absorption occurs; they
live a few days and are replaced as the tip advances.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Dittmer (1937) washed the entire \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} system of one rye plant out of
its box, counted and measured samples of every \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} order and of the
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hairs} under the microscope, and scaled up: \qty{13.8}{million}
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} totalling \qty{623}{km}, and \qty{14}{billion} hairs totalling
\qty{10620}{km}. Soil water and minerals move so slowly that a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}
must be within a millimetre of them to take them up; the enormous
length is what puts a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} within reach of every droplet.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The oak's two surfaces]\label{ex:b1:flowering-plant-organization:oaksurfaces}
An oak whose crown shades \qty{100}{m^2} at $L = 5$ carries
\qty{500}{m^2} of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}. Its fine \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}, at a few kilometres per cubic
metre through \qty{100}{m^3} of soil, offer several hundred square
metres, and their \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hairs} a thousand more: the absorbing surface
underground is several times the photosynthetic surface above, over
the same square metres of ground.
\end{example}

\section{Growth and form}

\begin{definition}[Meristems, primary and secondary growth]\label{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:meristem}
A \emph{meristem}\index{meristem} is a tissue of small, undifferentiated
cells that keep dividing. The \emph{apical meristems}\index{apical
meristem} at the tips of every shoot and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} produce \emph{primary
growth}\index{primary growth}: elongation, and the primary tissues
above. The \emph{lateral meristems}\index{lateral meristem} ---
the vascular cambium between \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{phloem}, and the cork cambium
under the epidermis --- produce \emph{secondary growth}\index{secondary
growth}: thickening, by adding wood (secondary \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}) inward and bark
outward, in the stems and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} of trees and shrubs. The mechanisms of
meristem activity belong to the Year 2 volume.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Form follows site]\label{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:plasticity}
Because its growth is modular and indeterminate, a plant shapes its
body to its site (\emph{phenotypic plasticity}\index{phenotypic
plasticity}): a tree grown in the open branches low and wide, the same
species in a forest grows a tall bare trunk; a plant in dry soil
allocates more of its growth to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}, in shade more to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}. Species
of dry habitats (\emph{xerophytes}\index{xerophyte}) carry thick
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{cuticles}, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} sunk in pits or protected by hairs, small or
succulent \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} and deep \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}; species of wet habitats
(\emph{hydrophytes}\index{hydrophyte}) carry thin \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{cuticles}, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} on
the upper surface of floating \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}, and air-filled tissue
(\emph{aerenchyma}\index{aerenchyma}) that carries oxygen down to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}
in anoxic mud.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Mammal and plant, side by side]\label{ex:b1:flowering-plant-organization:comparison}
Nutrition: the mammal eats organic matter; the plant makes it from
carbon dioxide, water and light. \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{Exchange surfaces}: internal, fixed in
size, perfused by blood; external, growing, ventilated by the wind and
the soil. \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:milieu}{Internal environment}: regulated fluid at constant temperature
and composition; none --- the cells regulate their own contents and the
plant's temperature is the air's. Growth: to a fixed adult form; open
and modular, all life long. Movement: the animal goes to its food; the
plant grows toward light and water. Coordination: nerves and hormones
in seconds to hours; hormones alone, in hours to days. Each column is a
consistent way of being an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{open system}.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:1}
Name the three vegetative \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} of a flowering plant and the main
function of each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:2}
Define a module of the shoot and explain what "\omterm{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:modular}{indeterminate growth}"
means.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:3}
For each tissue --- epidermis, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{parenchyma}, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{collenchyma}, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{sclerenchyma},
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{phloem} --- say whether its cells are alive at maturity and what
it does.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:4}
From the light-interception figure, what fraction of the light reaches
the ground under a meadow of $L = 3$ with $k = 0.3$, and under a
broadleaf forest of $L = 6$ with $k = 0.5$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:5}
A section shows a central star of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} with four arms, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{phloem}
between the arms, a ring of cells with thickened radial walls around
it, and a wide zone of rounded cells outside. Identify the \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} and
the group, giving the two features that decide each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:6}
Explain, using the mechanics of a beam and of a cable, why the
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{vascular tissue} lies in a peripheral ring in the stem and in a central
cylinder in the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:7}
A crop has $L = 4$ and $k = 0.6$. What fraction of the light does it
intercept? By how much would the interception rise if $L$ went to 6?
Comment on the return of the extra \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:8}
Dittmer's rye plant had \qty{623}{km} of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} and \qty{240}{m^2} of
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} surface. Compute the mean \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} diameter. If its \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} totalled
\qty{5}{m^2}, what is the ratio of root-plus-hair surface to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}
surface?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:9}
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{Root hairs} are $\qty{10}{\micro m}$ wide and $\qty{0.8}{mm}$ long, at
\qty{250}{} per millimetre of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}. By what factor do they multiply the
surface of a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} $\qty{0.4}{mm}$ in diameter?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:10}
A tree in a forest has a bare trunk of \qty{20}{m} and a small crown;
the same species in a field is broad and branched from \qty{2}{m}.
Explain both forms from \omterm{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:modular}{modular growth} and the fate of axillary buds in
shade and in light, and say what this plasticity costs and gains.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:11}
A \omterm{prop:b1:flowering-plant-organization:plasticity}{xerophyte} has its \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} sunk in pits lined with hairs. Explain
what this does to the gradient of water vapour between the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s air
spaces and the wind, and what it costs in carbon dioxide uptake. Why
is this a good trade in a desert and a bad one in a rain forest?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:flowering-plant-organization:12}
"A plant has no \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:milieu}{internal environment}." Discuss in a paragraph: what
the plant lacks that the mammal has, what its cells face as a
consequence, and what it does instead --- surfaces, growth, and the
regulation it does perform.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Surfaces of an Oak}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- an oak's leaves counted by area,
its stomata by the billion, its carbon and water by the kilogram, and
its roots by the kilometre, ending on the exchange surface it spreads
over each square metre of its ground}]
\label{pb:b1:flowering-plant-organization:1}
An oak's crown shades \qty{100}{m^2} of ground with a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:lai}{leaf area index}
$L = 5$ and an extinction coefficient $k = 0.5$. Its lower \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}
surfaces carry \qty{150}{} \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} per square millimetre; an open
stomatal pore is \qty{10}{\micro m} by \qty{5}{\micro m}. Averaged over
the whole canopy and a \qty{12}{h} day, each square metre of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} takes
up \qty{4}{\micro mol} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and loses \qty{1}{mmol} of
water vapour per second. Fine \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} (diameter \qty{0.5}{mm}) run at
\qty{5}{km} per cubic metre through \qty{1}{m} depth of soil under the
crown; \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hairs} (\qty{10}{\micro m} by \qty{0.5}{mm}) grow at
\qty{200}{} per millimetre of fine \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}. Molar masses:
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ \qty{44}{g/mol}, C \qty{12}{g/mol},
$\mathrm{H_2O}$ \qty{18}{g/mol}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{Leaves} and light.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the total one-sided \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} area of the crown.
  \item Compute the fraction of light reaching the ground and the
        fraction intercepted.
  \item Compute the fraction intercepted if the oak had only $L = 2$,
        and if it had $L = 8$.
  \item Explain from these numbers why an oak stops at $L \approx 5$.
  \item The average \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} is \qty{50}{cm^2}. How many \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} does the
        crown carry?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{Stomata} and carbon.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the number of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} on the crown.
  \item Compute the area of one open pore and the fraction of the
        lower \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} surface that the open pores represent.
  \item Compute the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ uptake of the crown in moles per
        second and in grams per second.
  \item Compute the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ taken up in one \qty{12}{h} day, in
        kilograms, and the carbon it contains.
  \item Over a growing season of \qty{150}{} days, how much carbon
        does the crown fix? If half is respired by the tree itself,
        what mass of wood (\qty{50}{\%} carbon) can it add?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Water.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the water lost by the crown in moles and in grams per
        second.
  \item Compute the daily water loss in litres.
  \item Compute the ratio of water molecules lost to $\mathrm{CO_2}$
        molecules gained. Why is it so large?
  \item Rain of \qty{2}{mm} falls on the \qty{100}{m^2}. How many
        days of transpiration does it cover, if all of it reaches the
        \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{Roots}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the volume of soil explored and the total length of
        fine \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}.
  \item Compute the surface of the fine \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} (cylinders).
  \item Compute the number of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hairs}.
  \item Compute the surface of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root hairs}.
  \item Compute the total absorbing surface underground, and its ratio
        to the one-sided \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} area.
  \item The fine \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} are within \qty{1}{mm} of what fraction of the
        soil volume? (Take each \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} as the axis of a cylinder of
        radius \qty{1}{mm}.)
  \item Compare with the rye plant of the chapter (\qty{620}{km} of
        \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} in \qty{0.05}{m^3}): which of the two plants explores its
        soil more thoroughly, and by what factor?
  \item Sum the two-sided \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} surface and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} surface. Divide by
        the \qty{100}{m^2} of ground.
  \item Compare this with the ratio, for a human, of the folded
        \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surfaces} (\qty{130}{m^2}) to the outer surface
        (\qty{2}{m^2}).
  \item Explain in two sentences why the plant's ratio can keep rising
        through its life and the mammal's cannot.
  \item State the result: the \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface} an oak spreads per
        square metre of its ground, above and below, in square metres.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
