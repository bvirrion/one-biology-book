\chapter{Ecosystem Dynamics}\label{ch:b1:ecosystem-dynamics}

Where a glacier in the north Pacific has been retreating for two
centuries, a walk from its snout back down the fjord is a walk
through time. The rock uncovered last year is bare; a kilometre on,
it wears mosses and a nitrogen-fixing mat; further, thickets of
alder; then spruce, then a forest of spruce and hemlock two hundred
years old with a metre of soil beneath it. Nothing here is static:
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystems} change after every \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}{disturbance}, along a path that is
partly predictable, and their capacity to hold and recycle their
nutrients changes with them. This chapter follows those changes ---
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:succession}{succession}, \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}{disturbance} and recovery, the retention of nutrients,
the assembly of \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{communities} on islands --- and the experiments that
turned them from narratives into numbers.

\section{Succession}

\begin{definition}[Ecological succession]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:succession}
\index{succession}\index{primary succession}\index{secondary succession}\index{climax}
\emph{Succession} is the directional change of a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community} over time
after a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}{disturbance}, one set of species replacing another until a
relatively stable \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community}, the \emph{climax}, persists. \emph{Primary
succession} starts on new substrate without soil or living
\omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms}: rock left by a glacier, a lava flow, a dune, a new island.
\emph{Secondary succession} starts where a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community} has been
destroyed but the soil and its seed bank remain: an abandoned field,
a burnt or felled forest, a flooded meadow. The first arrivals,
\emph{pioneer species}, are dispersers that tolerate bare ground;
late arrivals are competitors that tolerate shade and need soil.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The mechanisms of replacement]\label{prop:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:mechanisms}
\index{facilitation}
Each stage of a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:succession}{succession} can bring the next about in three ways.
\emph{Facilitation}: the earlier species make the site fit for the
later ones --- building soil, fixing nitrogen, casting shade,
holding moisture --- and are then displaced by them. \emph{Inhibition}:
the first occupants hold the site against all comers until they die
or are damaged, and what replaces them is whatever arrives then.
\emph{Tolerance}: the later species arrive at the start too, grow
slowly and simply outlast the pioneers, whose presence neither helps
nor hinders them. Most \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:succession}{successions} show all three at different
stages; \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:succession}{primary succession} is dominated by facilitation, since the
first task is to make a soil.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
At Glacier Bay in Alaska, where the ice has retreated some
\qty{100}{km} since 1750, the age of each site is known from maps and
tree rings, and the stages can be visited in order: bare till, a
crust of cyanobacteria and mosses, mats of the nitrogen-fixing
\emph{Dryas}, alder thickets (nitrogen-fixing, adding
\qty{50}{kg} of nitrogen per hectare per year), then Sitka spruce,
then a spruce--hemlock forest after two centuries. Soil nitrogen rises
from nearly nothing to \qty{300}{kg/ha} under the alders and the
spruces establish only after them; the pH falls from 8 to below 5 as
the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}{litter} accumulates. Seedlings of spruce planted on the young till
grow only if fertilised with nitrogen: the alders are facilitators.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[An old field]\label{ex:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:oldfield}
A field abandoned in the eastern United States is colonised the first
year by annual weeds (crabgrass, ragweed) from the seed bank and the
wind; by the third year perennial herbs and goldenrod dominate; by
the tenth, shrubs and pine seedlings; by the twenty-fifth a pine
wood, under whose shade the pines' own seedlings cannot grow but
oaks and hickories can; and by the hundredth an oak--hickory forest,
whose seedlings tolerate its shade and which therefore replaces
itself. Each stage is displaced by species that were worse
dispersers and better competitors than itself; the soil was there
from the start, and the whole sequence takes a human lifetime rather
than the millennia of a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:succession}{primary succession}.
\end{example}

\section{Disturbance, stability and diversity}

\begin{definition}[Disturbance, resistance, resilience]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}
\index{disturbance}\index{resilience}\index{resistance}
A \emph{disturbance} is an event --- fire, storm, flood, drought,
grazing, felling, an epidemic --- that removes biomass and opens
space. An \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem}'s \emph{resistance} is its capacity to remain
unchanged through a disturbance; its \emph{resilience} is the speed
with which it returns to its former state afterward. A forest is
resistant to a dry summer and slow to recover from a fire; a
grassland is the reverse. \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{Communities} are shaped by their
disturbance regime: its frequency, intensity and extent. Many
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{communities} are mosaics of patches at different stages of
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:succession}{succession} since their last disturbance, and their diversity is the
sum over the mosaic.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The intermediate disturbance hypothesis]\label{prop:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:idh}
\index{intermediate disturbance hypothesis}
Diversity is highest at intermediate frequencies and intensities of
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}{disturbance}. Where \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:disturbance}{disturbances} are rare the best competitors
exclude the rest; where they are frequent only the fastest
colonisers survive; between the two, pioneers and competitors
coexist, each in the patches that suit it.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
On rocky shores and coral reefs, the number of species of algae or
corals on boulders and reef sections is greatest on those that are
overturned or broken by storms at intermediate rates: small boulders
(turned often) carry a few pioneer algae, large ones (rarely turned)
a monoculture of the best competitor, medium ones the most species.
The same hump appears in stream beds against flood frequency and in
grasslands against grazing intensity.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Does diversity make an ecosystem stable?]\label{rem:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:diversity-stability}
Grassland plots sown with more species keep their biomass more
steadily through droughts than plots with few, because with more
species some are always suited to the year's weather --- an insurance
effect. But diverse \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{communities} are not always more resistant, and
the individual \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} in them fluctuate as much as anywhere:
diversity stabilises the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem}'s functions (production, nutrient
retention) more than its composition.
\end{remark}

\section{Decomposition and the retention of nutrients}

\begin{definition}[Decomposition and nutrient budget]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}
\index{decomposition}\index{nutrient budget}\index{litter}
\emph{Decomposition} is the breakdown of dead organic matter
(\emph{litter}, dead wood, corpses, dung) by detritivores and
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers} into carbon dioxide, water and mineral ions ---
\emph{mineralisation}. Its rate depends on temperature, moisture,
oxygen and the litter's quality (nitrogen content, lignin): a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}
disappears in a year in a warm wet forest, in a decade under conifers
on a cold slope, in millennia in a bog. The mineral ions released
are taken up again by \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} and microbes, and in a mature \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem}
this internal cycle carries most of the nutrient flow: the input from
weathering and rain and the output in stream water are small beside
it. A \emph{nutrient budget} compares inputs, outputs and the internal
pools of one element for a defined \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem}; the biogeochemical
cycles at the scale of the planet are treated in the Year 2 volume.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The living community retains nutrients]\label{prop:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:retention}
Nutrients are kept in an \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem} by the uptake of living plants and
microbes; when the vegetation is removed, mineralisation continues
while uptake stops, and the released ions are washed out into the
streams --- the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem} leaks until a new cover regrows.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
At Hubbard Brook in New Hampshire, six small forested valleys are
each drained by one stream fitted with a weir, so that everything
leaving in the water can be measured against what arrives in the
rain. In 1965--66 one valley was clear-felled and kept bare with
herbicide for three years, its neighbour left as a control. Stream
flow from the cut valley rose by \qty{40}{\%} (no transpiration).
Nitrate in its stream rose forty- to sixtyfold, calcium tenfold,
potassium twentyfold; the total loss of dissolved substances was six
to eight times the control's --- nitrogen that the intact forest had
been holding in its cycle for centuries left in two summers. When
regrowth was allowed, the losses fell back within a few years, as
fast as the new vegetation took up what the soil released.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Ecosystem engineers]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:engineers}
\index{ecosystem engineer}
An \emph{\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem} engineer} is a species that changes the physical
environment for others: beavers flood valleys, earthworms mix and
aerate the soil, corals and mussels build reefs, trees cast shade and
hold water, elephants open forest into grassland, burrowers
oxygenate sediments. Engineers act on the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{biotope} rather than through
trophic links, and their effect can outlast them (a beaver meadow,
a reef, a peat bog).
\end{definition}

\section{Islands and the assembly of communities}

\begin{theorem}[The equilibrium theory of island biogeography]\label{thm:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:island}
\index{island biogeography}\index{species--area relationship}
The number of species on an island is a dynamic equilibrium between
immigration from a source pool of $P$ species and extinction on the
island. If the immigration rate of new species falls linearly with
the number $S$ already present, $I = I_0(1 - S/P)$, and the extinction
rate rises linearly with it, $E = eS$, the equilibrium is
\[
S^* = \frac{I_0 P}{I_0 + eP} ,
\]
reached when $I = E$, with a continual turnover of $eS^*$ species per
unit time. Islands near the source have a higher $I_0$ and more
species; large islands have a lower $e$ (larger \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations}) and more
species. Across a set of islands the number of species rises with area
as $S = cA^z$, a straight line of slope $z$ on a log--log plot, with
$z$ typically between \num{0.2} and \num{0.35}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Partial proof]
$S$ rises while immigration exceeds extinction and falls when
extinction exceeds immigration, so $\dd S/\dd t = I_0(1 - S/P) - eS$
is a first-order linear equation whose single equilibrium, $I_0 -
(I_0/P + e)S = 0$, gives $S^*$ and is stable, since the rate of change
is positive below it and negative above. At equilibrium species are
still arriving and vanishing at the rate $eS^*$: the number is steady,
the list is not. The power law is empirical; its exponent near
\num{0.25} means that a tenfold larger island holds about \num{1.8}
times as many species, and that losing \qty{90}{\%} of a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{habitat} will
eventually lose about half its species.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Simberloff and Wilson (1969) counted the arthropod species on small
mangrove islets off Florida, fumigated the islets to kill every
animal, and counted again at intervals. Within a year each islet had
recovered close to its original number of species --- more on the
near and large islets, fewer on the far and small --- but with a
different list, and the list kept changing: the number was in
equilibrium, the composition in turnover, as the theory required.
Krakatau, sterilised by its eruption in 1883, had \num{30} species of
plants after three years and over \num{270} after fifty, approaching
the richness of similar islands nearby.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Fragmentation]\label{prop:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:fragmentation}
\index{habitat fragmentation}
A \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{habitat} cut into fragments behaves like an archipelago: each
fragment loses species toward the equilibrium its area and isolation
allow, slowly at first (the \emph{extinction debt}), the large-bodied,
rare and specialised species first. Fragments also gain edge --- drier,
windier, more disturbed --- at the expense of interior; a square
fragment of \qty{1}{km^2} with a \qty{100}{m} edge zone keeps
\qty{64}{\%} of its area as interior, one of \qty{0.1}{km^2} only
\qty{15}{\%}. Corridors between fragments raise the immigration rate,
which is why they restore species faster than they add area.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Using a species--area relationship]\label{met:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:sar}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Plot $\log S$ against $\log A$ for a set of islands or
        fragments sampled with equal effort; fit a line by eye or
        least squares.
  \item Read $z$ as the slope and $c$ as the value of $S$ at $A = 1$.
  \item Predict the species of a new area as $cA^z$; predict the
        fraction of species retained when an area is reduced from
        $A_0$ to $A$ as $(A/A_0)^z$ --- with $z = 0.25$, keeping a tenth
        of the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{habitat} keeps \qty{56}{\%} of the species eventually.
  \item Beware: the loss is reached only after the extinction debt is
        paid, sometimes decades later; and the fit says nothing about
        which species go.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Problem: The Felled Valley and the Archipelago}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the nutrient budget of a
clear-felled valley, and the species--area law of an archipelago,
ending on the exponent $z$ and the equilibrium species number of an
island}]
\label{pb:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:1}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Reading a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:succession}{succession}.}
A chronosequence of sites of known age since a glacier's retreat
gives: at 10 years, mosses and a mat of nitrogen fixers, soil
nitrogen \qty{10}{kg/ha}; at 40 years, alder thicket, \qty{150}{kg/ha};
at 80 years, spruce forest, \qty{300}{kg/ha}; at 200 years,
spruce--hemlock, \qty{320}{kg/ha}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the mean rate of nitrogen accumulation in each
        interval and say when it is fastest.
  \item Which mechanism of replacement does the nitrogen curve point
        to, and for which transition?
  \item Why does the accumulation nearly stop after 80 years even
        though the forest is still alive?
  \item The alder fixes \qty{50}{kg/ha} of nitrogen a year. What
        fraction of it is retained in the soil between years 40 and
        80, and where does the rest go?
  \item Propose an experiment that would distinguish facilitation
        from tolerance for the alder-to-spruce transition.
  \item Why is a chronosequence a substitute for, and not the same as,
        watching one site for two centuries?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The felled valley.}
Two adjacent valleys of \qty{15}{ha} each receive \qty{1300}{mm} of
rain a year with \qty{6.5}{kg/ha} of nitrogen dissolved in it. The
forested control valley exports \qty{820}{mm} of stream water a year
carrying \qty{2}{kg/ha} of nitrate-nitrogen, \qty{9}{kg/ha} of calcium
and \qty{2}{kg/ha} of potassium. The felled valley, in the second year
after felling, exports \qty{1150}{mm} carrying \qty{120}{kg/ha} of
nitrate-nitrogen, \qty{90}{kg/ha} of calcium and \qty{36}{kg/ha} of
potassium. Its soil and \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-dynamics:decomposition}{litter} hold \qty{3500}{kg/ha} of nitrogen, the
felled biomass another \qty{350}{kg/ha}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item By how much did the water yield rise, and why?
  \item Compute the mean nitrate-nitrogen concentration of each stream
        in milligrams per litre (\qty{1}{mm} of water over \qty{1}{ha} is
        \qty{10}{m^3}).
  \item Compute the nitrogen balance (input minus output) of each
        valley. Which is accumulating nitrogen, and at what rate?
  \item What fraction of the felled valley's soil nitrogen left in
        that one year? At that rate, how long would the pool last?
  \item By what factors did the calcium and potassium exports rise?
        Why potassium more than calcium (think of where each is held
        in a soil)?
  \item Where did the exported nitrate come from, given that no
        vegetation was growing to release it? Name the two microbial
        steps.
  \item After regrowth is allowed, the export falls to \qty{20}{kg/ha}
        in year 4 and \qty{5}{kg/ha} in year 6. How long until the
        rain input again exceeds the output, and what has restored the
        retention?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The archipelago.}
Five islands of \qtylist{1;10;100;1000;10000}{km^2} carry \num{12}, \num{21}, \num{38}, \num{68} and
\num{120} species of land birds. The mainland pool is \num{300}
species.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute $\log_{10}$ of each area and each species count and
        plot them.
  \item Obtain $z$ from the two end points and $c$ from the smallest
        island; check the middle islands.
  \item Predict the species number of an island of \qty{250}{km^2}.
  \item The \qty{100}{km^2} island receives $I_0 = 6$ new species a year
        when empty and loses a fraction $e$ of its species a year.
        Using $S^* = 38$, compute $e$ and the turnover at equilibrium.
  \item An island of the same area twice as far away has $I_0 = 3$.
        Compute its equilibrium species number with the same $e$.
  \item A bridge connects the \qty{100}{km^2} island to the mainland
        and $I_0$ rises to \num{30}. Compute the new equilibrium and
        explain why it is still far below \num{300}.
  \item If $z$ were \num{0.35} instead of \num{0.25}, would the small
        islands have more or fewer species relative to the large? Say
        what a high $z$ means biologically.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Fragmentation and recovery.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A reserve of \qty{1000}{km^2} is planned in a forest of
        \qty{100000}{km^2} that is otherwise to be cleared. With $z =
        0.25$, what fraction of the forest's species can the reserve
        eventually hold?
  \item Would ten reserves of \qty{100}{km^2} hold more or fewer, if
        they are isolated? If they are connected by corridors?
  \item The reserve's edge zone is \qty{300}{m} wide. Compute the
        interior fraction of a square reserve of \qty{1000}{km^2} and
        of one of \qty{100}{km^2}.
  \item Sketch the number of species of a newly isolated fragment
        against time, and explain the term extinction debt.
  \item State the result: the exponent $z$ of the archipelago and the
        equilibrium number of species of the \qty{100}{km^2} island
        before and after the bridge.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
