\chapter{Blood and the Circulatory System}\label{ch:b2:blood-circulation}

In 1628 William Harvey did a sum. The heart, he estimated, holds
about two ounces of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} and beats seventy times a minute; even if
only a fraction of that were expelled at each beat, the heart would
pump in an hour several times the weight of the whole body. The
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} could not be made and consumed at that rate, as Galen had
taught for fourteen centuries; it must go round and round. Then he
showed it: tie a cord round the arm, and the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} below the cord
swell, not above; press the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} in a \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{vein} toward the hand, past a
valve, and it will not go. The \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} is pumped out through the
arteries, returns through the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins}, and the whole five litres pass
through the heart every minute. This chapter is about that closed
system --- what the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} is, how it is routed, the physics of its
flow through vessels from the aorta to a \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary}, and the
exchange, in the capillaries, that is the point of all of it.

\section{Blood}

\begin{definition}[The composition of blood]\label{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}
\index{blood}\index{plasma}\index{haematocrit}\index{red blood cell}\index{platelet}
Blood is a tissue in suspension: about \qty{5}{L} in an adult,
\qty{7}{\%} of body mass. Spun down, it separates into
\emph{plasma} (\qty{55}{\%}: water, \qty{70}{g/L} of proteins ---
albumin, globulins, fibrinogen --- and the salts, sugars, lipids,
gases and hormones it carries) and cells (\qty{45}{\%}, the
\emph{haematocrit}): \emph{red cells} ($5\times 10^{12}$ per litre,
biconcave discs of \qty{7}{\micro m} without a nucleus, each packed
with \num{280} million molecules of haemoglobin, living 120 days and
made in the marrow at two million a second), \emph{white cells}
($7\times 10^{9}$ per litre: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes,
eosinophils, basophils, the mobile arm of immunity), and
\emph{platelets} ($3\times 10^{11}$ per litre, fragments of marrow
cells that plug wounds and start clotting). The red cells carry
oxygen --- \qty{200}{mL} per litre of blood at full saturation,
seventy times what water dissolves --- and the plasma carries
everything else, including the \qty{20}{\%} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ that
is not in the cells, the heat of the muscles to the skin, and the
hormones of \cref{ch:b2:cell-signalling} to their targets. Blood
is also a solution under \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:oncotic}{oncotic pressure}}: its proteins,
which cannot leave the capillaries, hold about \qty{25}{mmHg} of
osmotic pull, and that pull is what keeps the plasma in the
vessels.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Haemostasis]\label{prop:b2:blood-circulation:clotting}
\index{haemostasis}\index{clotting cascade}\index{fibrin}
A cut vessel is sealed in three steps within minutes. The vessel
constricts; \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{platelets} stick to the exposed collagen, release
signals that recruit and activate more \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{platelets}, and form a plug;
and a \emph{cascade} of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma} proteases, each activating the next
(a dozen clotting factors, most made in the liver, several needing
vitamin K), converts fibrinogen into insoluble \emph{fibrin}, whose
threads mesh the plug into a clot. A cascade amplifies: a trace of
the trigger (tissue factor from the damaged wall) ends in grams of
fibrin. It must also be contained: anticoagulants in the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma}
(antithrombin, protein C) and on the intact endothelium confine the
clot to the wound, and a second system, fibrinolysis, dissolves it
as the vessel heals. Haemophilia is the lack of one factor; a
thrombosis is a clot where none was wanted, and the leading cause of
death in rich countries is a clot in a coronary or cerebral \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{artery}.
\end{proposition}

\section{Circuits}

\begin{definition}[Open and closed, single and double]\label{def:b2:blood-circulation:circuits}
\index{open circulation}\index{closed circulation}\index{double circulation}\index{pulmonary circulation}\index{systemic circulation}
In an \emph{open} circulation (insects, most molluscs) a heart pumps
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} into a body cavity where it bathes the organs directly and
returns at low pressure; flow is slow and poorly directed, and the
insects have separated the gas problem from the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} by piping air
straight to the tissues. In a \emph{closed} circulation (annelids,
cephalopods, vertebrates) the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} stays in vessels and is driven
through capillaries at pressure, so that its distribution can be
controlled organ by organ. Fish have a \emph{single} circuit: heart
$\to$ gills $\to$ body $\to$ heart, so that \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} reaches the tissues
at the low pressure left after the gill capillaries. Mammals and
birds have a \emph{double} circuit: the right heart drives the
\emph{pulmonary} circulation (lungs, low pressure, \qty{25}{mmHg}
systolic) and the left heart, after the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} returns, drives the
\emph{systemic} circulation (body, high pressure, \qty{120}{mmHg});
the two pumps are in series and move the same flow, about \qty{5}{L}
a minute at rest. Amphibians and most reptiles have a heart with a
single ventricle that partly mixes the two streams --- the
intermediate stage. The double circuit is what makes a warm-blooded
metabolism possible: high pressure to every organ and a lung
protected from it.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Harvey (1628) argued from quantity --- the output computed above ---
and from structure: the valves in the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins}, which Fabricius had
described, allow flow only toward the heart, as he showed by
pressing on the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} of a ligatured arm; the valves of the heart
allow flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries; and \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} in
the arteries spurts, in the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} seeps. He could not see the
capillaries and inferred them; Malpighi saw them in a frog's lung
in 1661, four years after Harvey's death.
\end{proof}

\section{Vessels and the physics of flow}

\begin{definition}[The vascular tree]\label{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}
\index{artery}\index{arteriole}\index{capillary}\index{vein}
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{Blood} leaves the heart through \emph{arteries}: thick-walled tubes
with elastic layers that stretch at each beat and recoil between
beats, smoothing the pulses into a steadier flow (the aorta is
\qty{2.5}{cm} across). They branch into \emph{arterioles}, vessels
of a tenth of a millimetre or less whose walls are mostly smooth
muscle: by contracting or relaxing, an arteriole changes its radius
and so, powerfully, its resistance --- arterioles are the taps of
the circulation and the site of most of the pressure drop. These
open into \emph{capillaries}: tubes of a single endothelial cell
wrapped round a lumen of \qtyrange{5}{10}{\micro m}, about
\qty{1}{mm} long, some forty billion of them with a total surface
near \qty{600}{m^2}, across whose walls all exchange takes place.
Capillaries drain into venules and \emph{veins}: thin-walled,
distensible, holding two thirds of the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} at low pressure, with
valves that keep the flow toward the heart while muscles squeeze
them. Every vessel is lined with a single layer of
\emph{endothelium}, which is not a passive lining but a gland ---
it releases nitric oxide to relax the arteriole, and signals that
recruit white cells --- and a filter.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Poiseuille's law and vascular resistance]\label{thm:b2:blood-circulation:poiseuille}
\index{Poiseuille's law}\index{vascular resistance}
The steady flow $Q$ of a fluid of viscosity $\eta$ through a tube
of radius $r$ and length $L$ under a pressure difference $\Delta P$
is
\[
Q = \frac{\pi r^{4}}{8\eta L}\,\Delta P, \qquad \text{so that}\qquad
R \equiv \frac{\Delta P}{Q} = \frac{8\eta L}{\pi r^{4}} .
\]
The resistance falls as the \emph{fourth power} of the radius: an
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arteriole} that narrows its radius by \qty{20}{\%} multiplies its
resistance by $1/0.8^{4} = 2.4$, and one that halves it by 16. This
is why a few millimetres of arteriolar muscle can redirect the
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} of the whole body --- to the gut after a meal, to the muscles
in exercise, to the skin in heat --- and why the pressure falls from
\qty{95}{mmHg} in the small arteries to \qty{35}{mmHg} at the
entrance of the capillaries, almost all of it across the
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles}, while the drop along the aorta is a fraction of a
millimetre of mercury.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In steady laminar flow the fluid moves in concentric shells; the
viscous force between shells balances the pressure. For the
cylinder of radius $s < r$ the pressure force $\pi s^{2}\Delta P$
equals the viscous drag on its surface $2\pi s L\,\eta\,(-\mathrm{d}v/
\mathrm{d}s)$, so $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}s = -s\Delta P/(2\eta L)$
and, with $v(r) = 0$, $v(s) = (\Delta P/4\eta L)(r^{2} - s^{2})$ --- a
parabolic profile. Integrating the velocity over the cross-section,
$Q = \int_{0}^{r} v\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s = (\pi\Delta P/2\eta L)
\int_{0}^{r}(r^{2}s - s^{3})\,\mathrm{d}s = \pi r^{4}\Delta P/8\eta
L$. (\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{Blood} is not quite a simple fluid and arteries are not rigid,
but the fourth-power law holds well enough to run a body.)
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Continuity: where the blood slows down]\label{thm:b2:blood-circulation:continuity}
\index{continuity equation}
The same volume per second passes every level of the tree, so the
mean velocity at a level is $v = Q/A$, where $A$ is the \emph{total}
cross-sectional area of all the vessels at that level. The aorta
(\qty{3}{cm^2}) carries \qty{5}{L/min} at \qty{28}{cm/s}; the
capillaries, with a total cross-section near \qty{3000}{cm^2}, carry
it at \qty{0.03}{cm/s}, so that a red cell takes a second or two to
cross a \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} --- time enough for its oxygen to diffuse out
(\cref{ch:b2:unicellular-diversity}: a micrometre in a
millisecond). The \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins}, of smaller total section than the
capillaries, speed the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} up again to \qty{10}{cm/s} in the venae
cavae. The tree is built so that the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} is slow exactly where it
must exchange and fast everywhere else.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Conservation of volume: what enters a level per second leaves it,
so $Q = A\,v$ is the same at every level. $\qty{5}{L/min} =
\qty{83}{cm^3/s}$; $83/3 = \qty{28}{cm/s}$; $83/3000 =
\qty{0.028}{cm/s}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The elastic artery as a reservoir]\label{thm:b2:blood-circulation:windkessel}
\index{Windkessel}\index{compliance!arterial}\index{pulse pressure}
The heart ejects in bursts; the tissues receive a nearly steady
flow. The large arteries do the smoothing: they stretch during
ejection, storing part of the stroke volume, and recoil during
diastole, driving it on. If the arteries have a \emph{compliance}
$C$ (volume stored per unit pressure) and the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} a
resistance $R$, then during diastole, with the aortic valve shut,
the pressure decays as
\[
P(t) = P_{s}\,e^{-t/RC},
\]
so that the diastolic pressure after a diastole of duration $t_d$
is $P_s e^{-t_d/RC}$: with $R = \qty{1}{mmHg.s/mL}$, $C =
\qty{2}{mL/mmHg}$ and $t_d = \qty{0.8}{s}$, a systolic
\qty{120}{mmHg} falls to \qty{80}{mmHg}. A stiffer aorta (smaller
$C$, as in old age) lets the pressure fall further between beats
and rise higher during ejection: the \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:windkessel}{pulse pressure}} widens,
and the heart works against a higher peak.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
During diastole no \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} enters the arteries and \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} leaves them
through the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} at $Q = P/R$; the arterial volume falls at
$\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}t = -P/R$, and since $\mathrm{d}V = C\,
\mathrm{d}P$, $C\,\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}t = -P/R$, whose solution is
the exponential with time constant $RC$. With $RC = \qty{2}{s}$:
$120\,e^{-0.4} = \qty{80}{mmHg}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Exchange in the capillaries}

\begin{proposition}[Two kinds of exchange]\label{prop:b2:blood-circulation:exchange}
\index{capillary exchange}\index{Starling forces}\index{oedema}\index{lymph}
Across the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} wall, solutes move by \emph{diffusion} --- gases
and lipids through the cells, water and small solutes through the
clefts between them, over the enormous area and the short distance
that make the flux ample (Fick's law, as for the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} of
\cref{ch:b2:mammal-reproduction}) --- and water moves by
\emph{bulk flow}, driven by the balance of two pressures. The
hydrostatic pressure in the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary}, $P_c$, pushes fluid out; the
\omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:oncotic}{oncotic pressure} of the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma} proteins, $\pi_c$, pulls it in
(\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:blood-circulation:exchange}{Starling forces}}). At the arterial end $P_c \approx
\qty{35}{mmHg}$ exceeds $\pi_c \approx \qty{25}{mmHg}$ and fluid
filters out; at the venous end $P_c \approx \qty{15}{mmHg}$ and fluid
is reabsorbed; over the whole body about \qty{20}{L} a day filter
out and \qty{17}{L} return, and the balance of \qty{3}{L}, with the
proteins that leaked, is collected by the \emph{lymphatic} vessels
and returned to the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} at the neck. \emph{Oedema} --- swelling
by fluid in the tissues --- follows whenever the balance tips:
high venous pressure (heart failure), low \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma} protein
(starvation, liver or kidney disease), leaky capillaries
(inflammation), or blocked lymphatics.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Oncotic pressure]\label{thm:b2:blood-circulation:oncotic}
\index{oncotic pressure}\index{albumin}
A solution of $c$ moles per litre of a solute that cannot cross a
membrane exerts across it an osmotic pressure $\pi = cRT$ (van 't
Hoff). \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{Plasma} albumin, \qty{40}{g/L} of a protein of molar mass
\qty{66}{kg/mol}, is $c = \qty{0.6}{mmol/L}$ and gives $\pi =
0.6\times 8.314\times 310 = \qty{1.55}{kPa} \approx \qty{12}{mmHg}$;
the other proteins and the extra ions that albumin's negative
charge retains bring the total to about \qty{25}{mmHg}. The sodium
chloride of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma}, at \qty{150}{mmol/L}, would exert
\qty{5800}{mmHg} --- but it crosses the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} wall freely and
exerts none across it. What matters for the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} is the
protein: halve the albumin, as in kidney disease that loses it in
the urine, and the reabsorbing pull halves, fluid accumulates in
the tissues, and the patient swells.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\qty{40}{g/L}/\qty{66000}{g/mol} = \qty{6.1e-4}{mol/L} =
\qty{0.61}{mol/m^3}$; $\pi = cRT = 0.61\times 8.314\times 310 =
\qty{1570}{Pa}$; $\qty{1}{mmHg} = \qty{133}{Pa}$, so \qty{11.8}{mmHg}.
The van 't Hoff law itself is the dilute-solution limit derived in
the Year 1 volume.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The circulation as a transport system]\label{ex:b2:blood-circulation:transport}
Five litres a minute through \qty{600}{m^2} of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} wall is a
delivery service without equal. It brings each cell oxygen within
a few cell diameters (no cell of the body is more than
\qty{20}{\micro m} from a \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary}), removes its $\mathrm{CO_2}$,
lactate and urea, carries the glucose of the liver to the brain and
the fatty acids of the fat stores to the muscles, distributes the
hormones of \cref{ch:b2:cell-signalling} from one gland to every
receptor in the body in a minute, moves the heat of the core to the
skin and back, and ferries the white cells to a wound within
hours. It is also the vulnerability: a clot, a haemorrhage or a
failing pump stops all of it at once, and the brain, which stores
no fuel, fails within seconds. The rest of this part of the book is
about the pump (\cref{ch:b2:heart}) and about how the pressure is
regulated so that the service continues through standing up,
running and bleeding (\cref{ch:b2:blood-pressure}).
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:1}
Give the composition of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} by volume and the number, size and
function of each cellular component.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:2}
Draw the circuit of a fish and of a mammal, mark the pressures, and
say what the double circuit gains.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:3}
For each vessel type --- \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{artery}, \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arteriole}, \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary}, \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{vein} --- give
the wall structure and the function it serves.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:4}
Reconstruct Harvey's argument from quantity, with modern numbers:
\qty{70}{mL} per beat, \num{72} beats a minute, \qty{5}{L} of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:5}
Compute the pressure drop along the aorta (radius \qty{1.25}{cm},
length \qty{40}{cm}, $\eta = \qty{3e-3}{Pa.s}$) carrying
\qty{5}{L/min} by \omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:poiseuille}{Poiseuille's law}, in pascals and in mmHg. Comment.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:6}
An \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arteriole} of radius \qty{15}{\micro m} constricts to
\qty{12}{\micro m}, then dilates to \qty{20}{\micro m}. By what
factor does its resistance change in each case, and its flow at
constant pressure?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:7}
The aorta has a cross-section of \qty{3}{cm^2} and the capillaries a
total of \qty{3000}{cm^2}; the flow is \qty{5}{L/min}. Compute the
mean velocity in each, and the time a red cell spends in a
\qty{1}{mm} \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary}. During exercise the output rises to
\qty{25}{L/min} and the muscle capillaries open: what happens to the
transit time?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:8}
With $P_c = 35$ and \qty{15}{mmHg} at the two ends, $\pi_c =
\qty{25}{mmHg}$, interstitial pressures negligible, compute the net
filtration pressure at each end. What happens if venous pressure
rises so that $P_c$ at the venous end is \qty{28}{mmHg}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:9}
Compute the \omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:oncotic}{oncotic pressure} of \qty{40}{g/L} of albumin
(\qty{66}{kg/mol}) at \qty{37}{\celsius}, and of \qty{20}{g/L}. Why
does the sodium of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma}, at \qty{150}{mmol/L}, contribute nothing
to the Starling balance?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:10}
With $R = \qty{1}{mmHg.s/mL}$ and $C = \qty{2}{mL/mmHg}$, compute the
diastolic pressure after \qty{0.8}{s} from a systolic of
\qty{120}{mmHg}. Recompute for an aorta half as compliant. Explain why
the \omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:windkessel}{pulse pressure} of the elderly is wide, and what it costs the
heart.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:11}
The systemic resistance is $R = (P_{\text{art}} - P_{\text{ven}})/Q$.
Compute it at rest ($95 - 5$ mmHg, \qty{5}{L/min}) and in exercise
($110 - 5$, \qty{25}{L/min}). By what factor must the mean arteriolar
radius have changed, if the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} carry the whole resistance?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:blood-circulation:12}
``The circulation is built so that the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} is slow where it must
exchange and fast everywhere else.'' Discuss, with the \omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:continuity}{continuity
equation}, \omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:poiseuille}{Poiseuille's law} and the geometry of the tree, and say
why an \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:circuits}{open circulation} cannot do the same.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Circulation in Numbers}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- Harvey's sum redone, the vessel
tree's pressures and speeds computed by Poiseuille and continuity,
the capillaries' daily filtration balanced, and the aorta's
smoothing modelled, ending on the cardiac output, the arteriolar
pressure drop, the day's filtrate and the diastolic pressure}]
\label{pb:b2:blood-circulation:1}
Data: stroke volume \qty{70}{mL}, heart rate \qty{72}{min^{-1}},
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} volume \qty{5}{L}, $\eta = \qty{3e-3}{Pa.s}$, $\qty{1}{mmHg} =
\qty{133}{Pa}$. Aorta: radius \qty{1.25}{cm}, length \qty{40}{cm}.
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{Arterioles}: $3\times 10^{6}$ in parallel, each of radius
\qty{15}{\micro m} and length \qty{1}{mm}. Capillaries: $4\times
10^{10}$, radius \qty{3}{\micro m}, length \qty{1}{mm}, a quarter of
them open at rest. Starling: $P_c$ from 35 to \qty{15}{mmHg} along a
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary}, $\pi_c = \qty{25}{mmHg}$. Windkessel: $C =
\qty{2}{mL/mmHg}$, diastole \qty{0.8}{s}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Harvey's sum.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the cardiac output in litres per minute and per day.
  \item How many times does the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} volume circulate in a day?
  \item Harvey's estimate was \qty{2}{ounces} (\qty{57}{g}) per beat,
        of which he supposed at least a quarter expelled, at 72 beats
        a minute. What mass of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} did that give per hour, and how
        did it compare with a man's weight?
  \item Why was this an argument for circulation rather than for
        continuous production and consumption?
  \item Describe the ligature experiment and what the valves showed.
  \item What could Harvey not see, and who saw it?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The tree.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the mean velocity in the aorta.
  \item Compute the pressure drop along the aorta by \omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:poiseuille}{Poiseuille's
        law}, in mmHg.
  \item Compute the resistance of one \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arteriole} and of the $3\times
        10^{6}$ in parallel.
  \item Compute the pressure drop across the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} at the
        resting output, in mmHg.
  \item Compute the total cross-section of the open capillaries and
        the mean velocity in them; then the transit time through one.
  \item The \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} constrict so that their radius falls by
        \qty{10}{\%}. Recompute the drop. What must the heart do to
        keep the same output?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The capillaries.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the net filtration pressure at the arterial end, at
        the venous end, and at the midpoint (take $P_c$ to fall
        linearly).
  \item Split the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} into the filtering half and the
        reabsorbing half and estimate the fluid filtered and
        reabsorbed per day over the whole body, taking the net
        pressures averaged over each half as $+10$ and
        \qty{-8.5}{mmHg} and each half moving \qty{2}{L} a day per
        mmHg.
  \item Deduce the lymph flow.
  \item \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{Plasma} albumin falls to \qty{20}{g/L}. Recompute $\pi_c$
        (assume it scales with albumin) and the net pressures at the
        two ends. What happens?
  \item Venous pressure rises so that $P_c$ runs from 35 to
        \qty{28}{mmHg}. Recompute the average net pressure and the
        daily balance. Where does the fluid go?
  \item Compute the total \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} surface (cylinders, all of
        them) and the time a molecule takes to diffuse to a cell
        \qty{20}{\micro m} from a \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} ($D = \qty{1e-9}{m^2/s}$).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The aorta as a reservoir.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the systemic resistance from a mean pressure of
        \qty{93}{mmHg}, venous \qty{3}{mmHg} and the resting output,
        in \unit{mmHg.s/mL}.
  \item Compute the time constant $RC$ and the diastolic pressure
        after \qty{0.8}{s} from a systolic of \qty{120}{mmHg}.
  \item Recompute for $C = \qty{1}{mL/mmHg}$. What has happened to
        the \omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:windkessel}{pulse pressure}?
  \item The heart rate rises to \num{120} and diastole shortens to
        \qty{0.3}{s}. Compute the diastolic pressure.
  \item Of the \qty{70}{mL} ejected, how much is stored in the
        arteries during systole if the pressure rises by
        \qty{20}{mmHg}? Where does the rest go?
  \item Explain why the coronary arteries, which fill in diastole,
        depend on the aorta's recoil.
  \item State the result: the cardiac output, the arteriolar
        pressure drop, the day's net filtrate, and the diastolic
        pressure for $C = 2$ and $C = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
