\chapter{Endocrinology and Homeostasis}\label{ch:b3:endocrinology}

In the summer of 1921 two young men in a hot Toronto laboratory tied
off the pancreatic ducts of dogs, waited for the digestive tissue to
wither, ground up what was left, and injected the extract into a dog
made diabetic by removing its pancreas. Its blood sugar fell. By
January a fourteen-year-old boy dying of diabetes in Toronto General
Hospital was receiving the extract, and within weeks he was eating,
walking and gaining weight; he lived another thirteen years. The
substance, \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}, is a peptide of fifty-one amino acids secreted by
a per cent of the pancreas into the blood, where at a concentration of
a few hundred picomolar it tells every muscle and fat cell in the body
what to do with the sugar of the last meal. This chapter is about such
messengers: what they are, how a cell that has never met the gland
knows what the message means, how the glands are themselves governed
in a hierarchy of feedback loops, and how the whole system holds the
blood's glucose, salt, calcium and temperature within a few per cent
for a lifetime --- and what happens, in the clinic, when one of the
loops fails.

\section{Hormones and their receptors}

\begin{definition}[Hormone]\label{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}
\index{hormone}\index{endocrine gland}\index{paracrine}\index{steroid hormone}\index{peptide hormone}\index{nuclear receptor}
A \emph{hormone} is a substance released by a cell into the blood
that acts on distant cells carrying its receptor; a \emph{paracrine}
signal acts on neighbours, an \emph{autocrine} one on the cell that
made it, and a neurohormone is released by a neuron into the blood.
The classical \emph{endocrine glands} --- \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary}, thyroid,
parathyroids, adrenals, pancreatic islets, gonads --- have been joined
by the heart (natriuretic peptide), the kidney (erythropoietin), fat
(leptin), the gut (a dozen peptides) and bone. Chemically hormones are
of three kinds, and the chemistry fixes the mechanism. \emph{Peptide
and protein hormones} (\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}, growth hormone, the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary}
hormones) are made on ribosomes, stored in granules, released by
exocytosis, travel free in plasma, cannot cross membranes, and act on
receptors at the cell surface --- G-protein-coupled or kinase-linked ---
through second messengers, within seconds to minutes; their half-lives
are minutes. \emph{Steroids} (cortisol, \omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:controls}{aldosterone}, oestrogens,
testosterone, and the secosteroid \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:calcium}{vitamin D}) are made from cholesterol
as needed, not stored, travel bound to carrier proteins, cross
membranes, and bind \emph{nuclear receptors} that are themselves
transcription factors: their effects take hours and last days.
\emph{Amines} straddle the two: adrenaline acts at the surface within a
second; the \omterm{prop:b3:endocrinology:thyroid}{thyroid hormones}, though amino-acid derivatives, act on
nuclear receptors like steroids. A hormone means nothing in itself: the
same adrenaline dilates one vessel and constricts another because their
smooth muscles carry different receptor subtypes, and the same cortisol
raises blood glucose in the liver and suppresses a \omterm{def:b3:adaptive-immunity:lymphocytes}{lymphocyte} because
the two cells' \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} offers its receptor different genes.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Dose and response]\label{prop:b3:endocrinology:dose}
\index{dose--response curve}\index{spare receptors}\index{down-regulation}
A cell with $R$ receptors of dissociation constant $K_{d}$ in a
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} concentration $H$ has $R\,H/(K_{d} + H)$ of them occupied, and
the response usually rises with occupancy along a sigmoid on a
logarithmic dose axis, half-maximal at an $\text{EC}_{50}$ that is
often far below $K_{d}$: a few per cent occupancy gives a full
response, because the signal is amplified downstream (one receptor
activates many G proteins, one kinase phosphorylates many
substrates), and the surplus \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:endocrinology:dose}{spare receptors}} shift the curve
to the left and make the cell sensitive to low doses. The cell tunes
its own sensitivity: prolonged exposure to a \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} removes its
receptors from the surface (\emph{down-regulation}), the mechanism by
which a persistently high \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} blunts \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}'s own effect;
deprivation increases them. \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{Hormones} circulate at $10^{-12}$ to
$10^{-9}$ molar --- a thousand to a million times below most
metabolites --- which is why receptors must bind them with
nanomolar affinity, and why measuring them needed a new method.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Occupancy is the mass-action isotherm of a single binding site. If
the response saturates when $n \ll R$ receptors are occupied, then it
is half-maximal when $R H/(K_{d} + H) = n/2$, i.e.\ $H = K_{d}\,
(n/2)/(R - n/2) \approx K_{d}\, n/(2R) \ll K_{d}$: the EC$_{50}$ falls
in proportion to the excess of receptors. Losing receptors raises it,
which is down-regulation's effect on the curve.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Radioimmunoassay]\label{met:b3:endocrinology:ria}
\index{radioimmunoassay}\index{immunoassay}
To measure a \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} at picomolar concentration (Yalow and Berson,
1959): (1) raise an \omterm{def:b3:adaptive-immunity:antibody}{antibody} against it; (2) mix a fixed small amount
of \omterm{def:b3:adaptive-immunity:antibody}{antibody} with a fixed amount of radioactively labelled \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone},
and add the sample; (3) the unlabelled \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} of the sample competes
with the labelled one for the \omterm{def:b3:adaptive-immunity:antibody}{antibody}'s sites, so the more \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} the
sample holds, the less label is bound; (4) separate bound from free
label and count; (5) read the sample's concentration off a standard
curve made with known amounts. Its descendants replace the isotope
with an enzyme or a fluorophore and use two antibodies (a ``sandwich'')
to gain specificity; they measure every \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} in this chapter from a
drop of blood, and made endocrinology a quantitative science.
\end{method}

\section{The hierarchy: hypothalamus and pituitary}

\begin{definition}[The axes]\label{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}
\index{hypothalamus}\index{pituitary}\index{portal system}\index{releasing hormone}\index{tropic hormone}\index{negative feedback}
The \emph{hypothalamus}, a few grams of brain above the pituitary,
turns neural information --- stress, cold, day length, the blood's
\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:problem}{osmolarity} and glucose --- into hormonal commands. Its
neurosecretory cells release \emph{releasing \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormones}} (peptides:
CRH, TRH, GnRH, GHRH, and the inhibitor somatostatin; dopamine for
prolactin) into a private \emph{portal system} of vessels that
carries them, undiluted, a centimetre down to the \emph{anterior
pituitary}, whose cells respond with \emph{tropic \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormones}}: ACTH to
the adrenal cortex, TSH to the thyroid, LH and FSH to the gonads,
growth \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} to the liver and tissues, prolactin to the breast. The
target glands' \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormones} --- cortisol, thyroxine, sex steroids ---
feed back on both pituitary and hypothalamus to shut off their own
commands: \emph{negative feedback}, in three tiers. The
\emph{posterior pituitary} is not a gland but the axon terminals of
hypothalamic neurons, releasing vasopressin and oxytocin straight into
the blood. Each axis has its own dynamics: the thyroid axis is slow
and steady, holding a \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{set point} for years; the adrenal axis is
pulsatile and circadian, cortisol peaking before dawn and secreted in
hourly bursts; the gonadal axis of a woman runs a monthly cycle on a
positive feedback that the others lack.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Berthold (1849) castrated cockerels, which lost their comb, crow and
combativeness; re-implanting a testis anywhere in the abdomen, without
its nerves, restored them --- the organ acted through the blood. Harris
(1950s) cut the portal vessels between \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{hypothalamus} and \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} in
rats: the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary}, its blood supply restored from elsewhere, stopped
responding to stress and the ovaries stopped cycling; transplanting a
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} under the kidney left it inert, but under the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{hypothalamus},
where portal vessels regrew into it, it worked --- the brain governs the
gland by blood-borne factors, and Guillemin and Schally then isolated
the first of them, TRH, from millions of sheep and pig hypothalami
(1969): three amino acids.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Feedback and the log-linear thyroid]\label{prop:b3:endocrinology:thyroid}
\index{thyroid hormone}\index{TSH}\index{hypothyroidism}\index{hyperthyroidism}\index{goitre}
The thyroid takes up iodide, and makes thyroxine T$_{4}$ (four
iodines), which the tissues convert to the active T$_{3}$; both raise
the metabolic rate of nearly every cell, set the heart rate, and are
needed for brain development before and after birth. TSH from the
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} drives both the synthesis and the growth of the gland; T$_{4}$
suppresses TSH. The relation at steady state is \emph{log-linear}: the
logarithm of TSH falls in proportion to the free T$_{4}$,
\[
\text{TSH} = \text{TSH}_{0}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa\,(T_{4} - T_{4,0})},
\]
so that a fall of free T$_{4}$ by a third raises TSH about tenfold ---
which is why TSH, and not T$_{4}$, is the sensitive test: a gland that is
beginning to fail shows a normal T$_{4}$ held up by a TSH already several
times normal. Failure of the gland (autoimmune destruction, iodine
deficiency) gives \emph{hypothyroidism} --- cold, slow, tired, with a
high TSH and, in iodine deficiency, a gland enlarged into a
\emph{goitre} by the TSH that cannot make it produce; an \omterm{def:b3:adaptive-immunity:antibody}{antibody} that
mimics TSH gives \emph{hyperthyroidism} (Graves' disease) with a hot,
fast, thin patient and a TSH suppressed to nothing, since the
stimulation escapes the feedback. A newborn without \omterm{prop:b3:endocrinology:thyroid}{thyroid hormone}
develops irreversible intellectual disability within months, which is
why every newborn's TSH is measured on a spot of blood.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} cell's TSH output responds to the receptor occupancy by
T$_{3}$, itself proportional to T$_{4}$, and the response of a
transcriptional repression to a change in occupancy is multiplicative
--- each increment of \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} represses the same fraction of what
remains --- so $\mathrm{d}\ln\text{TSH}/\mathrm{d}T_{4} = -\kappa$,
whence the exponential. With $\kappa$ fitted so that $T_{4}$ falling
from $15$ to \qty{10}{pmol/L} raises TSH from $1.5$ to \qty{15}{mU/L},
$\kappa = \ln 10/5 = \qty{0.46}{L/pmol}$: TSH doubles for each
\qty{1.5}{pmol/L} of T$_{4}$ lost, which is the amplification that makes
it the sensitive test.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Cortisol: the stress axis]\label{ex:b3:endocrinology:cortisol}
Cortisol, from the adrenal cortex under ACTH, mobilises fuel (glucose
from the liver, amino acids from muscle, fatty acids from fat),
sensitises vessels to adrenaline, and suppresses \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammation} and the
immune response --- the reason its synthetic relatives are the
commonest anti-inflammatory drugs. Its rhythm is set by the clock:
\qty{500}{nmol/L} at 8 a.m., a fifth of that at midnight, and pulses
every hour or two throughout, since the hypothalamic CRH neurons fire
in bursts. Stress --- haemorrhage, infection, surgery, fear --- raises it
tenfold within minutes, overriding the feedback. Too little (Addison's
disease, destruction of the adrenal cortex) gives weakness, low blood
pressure, low glucose, and, under stress, collapse and death unless
replaced; too much (Cushing's syndrome: a \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumour} making ACTH,
an adrenal \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumour}, or, most often, prescribed steroids) gives the round
face, thin skin, wasted muscle, high glucose, high pressure and brittle
bones of prolonged exposure. Stopping a long course of steroids
abruptly is dangerous for the reason the axis predicts: the suppressed
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{hypothalamus} and \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} take weeks to recover, and the adrenal,
unstimulated, has shrunk.
\end{example}

\section{Glucose: the tightest loop}

\begin{definition}[Insulin and glucagon]\label{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}
\index{insulin}\index{glucagon}\index{islet of Langerhans}\index{GLUT4}\index{diabetes mellitus}
The blood holds about \qty{5}{g} of glucose, at \qty{5}{mmol/L}
(\qty{90}{mg/dL}), and the brain burns \qty{120}{g} a day and can burn
nothing else; a meal delivers \qty{75}{g} in an hour. The
\emph{islets of Langerhans}, a million clusters of a few thousand cells
scattered through the pancreas, hold the loop. $\beta$ cells sense
glucose by metabolising it: more glucose, more ATP, closure of an
ATP-sensitive potassium channel, depolarisation, calcium entry and
exocytosis of \emph{insulin}, a \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{peptide hormone} cleaved from a single
precursor. Insulin tells muscle and fat to move the transporter
\emph{GLUT4} to their surfaces and take glucose up, the liver to store
glucose as glycogen and stop making it, and fat to stop releasing fatty
acids: it is the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} of the fed state, the only one that lowers
blood glucose. $\alpha$ cells release \emph{glucagon} when glucose
falls: the liver breaks down glycogen and makes new glucose from amino
acids and lactate. Adrenaline, cortisol and growth \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} raise
glucose too, and the redundancy is not symmetric: a body can defend
against a fall by four routes and against a rise by one. \emph{Diabetes
mellitus} is the failure of that one: type 1, the autoimmune
destruction of the $\beta$ cells, needs insulin from the first day;
type 2, nine cases in ten, begins as resistance of the tissues to
insulin, met for years by more insulin, until the $\beta$ cells fail to
keep up and glucose rises.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The insulin--glucose loop]\label{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}
\index{loop gain}\index{set point}\index{damped oscillation}
Write $g$ and $i$ for the deviations of plasma glucose and \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} from
their fasting values. Glucose is cleared at a rate proportional to its
own excess (the glucose effectiveness $a$) and to the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} excess
(sensitivity $s$); \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} is secreted in proportion to the glucose
excess ($\beta$) and cleared at rate $\gamma$; a load $u$ enters:
\[
\dot g = -a\,g - s\,i + u, \qquad \dot i = \beta\,g - \gamma\,i .
\]
Under a steady load $u$ the glucose settles at
\[
g^{*} = \frac{u}{a}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + L}, \qquad L = \frac{s\beta}{a\gamma},
\]
so the feedback divides the disturbance an unregulated body would
suffer by $1 + L$, the \emph{\omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{loop gain}} plus one. After a bolus the
return is governed by the eigenvalues
$\lambda = -\tfrac{a + \gamma}{2} \pm \sqrt{\tfrac{(a+\gamma)^{2}}{4} - (a\gamma + s\beta)}$:
monotone if $s\beta < (a - \gamma)^{2}/4$, and otherwise a \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{damped
oscillation} of angular frequency $\omega = \sqrt{a\gamma + s\beta -
(a+\gamma)^{2}/4}$ decaying as $\mathrm{e}^{-(a+\gamma)t/2}$ --- glucose
undershoots its fasting value before settling, which is the mild
hypoglycaemia two or three hours after a sugary meal. \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{Insulin}
resistance lowers $s$: the fasting steady state under the body's own
hepatic output rises, and the loop compensates with a higher $i^{*}$
until the $\beta$ cells' $\beta$ falls too, when the compensation fails.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Steady state: $\dot i = 0$ gives $i^{*} = \beta g^{*}/\gamma$; putting
it in $\dot g = 0$: $a g^{*} + s\beta g^{*}/\gamma = u$, whence $g^{*} =
u/(a + s\beta/\gamma) = (u/a)/(1 + L)$. Dynamics: the system matrix is
$\begin{pmatrix} -a & -s\\ \beta & -\gamma\end{pmatrix}$, with trace
$-(a+\gamma)$ and determinant $a\gamma + s\beta$; its characteristic
equation $\lambda^{2} + (a+\gamma)\lambda + (a\gamma + s\beta) = 0$ has
the roots stated. Both have negative real part, so the fasting state
is stable; the roots are complex when the discriminant $(a+\gamma)^{2}
- 4(a\gamma + s\beta) = (a-\gamma)^{2} - 4s\beta$ is negative, the
condition given, and then $g(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-(a+\gamma)t/2}(A\cos\omega t
+ B\sin\omega t)$, which crosses zero. Resistance: with $u$ the liver's
basal output and $s$ reduced, $L$ falls and $g^{*}$ rises for the same
$u$; $i^{*} = \beta g^{*}/\gamma$ rises with it (hyperinsulinaemia);
if $\beta$ then falls, $L$ falls further and $g^{*}$ climbs toward $u/a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Von Mering and Minkowski (1889) removed a dog's pancreas and produced
diabetes --- the flies gathering on its urine gave the sugar away; tying
the ducts, which destroyed the digestive tissue but spared the islets,
did not. Banting and Best (1921), with Collip's purification, extracted
the islet principle and lowered a diabetic dog's blood sugar, then
Leonard Thompson's (January 1922). Sanger sequenced \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} (1955),
the first protein sequence; Yalow and Berson measured it in blood
(1959) and found that adult-onset diabetics had, at first, more of it
than the healthy --- resistance, not deficiency. Genentech's bacteria
made human \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} in 1978, the first recombinant drug.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Reading the glucose loop in a patient]\label{met:b3:endocrinology:ogtt}
\index{glucose tolerance test}\index{HbA1c}
(1) Fasting plasma glucose: normal below \qty{5.6}{mmol/L}, diabetes at
or above \qty{7.0}{mmol/L} on two occasions. (2) Oral \omterm{met:b3:endocrinology:ogtt}{glucose tolerance
test}: \qty{75}{g} of glucose drunk; plasma glucose at two hours normal
below \qty{7.8}{mmol/L}, diabetes at or above \qty{11.1}{mmol/L} ---
the two-hour value reads the loop's gain, the fasting value its \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{set
point}. (3) Glycated haemoglobin, \emph{HbA1c}: glucose attaches
irreversibly to haemoglobin at a rate proportional to its
concentration, and red cells live 120 days, so the fraction glycated
integrates the mean glucose of the past three months without a fast
--- normal below \qty{5.7}{\%}, diabetes from \qty{6.5}{\%}. (4) To
separate resistance from deficiency, measure \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} (or its
by-product C-peptide) alongside: high \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} with high glucose is
resistance; absent C-peptide is type~1.
\end{method}

\section{Calcium, and the clock}

\begin{definition}[Calcium homeostasis]\label{def:b3:endocrinology:calcium}
\index{parathyroid hormone}\index{calcitriol}\index{vitamin D}\index{osteoporosis}
Ionised calcium in plasma is held at \qty{1.2}{mmol/L} within a few per
cent, because it sets the excitability of every nerve and muscle: too
low and they fire spontaneously (tetany), too high and they are
sluggish. The four parathyroid glands sense it with a surface receptor
and secrete \emph{parathyroid \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone}} (PTH) along a steep inverse
sigmoid: PTH mobilises calcium from bone, makes the kidney reabsorb it
and excrete phosphate, and activates \emph{vitamin D} --- made in skin
by ultraviolet light or eaten, hydroxylated in liver, then in kidney
to \emph{calcitriol} --- which makes the gut absorb calcium. Bone is
the reservoir, a kilogram of calcium, remodelled continuously by
osteoclasts and osteoblasts under PTH, calcitriol, oestrogen and load.
Deficiency of vitamin D gives rickets in children and soft bones in
adults; the fall of oestrogen at the menopause tilts remodelling toward
resorption and gives \emph{osteoporosis}; a parathyroid adenoma raises
calcium, dissolves bone and forms kidney stones --- ``bones, stones,
groans and moans.''
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The circadian clock]\label{def:b3:endocrinology:clock}
\index{circadian clock}\index{suprachiasmatic nucleus}\index{clock genes}\index{melatonin}\index{zeitgeber}
Nearly every cell carries a \emph{circadian clock}: a
transcription--translation loop in which the proteins CLOCK and BMAL1
switch on the genes \emph{Per} and \emph{Cry}, whose proteins
accumulate, enter the nucleus after a delay of hours and repress their
own transcription, then are degraded, releasing the repression --- one
cycle in about twenty-four hours, set by the rates of the delay and
degradation. The clocks of the body are synchronised by a master clock
of twenty thousand neurons in the \emph{suprachiasmatic nucleus} of the
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{hypothalamus}, which is itself set by light through a dedicated class
of retinal \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{ganglion cells} (containing melanopsin, not the rods' or
\omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{cones}' pigments), and which signals night to the body through the
pineal's \emph{melatonin}, secreted only in darkness. The clock
schedules cortisol before waking, body temperature lowest at 4 a.m.,
growth \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} in the first sleep, \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} sensitivity higher in the
morning; food, exercise and temperature are secondary
\emph{zeitgebers} that can pull the peripheral clocks away from the
central one, which is the malaise of the shift worker and of jet lag ---
a liver clock on one time and a brain on another.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Konopka and Benzer (1971) screened mutant flies for altered rhythms of
emergence and found three alleles of one gene, \emph{period}: a short
day (19 h), a long day (29 h), and no rhythm at all --- a clock with a
genetic period. Hardin, Hall and Rosbash (1990) found that
\emph{period} mRNA and protein oscillate with a lag between them, and
that the protein represses its own gene: the loop. Ralph and Menaker
(1990) transplanted the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:clock}{suprachiasmatic nucleus} of a mutant hamster with
a 20-hour rhythm into a normal hamster whose own nucleus had been
destroyed: the recipient ran on 20 hours --- the period belonged to the
transplanted tissue. In humans kept in a bunker without time cues the
rhythm free-ran at a little over 24 hours; a bright light in the
evening delayed it, in the morning advanced it.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Homeostasis as control]\label{rem:b3:endocrinology:control}
Every loop in this chapter has the same parts: a sensor (the
$\beta$ cell's metabolism, the parathyroid's calcium receptor, the
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary}'s \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{thyroid-hormone} receptor), a controller that compares with
a \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{set point}, an effector \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone} with its own half-life, and a
feedback that reduces the error by a factor $1 + L$ but never to zero.
What differs is the timing --- seconds for adrenaline, an hour for
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}, days for thyroxine, a lifetime for bone --- and the price of
the compromise: a fast loop with a delayed effector oscillates, a slow
loop lets a disturbance persist. The clinic sees the loops from
outside, through the pairs the feedback couples: a high TSH with a low
T$_{4}$ is a failed gland, a high TSH with a high T$_{4}$ a failed
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary}; a high \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} with a high glucose is resistance, a low
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} with a high glucose is destruction. To read a \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone}'s level
one must always ask what its commander was doing.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Sugar in the Blood}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a \omterm{met:b3:endocrinology:ogtt}{glucose tolerance test} read through
the linear insulin--glucose loop: the distribution of a \qty{75}{g} dose,
the \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{loop gain} and the damped return, what resistance and $\beta$-cell
failure do to the fasting \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{set point}, a diagnosis from the numbers, and
the thyroid axis beside it, ending on the \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{loop gain}, the return time
and the fasting glucose of the failing loop}]
\label{pb:b3:endocrinology:1}
Data: a healthy adult, glucose distribution volume \qty{15}{L},
fasting glucose \qty{90}{mg/dL} (\qty{5}{mmol/L}), fasting \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}
\qty{10}{mU/L}. Loop parameters: $a = \qty{0.02}{min^{-1}}$, $\gamma =
\qty{0.1}{min^{-1}}$, $\beta = 0.05$ (mU/L per min per mg/dL), $s = 0.2$
(mg/dL per min per mU/L). Basal hepatic glucose output $u_{0} =
\qty{2}{mg/dL/min}$ (already balanced at the fasting state by the
fasting disposal). An oral dose of \qty{75}{g} is absorbed over an
hour. Thyroid: $\text{TSH} = 1.5\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.46(T_{4} - 15)}$ mU/L.
Glucose molar mass \qty{180}{g/mol}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The dose.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Express the fasting glucose in mmol/L and check the conversion
        from mg/dL. How many grams of glucose are in the distribution
        volume at fasting?
  \item If the whole \qty{75}{g} appeared at once in \qty{15}{L}, by how
        much would glucose rise (mg/dL and mmol/L)? Why does the real
        peak, about \qty{60}{mg/dL} above fasting, fall so far short?
  \item Absorbed over \qty{60}{min}, the dose enters at what rate in
        mg/dL per minute? Compare with $u_{0}$.
  \item Without any \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} response ($s = 0$), glucose would obey $\dot
        g = -a g + u$. Steady excess under the absorption rate, and the
        time constant of the approach. Where would glucose be after an
        hour?
  \item With the loop, compute $L$ and the steady excess under the same
        rate. Compare with question~4.
  \item Explain why the brain is protected on both sides: what it needs,
        and what too much glucose does to tissues over years.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The return.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Write the characteristic equation of the loop and compute the
        trace, the determinant and the discriminant.
  \item Compute $\omega$ and the period of the \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{damped oscillation}, and
        the decay time $2/(a + \gamma)$.
  \item Starting from an excess of \qty{100}{mg/dL} with \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} at its
        fasting value, the solution is $g(t) = 100\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.06t}
        (\cos\omega t + c\sin\omega t)$. Find $c$ from $\dot g(0) = -a\cdot
        100$.
  \item When does glucose first return to fasting (first zero of $g$)?
        Estimate the undershoot at the first minimum.
  \item \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{Insulin}: $i(t)$ has the same exponential and frequency. Argue
        from $\dot i = \beta g - \gamma i$ that its peak comes after
        glucose has started falling and before glucose reaches fasting.
  \item Why does a real tolerance test, with absorption over an hour
        rather than a bolus, show a peak at 30--60 minutes and a return
        by two hours, and only a mild dip at three?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- When the loop fails.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{Insulin} resistance halves $s$. New $L$? The liver's $u_{0}$ is
        now met by a new fasting steady state: using $g^{*} = (u_{0}/a)/(1
        + L)$ as the excess over an idealised unregulated baseline, find
        by how much the fasting glucose rises relative to the healthy
        state (compute both $g^{*}$ values and subtract).
  \item Fasting \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} in the resistant state: compute $i^{*} = \beta
        g^{*}/\gamma$ for both states and the ratio. What does the
        clinic call this?
  \item Now the $\beta$ cells fail: $\beta$ falls to a fifth as well.
        New $L$, new fasting excess. Convert to mmol/L and compare with
        the diabetic threshold of \qty{7}{mmol/L}.
  \item Is the return still oscillatory in the state of question~15?
        Compute the discriminant and the time constant of the slowest
        mode.
  \item A patient has fasting glucose \qty{8}{mmol/L}, fasting \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}
        \qty{30}{mU/L}, two-hour value \qty{13}{mmol/L}. Diagnose type
        and mechanism from the pairs.
  \item Another has fasting glucose \qty{15}{mmol/L}, C-peptide
        undetectable, and lost \qty{8}{kg} in two months. Diagnose, and
        explain the weight loss in terms of what the tissues do without
        \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}.
  \item HbA1c: if the mean glucose of the first patient is
        \qty{9}{mmol/L}, and \qty{5.5}{mmol/L} gives \qty{5}{\%}, estimate
        their HbA1c assuming proportionality.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The gland next door.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
