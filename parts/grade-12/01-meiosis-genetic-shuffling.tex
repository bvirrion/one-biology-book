\chapter{Meiosis and Genetic Shuffling}\label{ch:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling}

Two parents, each with 46 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, produce a child with 46 --- not
92. Somewhere between the parents' bodies and the child, the count is
halved, and it is halved in a way that deals each child a hand no
sibling has ever held: the same parents, forty years of children, and
no two alike except identical twins. The halving is a special division
called \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis}; the dealing is what it does with the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} on the
way. This chapter follows one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} through \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis}, counts the
combinations it can produce, and reads the results of crosses that
reveal, from the outside, what happened inside.

\section{Halving the set}

\begin{definition}[Diploid, haploid, meiosis]\label{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}
A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} carrying two copies of each \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} --- one from each parent,
the two forming a pair of \emph{homologous chromosomes} --- is
\emph{diploid}\index{diploid}, written $2n$ ($2n = 46$ in humans). A
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} carrying one copy of each is \emph{haploid}\index{haploid},
written $n$. \emph{Meiosis}\index{meiosis} is the sequence of two
divisions by which one diploid \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, after a single replication of its
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, produces four haploid \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} --- the gametes, or their precursors.
\emph{Fertilisation} unites two haploid gametes and restores the
diploid number: the alternation of meiosis and fertilisation is the
cycle of every sexually reproducing species.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The two divisions]\label{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:divisions}
Before \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is replicated: each \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} has two
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatids}. Then:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{First division} (\omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} I, \emph{reductional}): the
        homologous chromosomes pair up, two by two, on the equator;
        the members of each pair are pulled to opposite poles. Each
        daughter \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} receives \emph{one \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} of each pair}, still
        made of two \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatids}: $n$ \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} amount $q$ (the
        amount of a \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{diploid} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} before replication).
  \item \emph{Second division} (\omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} II, \emph{equational}): like a
        \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:cycle}{mitosis}, the \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatids} of each \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} separate. Each of
        the four \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} receives $n$ \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{single-chromatid} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes},
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} amount $q/2$.
\end{itemize}
The first division halves the number of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}; the second
halves the amount of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} back to one \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatid} per \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Human numbers]\label{ex:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:numbers}
A germ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} of the testis, $2n = 46$, replicates to 46 \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{two-chromatid}
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} ($2q$); after \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} I, two \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} of 23 \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{two-chromatid}
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} ($q$); after \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} II, four sperm \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} of 23
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{single-chromatid} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} ($q/2$). At fertilisation, $23 + 23 =
46$ and $q/2 + q/2 = q$: the zygote is back to a \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{diploid} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} at rest.
In the ovary, the same divisions produce one large egg \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} and three
tiny \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that are discarded; the arithmetic is identical.
\end{example}

\section{The shuffle}

\begin{proposition}[Independent assortment]\label{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:assortment}
At metaphase I, each pair of homologues lines up on the equator
independently of the other pairs: which member of a pair goes to which
pole is decided by chance, pair by pair. A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} with $n$ pairs can
therefore produce $2^n$ different combinations of paternal and maternal
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} in its gametes --- $2^{23} \approx \num{8.4e6}$ in a human.
Fertilisation, uniting two such gametes at random, yields $2^{23}
\times 2^{23} \approx \num{7e13}$ combinations of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} for one
couple's children, before any other source of variety is counted.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{proposition}[Crossing-over]\label{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:crossingover}
While the homologues are paired, early in \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} I, two non-sister
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatids} break at the same point and rejoin crosswise: a
\emph{crossing-over}\index{crossing-over}. Each \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatid} beyond the
exchange point now carries the other homologue's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}. The
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} that reach the gametes are therefore not the parents'
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} but \emph{recombined} ones, mixing along their length the
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} received from the two grandparents. One to three exchanges
occur on every pair at every \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis}, at variable positions: the
number of possible gametes is, in practice, unlimited.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Under the microscope, paired homologues in \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} I show crossed
points, \emph{chiasmata}, where two \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatids} visibly swap partners.
Crosses in which two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} lie on the same \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} give, among the
offspring, a minority carrying new combinations of the two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}'
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} --- combinations neither parent carried --- in a proportion
that depends on the distance between the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}; \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} far apart on a
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} recombine as often as \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} on different \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}.
Molecular markers followed through families show the segments of each
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} switching between grandparental origins at one or two
points per \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} per generation.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Three sources of variety, in order]\label{ex:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:sources}
A child of two parents is new in three ways: \omterm{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:crossingover}{crossing-over} rebuilt each
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} of each gamete from the two grandparental copies;
independent assortment dealt one recombined \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} of each pair
into the gamete, among $2^{23}$ deals; fertilisation joined one such
gamete to another drawn from the other parent's $2^{23}$. \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{Mutation},
which created the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} being shuffled, acts on a far slower
timescale: the shuffle is what makes every generation new from the
same deck.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[The test cross]\label{met:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:testcross}
To see what gametes an individual makes, cross it with a partner
carrying only \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} (a \emph{test cross}): each offspring
then shows exactly the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} the tested parent's gamete carried.
For two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, with the tested parent $AaBb$ and the partner $aabb$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Count the four kinds of offspring: $AB$, $Ab$, $aB$, $ab$
        (each also carrying $ab$ from the partner).
  \item If the four are equally frequent ($1:1:1:1$), the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} lie on
        different \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} and assort independently.
  \item If two kinds (the parental combinations) are much commoner than
        the other two (the recombinants), the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} lie on the same
        \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}; the recombinants come from \omterm{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:crossingover}{crossing-over}, and
        their proportion measures the distance between the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}.
  \item Recombinants are always the minority and always come in two
        equal classes; parentals likewise.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Two crosses in the fruit fly]\label{ex:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:fly}
Fly A, heterozygous for body colour and wing shape, test-crossed: 254
grey-normal, 248 black-vestigial, 251 grey-vestigial, 247
black-normal --- four equal classes, the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} are on different
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}. Fly B, heterozygous for body colour and eye colour:
412 grey-red, 405 black-purple, 44 grey-purple, 39 black-red --- two
parental classes of about 45\% each and two recombinant classes of
about 5\%: the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} are on the same \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}, close together,
separated by a \omterm{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:crossingover}{crossing-over} in 9\% of meioses.
\end{example}

\section{When the shuffle goes wrong}

\begin{proposition}[Errors of meiosis]\label{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:errors}
Occasionally a pair of homologues fails to separate at \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} I, or
two \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatids} at \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} II: one gamete receives two copies of a
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} and another none. Fertilised, they give a zygote with three
copies (\emph{trisomy}) or one (\emph{monosomy}). Most such zygotes
do not develop; those that do carry a set of characteristic
features. Trisomy 21 (about one birth in 700) causes intellectual
disability and heart defects and is the commonest; its frequency
rises steeply with the mother's age. An unequal \omterm{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:crossingover}{crossing-over} ---
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromatids} exchanging unequal segments --- produces a \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} with
a duplicated \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} and one with a deletion: the mechanism by which the
\omterm{def:g11:the-eye:photoreceptors}{opsin} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} of \cref{ch:g11:the-eye} were multiplied, and by which
new \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} arise (\cref{ch:g12:diversification-of-life}).
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Sex chromosomes miscounted]\label{ex:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:sexchrom}
A gamete with no sex \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} fertilised by an X-bearing one gives
an XO zygote: a girl of short stature whose ovaries do not develop.
An XX egg fertilised by a Y sperm gives XXY: a boy whose testes stay
small and make no sperm. In both, \omterm{prop:g11:becoming-male-female:sry}{SRY} decides the gonad as in
\cref{ch:g11:becoming-male-female}; the number of X \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}
decides much of the rest, which is why these are the mildest of the
chromosomal errors.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Why sex]\label{rem:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:whysex}
A bacterium copies itself; a rose can grow from a cutting. Sexual
reproduction is costlier --- two parents, \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis}, the search for a
mate --- and its product is a set of offspring each different from
its parents and from each other. That difference is the point: in an
environment that changes, and against parasites that evolve, a
population of varied individuals is likelier to contain some that
survive than a population of copies. The shuffle of this chapter is
the raw material that the next two chapters' selection will sort.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Geneticist's Flies}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- two genes followed through
meiosis and a test cross: the gametes counted, linkage decided, the
recombination measured, and the number of hands a couple can deal}]
\label{pb:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:1}
In the fruit fly, the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} $b$ (black body) is \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive} to $b^+$
(grey), and $vg$ (vestigial wings) \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive} to $vg^+$ (normal). A
grey, normal-winged female whose parents were pure grey-normal and
pure black-vestigial is crossed with a black, vestigial male. Offspring:
grey-normal 965, black-vestigial 944, grey-vestigial 206, black-normal
185.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The cross.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Give the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} of the female and of the male, \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} by \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}.
  \item What gametes does the male produce? Why does that make the
        cross a test cross?
  \item List the four gametes the female could produce, and the
        offspring each gives.
  \item Compute the percentage of each class among the 2300 offspring.
  \item Are the two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} on the same \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} or on different ones?
        Justify from the proportions.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Inside the female's \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Which two classes are the parental combinations, and where do
        they come from in the female's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}?
  \item Which two are recombinant? By what event of \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} I did they
        arise?
  \item Compute the recombination frequency (recombinants over the
        total). What fraction of the female's meioses had a
        \omterm{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:crossingover}{crossing-over} between the two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}?
  \item Explain why the two recombinant classes are nearly equal, and
        why the two parental classes are.
  \item The same two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, in a female whose parents were pure
        grey-vestigial and pure black-normal: predict the four classes
        and their frequencies.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- A third \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}.}
A third \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, $cn$ (cinnabar eyes), is test-crossed with $b$ in another
female: recombinants 9\%. Test-crossed with $vg$: recombinants 8\%.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Is $cn$ on the same \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} as $b$ and $vg$?
  \item Using the three recombination frequencies (17\% for $b$--$vg$,
        9\% for $b$--$cn$, 8\% for $cn$--$vg$), place the three \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}
        in order along the \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}.
  \item Explain why the frequencies add up (nearly), and what this
        says about the relation between recombination and distance.
  \item Two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} at opposite ends of a long \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} give 50\%
        recombinants. Explain why the frequency cannot exceed 50\%.
  \item A fourth \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} gives 50\% recombinants with all three. What can
        you conclude, and what can you not?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The size of the deck.}
The fruit fly has $2n = 8$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} combinations can a fly's gametes show by
        independent assortment alone? And a fly couple's offspring?
  \item With one \omterm{prop:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:crossingover}{crossing-over} at a variable position on each pair,
        explain why the number of distinct gametes becomes practically
        unlimited.
  \item For a human couple, compute the number of \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}
        combinations of their children by assortment alone, and
        compare with the number of humans who have ever lived (about
        $10^{11}$).
  \item Explain why two siblings are nevertheless more alike than two
        strangers, in terms of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} they can have received.
  \item State the result: the recombination frequency between $b$ and
        $vg$, what it measures, and the two mechanisms of \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} that
        make every gamete of the female different.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
