\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b1:classifying-biodiversity} --- Classifying the Living World}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:1}
\emph{Felis catus}: \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{genus} \emph{Felis}, epithet \emph{catus}; family
Felidae, order Carnivora, class Mammalia, phylum Chordata.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:2}
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{Homology}: similarity by descent from a common ancestor (the forelimb
bones of a bat and a human). \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{Homoplasy}: similarity by convergence or
reversal (the wings of a bat and a bird). Only shared derived
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homologies} are evidence for a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clade}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:3}
"Fish": paraphyletic (excludes the tetrapods that descend from
fish). Birds: monophyletic (all descendants of one feathered
ancestor). "Warm-blooded animals": polyphyletic (birds and mammals
got endothermy separately).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:4}
Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya. Archaea have ether-linked membrane
\omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{lipids} and no \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{peptidoglycan}; their \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:transcription}{RNA polymerase} and ribosomal
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} resemble the eukaryotes'.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:5}
Bacteria do not interbreed --- they divide, and exchange \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes} across
what would be \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} boundaries --- and fossils cannot be tested for
interbreeding. Bacteria are delimited by sequence similarity (a
threshold on \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{ribosomal RNA} or \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genomes}); fossils by morphology.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:6}
Tree (X,Y),Z: characters 1, 3, 4 one change each, character 2 two
changes: length 5. Tree (Y,Z),X: character 2 one change, characters
1, 3, 4 two each: length 7. (X,Y) is preferred; character 2 is the
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homoplasy}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:7}
The \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup} fixes the polarity of every character: its state is
taken as ancestral, so that only the derived states count as
evidence. An \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup} that is really inside the group makes some
derived states look ancestral and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} the tree in the wrong place,
turning \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clades} into paraphyletic groups.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:8}
Ribosomal and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} sequences place animals and fungi as sister
lineages, both within the opisthokonts, whose swimming \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}
(sperm, chytrid zoospores) have a single posterior flagellum; plants
lie in a separate lineage defined by the primary chloroplast.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:9}
A kingdom within the bacteria would imply the methanogens are closer
to other bacteria than to eukaryotes; the sequences showed three
lineages equally distant from one another, so the prokaryotes were
not one group and the deepest division of life was threefold.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:10}
Host-specific beetles per tree \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}: $0.2\times 1200 = 240$;
canopy insects specific to the tree: $240/0.4 = 600$; whole tree:
$600/(2/3) = 900$; times \num{50000} trees: \num{45} million.
Assumptions: the fogged tree is typical, the specificity is real
and not sampling error, beetles are \qty{40}{\%} of insects on every
tree, and \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} specific to one tree are not counted twice ---
each is uncertain by a factor of two or more, and later estimates
have fallen toward \num{5} million.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:11}
The \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{type specimen} is the object the name refers to: the living
\omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} are the same \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} if they are judged conspecific with
the type, and a new \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} if not; in the second case the old name
stays with the type and the new \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} need a new name. Priority
guarantees that if two names are found to refer to one \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}, the
older is kept, so that names do not multiply or drift with each new
opinion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:12}
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:parsimony}{Parsimony} assumes only that convergences and reversals are rarer
than shared descent, so that a tree should not be made to require
more of them than the data force. It fails when convergence is common
(similar \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{habitats}), when characters are correlated (one adaptation
counted as many), and when fast-evolving lineages accumulate chance
similarities and are drawn together ("long branches attract").
Molecular sequences supply many independent characters and models of
substitution to test the same tree; fossils supply the order of
appearance of the derived states and dates for the splits; agreement
among independent data is the test.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b1:classifying-biodiversity:1}
\textbf{1.} Character 1, jaws: shared by all but the lamprey, so it
defines the ingroup and confirms the lamprey as \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup}.
\textbf{2.} 1 (7 taxa: jawed vertebrates), 2 (6: bony vertebrates), 3
(5: lungfish and tetrapods), 4 (4: tetrapods), 5 (3: amniotes), 8, 9
and 10 (2 each: lizard+pigeon, mouse+pigeon, lizard+pigeon), 6 and 7
(1 each).
\textbf{3.} Hair (mouse) and feathers (pigeon): unique states,
autapomorphies, which diagnose the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{taxon} but say nothing about its
relatives.
\textbf{4.} Character 9 (endothermy: mouse+pigeon) conflicts with 8
and 10 (lizard+pigeon); the conflict is among the three amniotes.
\textbf{5.} Absence of \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lungs} is ancestral (the lamprey, shark and
trout lack them), presence derived; the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup}'s state is taken as
ancestral.
\textbf{6.} Three: any one of the three can be the odd one out ---
(lizard,pigeon) with mouse outside, (mouse,pigeon) with lizard
outside, (lizard,mouse) with pigeon outside. Only characters 8, 9
and 10 differ among the three, so only they bear on the choice.
\textbf{7.} (lamprey,(shark,(trout,(lungfish,(frog,(lizard, mouse,
pigeon)))))), each node marked by one character.
\textbf{8.} Tree A: (mouse,(lizard,pigeon)); tree B: (lizard,(mouse,
pigeon)).
\textbf{9.} Tree A: characters 1--8 and 10 one change each (9), character
9 two changes: length \num{11}.
\textbf{10.} Tree B: characters 1--7 and 9 one each (8), characters 8 and
10 two each: length \num{12}.
\textbf{11.} Tree A, by one step.
\textbf{12.} Endothermy: it arose twice, once in the mammal line and
once in the bird line --- a convergence, consistent with their
different mechanisms (fur and sweat versus feathers and air sacs).
\textbf{13.} Without 8 and 10: tree A length 9 (endothermy twice), tree
B length 8 (all consistent): B would be preferred. The conclusion
depends on the characters chosen; with few characters one \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homoplasy}
can tip the result, which is why many characters, and molecular
ones, are needed.
\textbf{14.} No: their common ancestor is also the ancestor of the
tetrapods, so all four tetrapods would have to be added.
\textbf{15.} Yes: the amniotic egg.
\textbf{16.} No: the pigeon descends from within the reptiles (it is
sister to the lizard here, and to the crocodiles in fuller trees), so
reptiles without birds are paraphyletic.
\textbf{17.} No: their nearest common ancestor is the ancestor of all
amniotes, which was not endothermic; polyphyletic, grouped by a
convergent character.
\textbf{18.} Closer to the frog: it shares \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lungs} (character 3) with the
tetrapods, which the trout lacks; "fish" without the tetrapods is
paraphyletic.
\textbf{19.} Class Reptilia, class Mammalia, class Aves: three classes
of equal rank, though the lizard--pigeon split is far more recent
than the split of either from the mammals; ranks are conventions, and
equal rank does not mean equal age or equal distinctness.
\textbf{20.} Mammals: \num{20} new a year on \num{6500} (\qty{0.3}{\%});
fishes: \num{300} on \num{35000} (\qty{0.9}{\%}) --- the mammal
inventory is nearer completion, the fish inventory still growing
fast.
\textbf{21.} Nine tenths of the sequences belong to \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} never
cultured or named: the described prokaryotes are a small fraction of
those in even one litre of water, and the inventory is barely begun.
\textbf{22.} Background: \num{9} a year. A thousandfold: \num{9000} a
year --- most of them \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} never described.
\textbf{23.} Its characters, sequence and place on the tree are lost
with it; a branch of the tree of life is erased before it was drawn,
and any \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clade} it alone would have defined goes with it.
\textbf{24.} Families are large, conspicuous and nearly all described
(new families are now rare), so their count is close to complete;
the regular ratio of \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} to genera to families across the
well-known groups then lets the family count be extrapolated to
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} where the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} count is not complete.
\textbf{25.} (lamprey,(shark,(trout,(lungfish,(frog,(mouse,(lizard,
pigeon))))))), length \num{11}.
\end{solution}
