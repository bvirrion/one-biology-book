\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:dna-repair} --- Genome Stability: DNA Damage, Repair and Recombination}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:1}
Uracil: spontaneous (\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{deamination} of C), \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{base excision repair} by
uracil-DNA \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{glycosylase}. Thymine dimer: environmental (UV), \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{nucleotide
excision repair}. \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{8-oxoguanine}: spontaneous (oxidation), \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{base excision
repair} by the 8-oxoG \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{glycosylase}. G--T mismatch behind the fork:
replication error, \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair}. \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{Double-strand break} in G1:
environmental (radiation) or spontaneous; \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{non-homologous end joining},
since no sister chromatid is available.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:2}
Nucleotide excision recognises a physical property shared by all bulky
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} --- the distortion of the helix, or a stalled RNA polymerase
--- and removes a patch containing whatever caused it. Small \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions}
(uracil, 8-oxoG, alkylated bases) hardly distort the helix, so they
must be recognised by their chemistry, one \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{glycosylase} per kind of
wrong base.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:3}
A mismatch consists of two normal bases, and only the one on the new
strand is wrong; the system must know which strand is new.
\emph{E.~coli} marks the parental strand with methyl groups at GATC,
which the new strand lacks for a few minutes; MutH nicks the
unmethylated strand. In a \emph{dam} mutant neither strand is
methylated: MutH nicks either, the repair removes the right base as
often as the wrong one --- fixing half the errors as mutations
(a mutator) --- and nicks on both strands at nearby GATC sites make
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand breaks}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:4}
About $35\times 2 = 70$ \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand breaks}. After an hour most are
joined: with a half-life near \qty{30}{min}, about $70/4 \approx 18$
foci. After six hours nearly all have been repaired ($70/2^{12} < 1$),
leaving only a few persistent, complex breaks.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:5}
(a) $10^{-5}\times 10^{-2}\times 10^{-3} = 10^{-10}$; $6.4\times
10^{9}\times 10^{-10} = 0.64$ mutations per division. (b) Without
\omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair} $10^{-7}$; $640$ per division. (c) Without proofreading
$10^{-8}$; $64$ per division. The mismatch-repair mutant is the more
dangerous, a thousand-fold over normal.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:6}
$k = \ln 2/\qty{0.25}{h} = \qty{2.8}{h^{-1}}$; $D = 10^{4}/24 =
\qty{420}{h^{-1}}$; $L^{*} = 420/2.8 \approx 150$ abasic sites present
at any moment. Tenfold slower repair: $L^{*} \approx \num{1500}$. The
fork passes every locus once, meeting the \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} present there at
that moment; averaged over the genome it meets about $L^{*}$ of them
--- some $150$ normally, $\num{1500}$ with the drug.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:7}
$k_{\text{NHEJ}} = \ln 2/0.5 = \qty{1.39}{h^{-1}}$, $k_{\text{HR}} =
\ln 2/4 = \qty{0.17}{h^{-1}}$; HR fraction $0.17/1.56 = 0.11$;
half-life $\ln 2/1.56 = \qty{27}{min}$. A collapsed fork gives a
\emph{one-ended} break: there is no second end for NHEJ to join, so
only recombination with the sister can restore the fork --- and it is
the only accurate pathway in any case.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:8}
(a) Lynch: microsatellites unstable (lengths differ between tumour
cells), colorectal, endometrial, gastric and ovarian cancers, normal
response to sunlight. (b) XP-A: microsatellites stable, extreme
sunburn on minimal exposure, skin and eye cancers in childhood, and
in this group progressive neurological degeneration. (c) XP-V:
microsatellites stable, excision repair normal so sunburn is milder,
but the dimers that escape excision are bypassed by error-prone
polymerases --- skin cancers, of later onset than in group~A.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:9}
Liver cells: Cre has excised exon~3 from both alleles (as a circle
that is lost), so the gene is inactive in every hepatocyte from birth
--- a liver-specific knockout. Neurons: no Cre, the floxed alleles
are intact and functional. Opposite orientations: Cre inverts the
exon and inverts it back indefinitely, so at any time about half the
cells carry the inverted, non-functional exon --- a mosaic, not a clean
deletion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:10}
As LexA is cleaved its concentration falls gradually. Operators that
bind LexA weakly are vacated at a small fall --- \emph{uvrA},
\emph{uvrB}, \emph{recA} --- so accurate repair starts first; the
operators of \emph{sulA} and of the error-prone polymerases bind LexA
tightly and are vacated only when it is nearly gone, that is, when
damage has persisted for a long time. A bacterium without the
error-prone polymerases would die at forks blocked by \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} it could
not bypass, and, with no induced mutagenesis, would evolve resistance
to the antibiotic far more slowly --- a disadvantage to the bacterium,
an advantage to the patient; inhibitors of the \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:sos}{SOS response} are
studied for that reason.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:11}
Chain: PARP inhibited $\to$ single-strand breaks persist $\to$ a
replication fork converts each into a one-ended \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand break}
$\to$ repair requires \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{homologous recombination} $\to$ the tumour cell,
lacking \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{BRCA}, cannot $\to$ mis-joining, chromosome loss, death. Normal
cells, with one functional allele, recombine and survive. Resistance:
(1) a second mutation in \emph{\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{BRCA}} that restores the reading frame
and a working protein --- detect by sequencing the tumour or the
tumour DNA circulating in blood, and by the return of \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{Rad51} foci after
irradiation; (2) loss of the proteins that block end resection
(53BP1--shieldin), which partly restores recombination without BRCA1
--- detect by their absence on staining and by restored \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{Rad51} foci;
also mutations of PARP1 that prevent its trapping on DNA, or drug
efflux pumps, detected by sequencing and expression respectively.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:dna-repair:12}
Two homologous duplexes, one carrying $A$ and the other $a$, exchange
single strands across the marker: each now has one strand from each
parent over the tract --- a heteroduplex with an $A/a$ mismatch. If
\omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair} converts the mismatch on the invaded duplex to $A$,
three chromatids carry $A$ and one $a$: $3{:}1$; converted to $a$:
$1{:}3$; left unrepaired, the chromatid segregates $A$ and $a$ at the
first mitosis after meiosis, giving two different daughter spores
(post-meiotic segregation, $5{:}3$ in an eight-spored ascus). The
heteroduplex tract is only a few hundred to a few thousand base pairs
around the initiating break, so only markers within it can be
converted, and they are, by construction, next to the crossover.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:dna-repair:1}
\textbf{1.} $10^{4} + 400 + 10^{4} \approx 2\times 10^{4}$ \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} a
day, $7\times 10^{6}$ a year --- roughly one per kilobase of the genome
per year.
\textbf{2.} $10^{-5}\times 10^{-2}\times 10^{-3} = 10^{-10}$ per bp;
$6.4\times 10^{9}\times 10^{-10} = 0.64$ mutations per division.
\textbf{3.} $0.64\times 0.015 \approx 0.01$ coding mutations per
division; over $50$ divisions, about $0.5$.
\textbf{4.} $10^{-7}$ per bp, $640$ mutations per division. Slipped
cells: without repair $10^{-4}\times 40\times 10^{9} = 4\times 10^{6}$;
with it $10^{-7}\times 40\times 10^{9} = 4000$ --- a thousand-fold
difference, visible as \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:mmr}{microsatellite instability}.
\textbf{5.} Uracil is foreign to DNA, is recognised by uracil-DNA
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{glycosylase} and excised: repaired. Thymine from 5-methylcytosine is a
normal base opposite a G; nothing marks it as wrong outside replication,
and it becomes a C$\to$T mutation --- the mechanism that has depleted
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG} from the genome.
\textbf{6.} The 8-oxoG \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{glycosylase} removes the oxidised base while it is
still opposite C (before replication); the second \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{glycosylase} removes
an A misincorporated opposite 8-oxoG (after replication, restoring a
chance to repair the G); the hydrolase destroys oxidised dGTP so it is
not incorporated opposite A. When all fail, 8-oxoG pairs with A and the
next round fixes a G$\cdot$C$\to$T$\cdot$A transversion.
\textbf{7.} Normal $k = \ln 2/2 = \qty{0.35}{h^{-1}}$; XP $k = \ln 2/70
= \qty{0.0099}{h^{-1}}$.
\textbf{8.} Normal $10^{5} e^{-2.77} \approx 6200$; XP $10^{5}
e^{-0.079} \approx \num{92000}$.
\textbf{9.} Mutations $6.2$ and $92$ per cell; coding $0.09$ and $1.4$.
\textbf{10.} $500$ exposures: $47$ coding mutations (normal) and $690$
(XP), against $0.5$ from replication --- sunlight dominates the
mutation load of skin even in a normal person.
\textbf{11.} Coding sequence $9.6\times 10^{7}$ bp; the driver genes
are $4\times 10^{4}/9.6\times 10^{7} = 4.2\times 10^{-4}$ of it. Mean
hits $690\times 4.2\times 10^{-4} = 0.29$; $P(\ge 1) = 1 - e^{-0.29} =
0.25$. Of $10^{9}$ cells, $2.5\times 10^{8}$ carry a driver mutation
(normal child: mean $0.02$, \qty{2}{\%}).
\textbf{12.} A dimer is not a mutation; it becomes one only when a
fork copies it by error-prone bypass, so dividing cells with many
unrepaired dimers are the ones that mutate. Ultraviolet light reaches
only the skin and the eye; internal tissues receive no dimers and, in
XP, are almost normal.
\textbf{13.} $70$ breaks; after \qty{1}{h} with NHEJ alone, $70\times
2^{-2} \approx 18$ foci.
\textbf{14.} $k_{\text{NHEJ}} = \qty{1.39}{h^{-1}}$, $k_{\text{HR}} =
\qty{0.17}{h^{-1}}$; HR fraction $0.17/1.56 = 0.11$.
\textbf{15.} Half-life $\ln 2/1.56 = \qty{27}{min}$; after \qty{1}{h}
$70\,e^{-1.56} \approx 15$ breaks.
\textbf{16.} $70\times 0.015\times 0.7 \approx 0.7$ genes disrupted per
cell.
\textbf{17.} $n = 70$: $2415$ pairs $\times 3\times 10^{-4} = 0.72$
translocations. $n = 7$: $21\times 3\times 10^{-4} = 0.006$. The break
number is $\propto D$ but the number of \emph{pairs} of simultaneous
breaks is $\propto n^{2} \propto D^{2}$.
\textbf{18.} $D = \qty{7}{h^{-1}}$, $L^{*} = 7/1.39 \approx 5$ breaks
present at a time: $10$ pairs, $0.003$ translocations --- two hundred
times fewer than the same dose in a flash. Fractionation lets normal
tissue repair between fractions and keeps simultaneous breaks few.
\textbf{19.} Intact repair: $e^{-2/1.5} = 0.26$ at \qty{2}{Gy},
$e^{-4} = 0.018$ at \qty{6}{Gy}. No NHEJ: $e^{-4} = 0.018$ and
$e^{-12} = 6\times 10^{-6}$. $D_{0}$ is the dose that leaves on average
one lethal unrepaired \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion} per cell (survival $1/e$).
\textbf{20.} The \qty{11}{\%} of two-ended G2 breaks that HR would have
repaired accurately now go through NHEJ, and every one-ended fork
break --- which NHEJ cannot repair correctly at all --- is mis-joined:
over years the genome accumulates deletions, translocations and
copy-number changes, the characteristic scars of \emph{\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{BRCA}} tumours.
\textbf{21.} $10^{4}\times 0.01 = 100$ one-ended breaks per day. A
one-ended break has no partner end; NHEJ can only leave it or join it
to some unrelated end, producing a translocation or a loss.
\textbf{22.} One functional \emph{BRCA2} allele makes enough protein for
recombination: the normal cells repair their fork breaks and survive
the inhibitor.
\textbf{23.} A second frameshift downstream of the first restores the
reading frame and yields a shortened but partly functional BRCA2, so
recombination returns and the drug no longer kills. Detect by
sequencing \emph{BRCA2} in the resistant tumour or in circulating
tumour DNA, and functionally by the reappearance of \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{Rad51} foci after
irradiation.
\textbf{24.} The killing needs \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair} to engage the alkylated
bases; a Lynch tumour lacking it is tolerant --- the drug fails to kill
it (such tumours are instead treated by other means, including
immunotherapy, to which their heavy mutation load makes them
sensitive).
\textbf{25.} About \num{92000} dimers at the fork in the XP cell;
$10^{-10}$ mutations per base pair per division; HR repairs
\qty{11}{\%} of G2 breaks.
\end{solution}
