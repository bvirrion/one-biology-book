\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b2:living-soil} --- The Living Soil}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:1}
O: litter and partly decayed organic matter, where decomposition
begins. A: mineral \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil} mixed with \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{humus}, dark and crumb-structured,
holding most roots, organisms, nutrients and water. B: the horizon
of accumulation, where clay, iron oxides and carbonates leached
from above are deposited. C: weathered parent material, rock
fragments in a fine matrix, where new mineral \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil} is released. R:
unweathered bedrock.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:2}
The negative charges on clay and \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{humus} surfaces hold exchangeable
cations that roots can take up in exchange for protons. Sand grains
are quartz, uncharged and coarse, with little surface per gram
and no \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{humus} to speak of. Liming adds calcium carbonate: the
calcium displaces the hydrogen and aluminium ions that saturate the
exchange sites of an acid \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil}, the carbonate neutralises the
acidity, and the sites are refilled with a nutrient cation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:3}
Decomposers: bacteria (\emph{Bacillus}) and fungi
(\emph{Trichoderma}); microbial grazers: \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:protist}{protists} (amoebae) and
bacterial-feeding nematodes; detritivores: earthworms, millipedes,
springtails, woodlice; predators: mites, predatory nematodes,
centipedes, ground beetles; and moles or shrews at the top. The
grazers, with a $\mathrm{C:N}$ higher than their bacterial prey,
excrete the surplus nitrogen as ammonium: they mineralise the
nitrogen the bacteria had locked up.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:4}
Arbuscular: the fungus (a glomeromycete) enters the root cortex
cells and forms arbuscules; found in most herbs and crops and in
tropical trees. Ectomycorrhizal: the fungus (basidiomycetes and
ascomycetes, many of them mushrooms) sheathes the root tip and
grows between the cells without entering them; found in temperate
and boreal trees. In both, the plant gives sugar and the fungus
gives phosphate, nitrogen, water, micronutrients and some
protection from pathogens.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:5}
$L^{*} = I/k = 4/0.8 = \qty{5}{t/ha}$; \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} $1/k = 1.25$
years; \qty{90}{\%} loss after $\ln 10/0.8 = 2.9$ years.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:6}
Per \qty{100}{kg} of carbon eaten the microbes keep \qty{40}{kg} and
need $40/8 = \qty{5}{kg}$ of nitrogen. Straw supplies $100/80 =
\qty{1.25}{kg}$: immobilisation of \qty{3.75}{kg} per \qty{100}{kg}
of carbon. Clover supplies $100/15 = \qty{6.7}{kg}$: mineralisation
of \qty{1.7}{kg}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:7}
\qty{20}{t/ha} of \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil} a year through the worms. The top
\qty{20}{cm} weighs $0.2\times 10^{4}\times 1.3 = \qty{2600}{t/ha}$:
130 years for one passage. Darwin's ten tonnes per acre is
\qty{25}{t/ha}, the same order --- his ``every few years'' referred
to the superficial mould, a few centimetres, not the whole topsoil.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:8}
Bacteria: $10^{9}\times 10^{-12} = \qty{1}{mg}$ per gram. Hyphae:
volume $\pi(2\times 10^{-4})^{2}\times 10^{4} = \qty{1.26e-3}{cm^3}$,
mass \qty{1.4}{mg} per gram. Per hectare ($2.6\times 10^{9}$ g):
\qty{2.6}{t} of bacteria and \qty{3.6}{t} of fungi.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:9}
$H^{*} = 1.5/0.02 = \qty{75}{t/ha}$ of carbon. Ploughed: $1.5/0.04
= \qty{37.5}{t/ha}$; loss \qty{37.5}{t/ha}, half of it gone after
$\ln 2/0.04 = 17$ years.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:10}
Phosphate: $\sqrt{2\times 10^{-13}\times 8.64\times 10^{6}} =
\qty{1.3}{mm}$ in a season --- a root \qty{1}{cm} from the next
reaches an eighth of the \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil} between them, while hyphae
\qty{0.1}{mm} apart reach all of it. Nitrate: \qty{4}{cm}, farther
than the root spacing: it comes to the root by itself (and by mass
flow with the water), so mycorrhizae add little for nitrate and
much for phosphate.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:11}
Mass $0.25\times 10^{4}\times 1.3 = \qty{3250}{t/ha}$. Net loss
\qty{19}{t/ha} a year: lifetime 170 years. Carbon lost $20\times
0.03 = \qty{0.6}{t/ha}$ a year. Under no-till the net loss is
\qty{1}{t/ha}: 3250 years.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:living-soil:12}
Litter turns over in a year or two, \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{humus} in fifty, the mineral A
horizon in thousands (its formation at \qty{0.1}{mm} a year), and
salt accumulates in decades and is drained in decades if there is
drainage. A farmer sees the litter and the crop respond within a
season and the \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{humus} within a career (a decline of a third takes
thirty years; a recovery as long); the erosion of the mineral \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil}
is invisible in one lifetime and irreversible in ten; salt is
visible in a generation and, without drainage, permanent. The
\omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil}'s slow variables are the ones no annual account records.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b2:living-soil:1}
\textbf{1.} $0.25\times 10^{4}\times 1.3 = \qty{3250}{t/ha}$;
carbon $\qty{81}{t/ha}$.
\textbf{2.} $81/12 = \qty{6.8}{t/ha}$ of nitrogen.
\textbf{3.} Bacteria \qty{1}{mg/g}, $\qty{3.25}{t/ha}$; hyphae
$\pi(2\times 10^{-4})^{2}\times 2\times 10^{4}\times 1.1 =
\qty{2.8}{mg/g}$, \qty{9}{t/ha}: microbial biomass about
\qty{12}{t/ha}.
\textbf{4.} About \qty{14}{t/ha} of living matter (fresh mass),
some \qty{17}{\%} of the \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{humus} carbon --- a few per cent of it as
carbon, since organisms are mostly water.
\textbf{5.} Microbial carbon about half of \qty{12}{t}, \qty{6}{t};
turning over twice a year with \qty{40}{\%} retained, they consume
$2\times 6/0.4 = \qty{30}{t}$ of carbon and respire \qty{18}{t/ha}
of carbon, \qty{66}{t} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (a crude estimate: the
retention and turnover are rough).
\textbf{6.} Three times the crop's net primary production of
\qty{5}{t}: the estimate is too high, as the assumptions (all the
biomass turning over twice, none of it dormant) are generous;
real \omterm{prop:b2:living-soil:humus}{soil respiration} under a crop is of the order of the crop's
own production, several tonnes of carbon per hectare a year. The
\omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil} breathes as much as the plants above it.
\textbf{7.} Carbon $5\times 0.45 = \qty{2.25}{t}$; nitrogen $5\times
0.03 = \qty{150}{kg}$; $\mathrm{C:N} = 15$.
\textbf{8.} Mineralises: the microbes need $2250\times 0.4/8 =
\qty{112}{kg}$ of nitrogen and the residue holds 150, so
\qty{38}{kg/ha} is released net.
\textbf{9.} $1 - e^{-0.5} = \qty{39}{\%}$ after 3 months; $1 - e^{-2}
= \qty{86}{\%}$ after a year.
\textbf{10.} $0.39\times 38 = \qty{15}{kg/ha}$.
\textbf{11.} The crop needs \qty{150}{kg}, most of it in its first
two months; the residue gives \qty{15}{kg} in three months and
\qty{38}{kg} in all --- a supplement, not a supply. Its value is
also in the nitrogen the clover fixed and holds in the residue's
own \qty{150}{kg}, most of which enters the \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{humus} and comes back
over years.
\textbf{12.} Straw: carbon \qty{2250}{kg}, nitrogen $2250/80 =
\qty{28}{kg}$; the microbes need \qty{112}{kg}, so \qty{84}{kg/ha}
is immobilised from the \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil} --- more than half the crop's need.
The farmer must add nitrogen with the straw, or plough it in well
before sowing, or leave it on the surface where it decays slowly.
\textbf{13.} Ploughed: $1.2/0.025 = \qty{48}{t/ha}$; no-till:
$1.2/0.015 = \qty{80}{t/ha}$.
\textbf{14.} $H(t) = 80 - 32\,e^{-0.015t}$: gain \qty{4.5}{t/ha}
after 10 years, \qty{17}{t/ha} after 50.
\textbf{15.} $\mathrm{d}H/\mathrm{d}t = 0.015\times 32 =
\qty{0.48}{t/ha}$ of carbon in the first year, \qty{1.8}{t} of
$\mathrm{CO_2}$.
\textbf{16.} $0.48\times 1.5\times 10^{9} = \qty{0.7}{GtC}$ in the
first year, \qty{7}{\%} of fossil emissions; by the fiftieth year
the rate has fallen to $0.48\,e^{-0.75} = \qty{0.23}{t/ha}$,
\qty{0.34}{GtC}.
\textbf{17.} The gain is the approach to a new \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{steady state}: it
decays to zero as $H$ reaches \qty{80}{t/ha}, and the whole gain is
returned to the air within decades if the field is ploughed again
--- a \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil} sink is a one-time, reversible transfer, not a permanent
offset of continuing emissions.
\textbf{18.} $k_h = 0.02$: $H^{*} = \qty{60}{t/ha}$, a loss of
\qty{20}{t/ha}, more than the no-till gain: warming can undo the
management.
\textbf{19.} Ploughed: $15 - 0.5 = \qty{14.5}{t/ha}$ a year; no-till:
\qty{1}{t/ha}.
\textbf{20.} $3250/14.5 = 220$ years; $3250/1 = 3250$ years.
\textbf{21.} Carbon $15\times 0.025 = \qty{0.38}{t/ha}$; nitrogen
$0.38/12 = \qty{31}{kg/ha}$ a year.
\textbf{22.} \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{Humus} decay at the ploughed \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{steady state} is $k_hH^{*} =
I = \qty{1.2}{t/ha}$ a year; erosion adds a third as much again,
but unlike decay it is not balanced by input --- it drains the
stock.
\textbf{23.} Partly. Eroded carbon buried in a sediment or a
\omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} may be protected from decay for a long time (a burial
sink); but the aggregates are broken in transport, much of the
carbon is oxidised on the way, and the field's lost fertility is
replaced by fertiliser whose manufacture emits carbon. The global
sign of erosion's carbon effect is still debated.
\textbf{24.} Litter $1/k = 0.5$ years; \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{humus} $1/k_h = 40$ years
under the plough and 67 under no-till; mineral \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil} $3250/0.5 =
6500$ years with respect to formation, and 220 years with respect
to loss under the plough.
\textbf{25.} Carbon stock \qty{81}{t/ha} in the A horizon; lifetime
220 years under the plough, 3250 under no-till; the clover residue
supplies about \qty{38}{kg/ha} of mineral nitrogen, 15 of them in
the first three months.
\end{solution}
