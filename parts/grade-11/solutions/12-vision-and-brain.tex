\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g11:vision-and-brain} --- Vision and the Brain}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:1}
Light from the left field falls on the right half of each retina; the
right-half fibres of the left eye cross at the chiasm, those of the
right eye stay on the right; together they form the right optic
tract, relay in the thalamus and reach the right \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{primary visual
cortex}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:2}
The two optic nerves meet; the fibres from the nasal half of each
retina cross to the other side, those from the temporal half do not.
Beyond it each tract carries one half of the visual field.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:3}
Colour area (world seen in grey), movement area (no perception of
motion), face area (faces not recognised).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:4}
Plasticity: the ability of neuronal connections to be strengthened,
weakened, formed or pruned according to use. Critical period: the
early phase of life during which experience shapes the cortex
decisively and irreversibly.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:5}
It binds the serotonin receptors of neurons in the visual areas,
disturbing the flow of signals and producing hallucinations without
any corresponding light.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:6}
After the chiasm, on the left: the left optic tract, the left
thalamic relay or the left \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{primary visual cortex}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:7}
The crossing fibres come from the nasal halves of both retinas, which
see the outer (temporal) halves of the field: the patient loses the
left half of the left eye's field and the right half of the right
eye's --- tunnel vision on both sides.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:8}
Normal: $12 + 20 + 36 + 20 = 88\%$. Deprived: $2 + 3 + 5 + 30 = 40\%$,
of which only 10\% mainly.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:9}
Patching forces the cortex to use the weak eye's input, so its
connections are kept and strengthened during the critical period
instead of being pruned. In an adult the period is over: connections
no longer reorganise on that scale, so the weak eye cannot regain its
territory.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:10}
Her \omterm{def:g11:the-eye:photoreceptors}{cones} and their \omterm{def:g11:the-eye:photoreceptors}{opsins} are normal; what is lost is the cortical
stage that interprets their ratios. An electrical recording of the
retina's response to coloured lights would be normal in her and
abnormal in a colour-blind person; and her deficit is total and
acquired, theirs partial and inborn.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:11}
Years of practice have strengthened and multiplied the connections
serving her fingers, enlarging their cortical territory. Without
practice the area would shrink back over years, as the juggling
studies show.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:12}
At first she sees light, colours and moving blobs but cannot
recognise shapes, faces or depth: the retina works, but the cortex
never built, during the critical period, the connections that
interpret its signals. She will learn to use vision partly, slowly,
but never with the fluency of someone who saw from birth.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:13}
At the fovea each cone has its own ganglion \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, so the centre of
the field sends far more fibres per degree than the periphery, where
a hundred \omterm{def:g11:the-eye:photoreceptors}{rods} share one; the cortex allots area in proportion to
fibres, hence to detail, not to degrees of field.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:14}
The area's activity is the perception of colour, whether the signal
comes from the eye or from memory; perception is what the areas do,
not what the eye delivers. A hallucination is that activity started
by a drug instead of by intention or light.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:vision-and-brain:15}
The eye converts light into signals; seeing is done by the brain. A
patient with intact eyes can lose colour, motion or faces from a
cortical lesion; a kitten with intact eyes goes functionally blind in
one of them if the cortex is not allowed to connect it; and a drug
that touches neither eye nor cortex's structure makes a person see
what is not there. The eye supplies; the brain sees.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g11:vision-and-brain:1}
\textbf{1.} The left eye or its optic nerve, before the chiasm: only
one eye's fibres are affected.

\textbf{2.} The right optic tract, thalamus or \omterm{prop:g11:vision-and-brain:pathway}{visual cortex}: a deficit
shared by both eyes for the same half-field can only lie where the
two eyes' fibres for that half have been gathered together, i.e.
after the chiasm.

\textbf{3.} The chiasm itself: the crossing fibres, from the nasal
halves of both retinas, which see the temporal halves of the field,
are cut.

\textbf{4.} The movement area, beyond the primary cortex. Intact
fields and acuity show the primary cortex and everything before it
are working.

\textbf{5.} B, C and D have intact retinas; A's may or may not be.
Check by examining the retina with an ophthalmoscope and recording
its electrical response to flashes.

\textbf{6.} The right cortex receives the left half of the field.

\textbf{7.} Central $2^\circ$: \qty{1}{cm}; a $2^\circ$ band at
$40^\circ$: \qty{0.1}{cm}. Ratio 10.

\textbf{8.} The fovea packs its \omterm{def:g11:the-eye:photoreceptors}{cones} densely, each with its own
ganglion \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, so the central degrees send many more fibres than the
periphery; the cortex gives them proportionally more area.

\textbf{9.} A small blind spot at the centre of the left field, which
is crippling for reading; further forward, a larger blind region in
the periphery of the left field, less noticed.

\textbf{10.} By moving the eyes the patient brings each word onto a
part of the retina whose cortex is intact, using the border of the
lesion; slow, but possible.

\textbf{11.} $20 + 36 + 20 = 76\%$.

\textbf{12.} $2 + 3 + 5 + 30 = 40\%$, and only 10\% with the left eye
as their main or equal input.

\textbf{13.} Its input reaches the cortex but its connections were
weakened and pruned for lack of use; the right eye's connections
expanded into the space.

\textbf{14.} That the reorganisation is confined to a critical period
of early life; the human counterpart is amblyopia, which develops only
in childhood and is untreatable after it.

\textbf{15.} With neither eye favoured, no takeover: the histogram
stays roughly symmetric but with fewer responsive neurons in all,
and the kitten's vision is poor in both eyes --- deprivation without
competition weakens both.

\textbf{16.} The brain suppresses the turned eye's image to avoid
double vision; unused, its connections are pruned during the critical
period and the eye becomes permanently weak. Glasses correct optics,
but the eye is not used, so the pruning proceeds.

\textbf{17.} Covering the good eye forces the cortex to use the weak
eye's signals, keeping and strengthening its connections.

\textbf{18.} The critical period ends around seven; after it the
cortex no longer reallocates territory on that scale, so the weak
eye's lost connections cannot be rebuilt.

\textbf{19.} In the adult the connections are already established and
stable; the takeover by the other eye is a phenomenon of the
developing cortex, during the critical period, not of the mature one.

\textbf{20.} A: eye or optic nerve; C: chiasm; B: right tract, thalamus
or primary cortex; D: the movement area beyond it. The cortex's
plasticity, confined to a critical period, makes the child's patching
work at four and fail at fifteen.
\end{solution}
