\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:stem-cells} --- Stem Cells, Regeneration and Ageing}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:1}
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{Self-renewal}: a division that produces at least one daughter that is
still a \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} --- the \emph{Lgr5} cells at the crypt base. Potency:
the range of fates a cell can still adopt --- the crypt \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} is
multipotent, making absorptive cells, goblet, enteroendocrine, Paneth
and tuft cells. \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{Niche}: the \omterm{prop:b3:stem-cells:crypt}{Paneth cells} and their Wnt, Notch and EGF
signals, which hold stemness in place. \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{Transit-amplifying cell}: a
committed daughter that divides four or five more times in the crypt
before differentiating on its way up the villus.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:2}
Single marrow cells produced spleen colonies containing red cells,
granulocytes and platelets: one cell, several lineages ---
multipotency, and the linear dose response showed each colony came from
one cell. \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{Self-renewal} needed a second mouse: cells from a colony,
injected into another irradiated recipient, made new colonies, so the
founding cell had produced daughters with its own capacity.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:3}
\emph{Oct4}, \emph{Sox2}, \emph{Klf4}, \emph{c-Myc}. The fibroblast's
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} has closed the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:pluripotency}{pluripotency} genes behind \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{heterochromatin} and
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{DNA methylation}, so the factors find few accessible sites at first and
must recruit remodelling enzymes over many \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cell cycles}; the cell must
also shut its own programme, escape \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:other}{senescence} and pass through a
stochastic intermediate state in which most cells stall or die. Only
the rare cell in which every step succeeds emerges pluripotent, weeks
later.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:4}
The planarian keeps pluripotent adult \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cells}, the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{neoblasts}, which
migrate to the wound and build whatever is missing. The salamander has
no such pool: differentiated cells at the stump dedifferentiate into
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{progenitors}, which with resident tissue \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cells} form a \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{blastema}
that re-runs the limb programme. Kragl's grafts of single labelled
tissues showed that \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{blastema} cells stay true to their origin ---
muscle makes muscle, cartilage cartilage --- so the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{blastema} is a
mixture of lineage-restricted \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{progenitors}, not a pluripotent mass.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:6}
Fourteen stem-cell divisions a day yield 28 daughters; 14 must remain
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cells}, so 14 commit --- one committed daughter per \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} per
day. Each commits to $2^{4} = 16$ cells: $14\times 16 = 224$ villus
cells a day, the order of the \num{300} observed (a fifth transit
division gives \num{448}). The stem tier contributes fourteen cells'
worth of divisions, the transit tier the other \num{200}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:7}
$2\times 10^{11}/2^{20} = 1.9\times 10^{5}$ committed daughters a day;
with \num{10000} \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cells}, 19 per \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} per day --- against one
division a year. The count of twenty divisions is per lineage from
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} to red cell, but the intermediate \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{progenitors} are not
one-shot: multipotent and committed \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{progenitor} pools sustain themselves
for weeks by their own divisions, so nearly all the division happens
in the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{progenitor} tiers and the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} is called on rarely.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:8}
$\mu(50) = 10^{-3}\mathrm{e}^{1.74} = 5.7\times 10^{-3}$; $\mu(70) =
10^{-3}\mathrm{e}^{3.48} = 0.032$; $\mu(90) = 10^{-3}\mathrm{e}^{5.22} =
0.19$ per year. With $\mu_{0}/\gamma = 0.0115$: $S(70) = \exp[-0.0115
\times 31.5] = 0.70$, $S(90) = \exp[-0.0115\times 184] = 0.12$. Median:
$30 + \ln(61)/0.087 = 77$. Halving $\mu_{0}$: $\ln(121)/0.087 = 55$,
median 85 --- one doubling time, eight years, gained. Halving $\gamma$
to $0.0435$: $\ln(31)/0.0435 = 79$, median 109: slowing the exponent
gains four times more than halving the constant.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:9}
The remaining third must triple: $\log_{2}3 = 1.6$ divisions per cell.
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{Compensatory growth}, because the existing lobes enlarge by division of
cells in place; the removed lobes, with their ducts, vessels and
architecture, are not rebuilt --- the liver recovers its mass and
function but not its shape.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:10}
At each event the clone's size $k$ goes to $k + 1$ or $k - 1$ with equal
probability, so its expected size never changes; when the walk ends it
is $N$ (take-over) or $0$ (loss), and the expectation $N\,P + 0 = k$
gives $P = k/N$. A single cell: $1/14$. A neutral mutation in one \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem
cell} fixes in its crypt with probability $1/14$ and is otherwise lost
within months --- drift purges thirteen of fourteen mutations.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:11}
The walk is now biased: the clone gains a place more often than it
loses one, so its expected size grows and the take-over probability
rises toward certainty as $k$ grows --- for a \qty{10}{\%} advantage in
a \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{niche} of fourteen, roughly threefold above $1/14$ for a single cell,
and higher still once the clone holds a few places. Over decades, \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem
cells} with such mutations (\emph{DNMT3A}, \emph{TET2} in marrow) take
over their \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{niches}, and by seventy a fifth of people have a large share
of their blood from one clone. It is not \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{cancer}: the cells still
differentiate and obey their hierarchy. But the next mutation now has
a clone of billions to arise in, and the risk of leukaemia is raised
tenfold.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:stem-cells:12}
Median $= 30 + \gamma^{-1}\ln(1 + \gamma\ln 2/\mu_{0})$: for $\mu_{0} =
10^{-2}$, $30 + \ln 7/0.087 = 52$; $10^{-3}$: 77; $10^{-4}$: $30 +
\ln 604/0.087 = 104$. In the pure Gompertz form each tenfold cut adds the
same $\ln 10/\gamma = 26$ years. The returns diminish in practice
because the historical gains came from removing an age-independent term
--- infection, childbirth, accident, Makeham's constant $A$ in $\mu = A
+ \mu_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma t}$ --- and once $A$ is small compared with the
exponential at the ages that matter, cutting it further changes almost
nothing; the intrinsic $\mu_{0}$ has moved much less. A \qty{20}{\%} cut
in $\gamma$ (to $0.070$) gives $30 + \ln 49/0.070 = 86$: nine years,
gained at every age rather than by postponing a few causes of death.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:stem-cells:1}
\textbf{1.} $3.5\times 10^{11}$ a day, $4\times 10^{6}$ a second.
\textbf{2.} $2^{20} \approx 10^{6}$ cells per committed daughter;
$3.5\times 10^{11}/10^{6} = 3.3\times 10^{5}$ committed daughters a day.
\textbf{3.} $33$ per \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} per day, \num{12000} a year.
\textbf{4.} The \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{progenitor} pools must sustain themselves: multipotent
and committed \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{progenitors} divide for weeks and keep their own numbers,
so almost every division of the twenty happens in tiers that renew
themselves, and the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} contributes a committed daughter rarely
--- of the order of once a year.
\textbf{5.} $10 - 20\times 0.06 = \qty{8.8}{kb}$: well above
$L_{\text{crit}}$, and the red cell never divides again; the reserve
matters only for \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{progenitors} that are re-used.
\textbf{6.} Net loss $0.3\times 60 = \qty{18}{bp}$; $6000/18 = 333$
divisions; at one a year, $333$ years --- far beyond a life, so
telomeres do not limit the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} itself; other damage does.
\textbf{7.} $14$ divisions a day; $7$ replace lost \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cells} and $7$
commit; $7\times 2^{4} = 112$ villus cells a day.
\textbf{8.} $N^{2} = 196$ events per cell at one a day: about
\qty{200}{days}, six to seven months --- the time over which confetti
crypts became single-coloured.
\textbf{9.} $1/14 \approx \qty{7}{\%}$; $10^{7}/14 \approx 7\times 10^{5}$
crypts.
\textbf{10.} Drift throws out thirteen of every fourteen mutations
within months. Lifelong \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{asymmetric division} would keep every mutant
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} for life, so mutations would accumulate in every lineage
without ever being purged.
\textbf{11.} Stemness is a position in the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{niche} and a set of signals,
not a permanent property of a cell: any cell that reaches the Paneth
signals can take the role.
\textbf{12.} Induce a multicolour label in the \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cells} at one moment
and follow the crypts. Symmetric drift: crypts become single-coloured
within months. \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{Asymmetric division}: each crypt keeps all its colours
indefinitely, each as a thin ribbon up the villus.
\textbf{13.} $6000/60 = 100$ doublings.
\textbf{14.} About $75$ left: the cell counts divisions, not calendar
time, since the decade in the freezer cost nothing.
\textbf{15.} $(8000 - 4000)/30 = 133$ years after twenty --- age 153.
Mean leukocyte telomere length does not itself limit life; the shortest
telomeres, and the other hallmarks, matter more.
\textbf{16.} \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:telomerase}{Telomerase} removes one barrier, \omterm{thm:b3:stem-cells:hayflick}{replicative senescence},
and is therefore found in nearly every \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{cancer}, which needs unlimited
division; but the fibroblasts given \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:telomerase}{telomerase} kept their checkpoints,
contact inhibition and intact p53, so unlimited division alone did not
make them \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{cancers}. Necessary, not sufficient.
\textbf{17.} $\mu(40) = 2.4\times 10^{-3}$, $\mu(60) = 0.014$, $\mu(80) =
0.077$, $\mu(100) = 0.44$ per year. $S = \exp[-0.0115(\mathrm{e}^{\gamma
(t-30)} - 1)]$: $0.98$ at 40, $0.87$ at 60, $0.42$ at 80, $0.006$ at
100.
\textbf{18.} Median $30 + \ln 61/0.087 = 77$. Ten per cent survive at
$\mathrm{e}^{\gamma(t-30)} = 1 + \gamma\ln 10/\mu_{0} = 201$: $t = 30 +
5.3/0.087 = 91$.
\textbf{19.} \qty{0.67}{mm/day}; $\log_{2}1000 = 10$ doublings in 60
days, one every six days.
\textbf{20.} About $10^{8}$ divisions, so $0.1$ oncogenic mutations per
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{regeneration} --- one in ten limbs. Axolotls almost never develop
\omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumours}: cold-blooded and slow, with robust \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumour} suppression, and the
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{blastema}'s cells remain lineage-restricted and re-differentiate rather
than continuing to divide; regenerating tissue even suppresses induced
\omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumours}. A mammal has more cells, a higher temperature and metabolic
rate, and a longer life over which an escaped clone could grow.
\textbf{21.} Denervate the stump and implant a bead releasing the
candidate protein, or graft nerve-conditioned tissue: \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{regeneration}
restored means a diffusible factor. Candidates identified in newts and
axolotls: the anterior-gradient protein nAG, neuregulin-1, and FGFs.
\textbf{22.} \omterm{prop:b3:stem-cells:crypt}{Wnt signalling} is high at the tail and low at the head, and
the wound at a piece's anterior end normally makes a head because Wnt
there is low; knocking down a Wnt inhibitor raises Wnt everywhere,
both wounds read ``posterior,'' and two tails grow. The piece knows its
own axis from the residual gradient in its muscle, and the wound
re-establishes the gradient relative to it.
\textbf{23.} The remaining third must triple: every hepatocyte divides
once (to two thirds) and half divide again --- a mean of $1.6$ divisions.
The surviving lobes swell to the original mass; the missing lobes and
their ducts and vessels are not rebuilt.
\textbf{24.} \omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:regeneration}{Regeneration} that replaces worn and damaged tissue would
slow the accumulation the exponent measures, lowering $\gamma$ and
flattening the curve --- axolotls show little \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:other}{senescence}. The price
would be a higher constant term from \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{cancers} in a warm, large,
long-lived body.
\textbf{25.} Hayflick count about $100$ doublings; a telomerase-aided
\omterm{def:b3:stem-cells:stem}{stem cell} has some $330$ divisions, more than three centuries at one a
year; median lifespan $77$ years.
\end{solution}
