\chapter{From Intention to Movement}\label{ch:g12:brain-and-movement}

A woman wakes one morning unable to move her right arm or leg, though
she can feel them, and her right knee jerks harder than ever when the
doctor taps it. The scan shows a small dead patch in the left half of
her brain, near the top. Everything below is intact --- the nerves,
the spinal cord, the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex arcs} of the last chapter, the muscles ---
and yet the intention to move does not reach them. Six months of
rehabilitation later, she walks, and the scan of her working brain
shows regions lighting up that did not command her leg before. This
chapter follows a voluntary movement from the cortex to the motor
\omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, and shows how the map that commands it is drawn, crossed, and
redrawn.

\section{The motor cortex}

\begin{definition}[Motor cortex]\label{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}
The \emph{primary motor cortex}\index{motor cortex} is a strip of the
cerebral cortex running over the top of each hemisphere, just in front
of the groove that separates the frontal lobe from the parietal. Its
\omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} are the origin of voluntary movement: their axons descend
through the brainstem and the spinal cord to the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} of the
muscles. The strip is a \emph{map} of the body: each part of it
commands one part of the body, in an order that runs from the foot at
the top of the hemisphere to the face at its side, and each part's
area is proportional not to the size of the body part but to the
fineness of its movements --- the hand and the face take up most of it.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The map is real, and crossed]\label{prop:g12:brain-and-movement:map}
Stimulating a point of the \omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{motor cortex} electrically produces a
movement of the corresponding body part on the \emph{opposite} side
of the body; damage to a point abolishes voluntary movement of that
part on the opposite side.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
During brain surgery under local anaesthesia, Penfield (1930s) applied
a weak current to points of the exposed cortex and recorded which
muscles moved: the points formed the map, with the hand and face
areas out of proportion to their size, and every movement on the side
opposite the stimulated hemisphere. Functional imaging of healthy
volunteers moving one finger shows a spot of activity at the
predicted place in the opposite hemisphere; moving the foot, a spot at
the top. Strokes destroying part of one \omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{motor cortex} paralyse the
mapped parts on the other side, with sensation intact.
\end{proof}

\section{The descending pathway}

\begin{proposition}[From the cortex to the motor neuron]\label{prop:g12:brain-and-movement:pathway}
The axons of the \omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{motor cortex} \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} form the \emph{corticospinal
tract}\index{corticospinal tract}. It descends through the brainstem,
where most of its fibres cross to the other side --- which is why each
hemisphere commands the opposite half of the body --- and continues
down the spinal cord, where at each level fibres leave to end on the
motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} of that level's muscles, or on interneurons beside them.
The motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} of \cref{ch:g12:stretch-reflex} is thus the meeting
point of two commands: the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex arc}'s, from the muscle spindle, and
the cortex's, from a metre above.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Tracing the fibres from the cortex through the brainstem shows the
crossing; a lesion of the tract above the crossing paralyses the
opposite side, below it the same side. A lesion of the spinal cord
paralyses everything below its level on both sides while sparing the
face, whose fibres leave above; the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes} below the lesion persist,
since their arcs are intact, and become exaggerated, since the
cortex's damping influence no longer reaches them.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Reading a paralysis]\label{ex:g12:brain-and-movement:paralysis}
Right arm and leg paralysed, face too, sensation kept: a lesion of
the left hemisphere's \omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{motor cortex} or its fibres above the crossing
--- the woman of the opening. Both legs paralysed, arms and face
spared, \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes} of the legs exaggerated: the spinal cord damaged at
the level of the lower back. One arm paralysed and limp, its \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes}
absent: the nerve or the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} themselves, below which
nothing can fire. The map, the crossing and the arc locate the damage
before any scan.
\end{example}

\section{The motor neuron decides}

\begin{proposition}[Integration at the motor neuron]\label{prop:g12:brain-and-movement:integration}
A motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} receives thousands of \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapses}: excitatory ones from
the cortex and from the spindles, inhibitory ones from interneurons
driven by the antagonist's spindles, by the cortex, and by other
centres. Each arriving signal shifts the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}'s voltage a little,
towards threshold or away from it; the shifts \emph{add up} over
milliseconds and over the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s surface, and the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} fires only
when their sum crosses threshold. The motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} is therefore not a
relay but a calculator: the muscle contracts when the balance of all
the commands reaching its motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} says so --- the \emph{final
common pathway}\index{final common pathway} of movement.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Recording inside a motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} shows each excitatory input producing
a small depolarisation of a millivolt or so, far below the
\qty{15}{mV} needed; a burst of inputs, or inputs from several
sources at once, summed to threshold produce an \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potential}; an
inhibitory input arriving at the same moment cancels an excitatory
one. The strength of a voluntary contraction rises with the number of
motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} recruited and with their firing rate, and the knee jerk
is stronger when the subject clenches a fist --- the cortex's
excitation adding to the spindle's.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Holding a glass]\label{ex:g12:brain-and-movement:glass}
Lifting a full glass, the cortex sends a steady excitation to the
motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} of the arm; the spindles, stretched as the glass tips,
add theirs; the antagonists' interneurons subtract when the movement
overshoots; the cerebellum, comparing the intended and the actual
movement, corrects the cortex's command a few tens of milliseconds
later. Each motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} sums the whole conversation and fires just
enough. The smoothness of the movement is the balance of the sums.
\end{example}

\section{A map that can be redrawn}

\begin{proposition}[Plasticity of the motor cortex]\label{prop:g12:brain-and-movement:plasticity}
The motor map is not fixed. Practising a movement enlarges the
cortical area that commands it and sharpens its connections; a
limb's area shrinks when the limb is immobilised, and after an
amputation is taken over by the neighbouring parts. After a stroke,
undamaged cortex around the lesion, and the corresponding area of the
other hemisphere, can learn to command the affected muscles ---
provided they are used: rehabilitation works by forcing the movement,
repeatedly, so that the surviving circuits are selected and
strengthened. The plasticity of \cref{ch:g11:vision-and-brain} applies
to the commanding side of the brain as to the sensing side.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Imaging the cortex of violinists shows an enlarged area for the
fingers of the left hand, in proportion to the age at which they
began; after five days of practising a finger sequence, ordinary
volunteers show a measurably enlarged area for those fingers, which
shrinks again if practice stops. After a stroke, patients whose
paralysed arm is trained intensively --- the good arm restrained ---
recover more function than those who compensate with the good arm,
and imaging shows the recovered movement commanded by regions
neighbouring the lesion. Monkeys whose finger area is lesioned recover
finger movement only if the fingers are exercised.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Six months after the stroke]\label{ex:g12:brain-and-movement:sixmonths}
Week 1: the right leg does not move; its \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes} are exaggerated.
Weeks 2--6: the physiotherapist moves the leg, then makes the patient
attempt each movement; a faint contraction returns. Months 2--4:
walking between bars, a thousand steps a day; imaging shows activity
spreading in the cortex around the lesion and in the other
hemisphere. Month 6: walking with a stick. The lesion has not healed
--- \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} of the cortex do not regrow --- but neighbouring circuits
have been recruited, and each step of the recovery was made by
attempting the movement.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Locating a motor disorder]\label{met:g12:brain-and-movement:locate}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which parts are affected? One side of the body, face
        included: the opposite hemisphere or its fibres above the
        crossing. Both sides below a level, face spared: the spinal
        cord at that level. One limb or muscle group only: its nerve or
        motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}.
  \item Are the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes} preserved? Exaggerated: the arc is intact and
        the cortical damping is lost (a lesion above the motor
        \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}). Absent: the arc itself is broken (the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron},
        nerve or muscle).
  \item Is sensation preserved? A pure motor deficit points to the
        motor pathway; a mixed one to a lesion affecting both.
  \item What can be recovered? Cortical lesions, through plasticity
        and training; a severed cord or nerve, much less; muscle and
        motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} diseases, according to their cause.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[The year's last loop]\label{rem:g12:brain-and-movement:loop}
The intention to move is a pattern of \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials} in a strip of
cortex; it descends a metre of axon, crosses, and is weighed by a
motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} against the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} of the muscle it will move; the
muscle contracts by the sliding of filaments, paid for by the \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} of
the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria}, on glucose the liver released under the eye of the
pancreas, on oxygen the heart and lungs delivered --- and the cortex
that gave the order was shaped, by its own use, into the map it is.
Every chapter of this book has been a piece of that loop. The volumes
that follow open each piece further; none of them changes the
loop.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Left Hemisphere}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a stroke read from the symptoms
to the lesion, the motor neuron's arithmetic, the map redrawn over six
months, and the whole loop of the year closed}]
\label{pb:g12:brain-and-movement:1}
A woman of 62 wakes with her right arm and leg paralysed and the right
side of her face drooping; sensation is normal; the right knee jerk
is exaggerated, the left normal. A scan shows a lesion of
\qty{2}{cm} in the upper part of the left hemisphere.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Locating.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Explain why the lesion is on the left although the deficit is
        on the right.
  \item Which part of the map is affected? Justify from the three
        body parts involved.
  \item Why is sensation normal? Which strip of cortex is spared?
  \item Why is the right knee jerk exaggerated rather than abolished?
  \item A second patient has the same paralysis but with the knee jerk
        absent and the muscles wasting. Locate his lesion and explain
        the difference.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}'s arithmetic.}
A motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} of the right leg has a threshold \qty{15}{mV} above its
resting voltage. Each cortical input depolarises it by \qty{1}{mV},
each spindle input by \qty{2}{mV}, each inhibitory input from the
antagonist's interneuron by \qty{-2}{mV}; inputs add if they arrive
within \qty{5}{ms}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Before the stroke, a voluntary step sent 12 cortical inputs
        and the spindle 3 within \qty{5}{ms}. Did the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} fire?
  \item During the same step, 2 inhibitory inputs also arrived. Sum
        again.
  \item After the stroke, the cortical inputs are zero. What is the
        sum during an attempted step? What does the leg do?
  \item The doctor taps the knee: the spindle sends 10 inputs within
        \qty{5}{ms}. Before the stroke the cortex also sent 3
        inhibitory (damping) inputs during a tap; after, none. Compute
        the sums and explain the exaggerated jerk.
  \item Three months later, cortical inputs are back to 8 per step.
        With the spindle's 3, does the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} fire? How would you
        expect the movement to look?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The map redrawn.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The dead \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} of the lesion do not regrow. Where can the
        cortical inputs of question 10 come from?
  \item Rehabilitation requires the patient to attempt the movement
        thousands of times. Explain, with plasticity, why attempting
        matters and passive movement of the leg by the therapist is
        not enough.
  \item Imaging at month 4 shows activity in the right hemisphere's
        leg area during a right-leg step. Which fibres could carry
        that command, given the crossing?
  \item The patient's right hand recovers less than her leg. Propose
        an explanation from the map and from the demands of hand
        movements.
  \item Compare her recovery with that of a kitten whose closed eye
        is reopened after the critical period. What is the same, what
        is different?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The loop closed.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Trace one step of her recovered walk from the cortex to the
        contraction: name the tract, the crossing, the meeting point,
        the transmitter at the muscle, the source of the muscle's \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}.
  \item The step costs her muscles \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} made from glucose released by
        the liver. Name the \omterm{def:g11:hormones-and-reproduction:hormone}{hormone} that let the liver release it and
        the organ that measured the need.
  \item The oxygen for that \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} was delivered by a heart beating
        faster. Name the two mechanisms that raised its rate at the
        start of the walk.
  \item Her recovery depended on plasticity; her stroke, on a blocked
        artery; her survival, on innate and \omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:immunity}{adaptive immunity} meeting
        the infections of a hospital bed. Name one chapter of this
        volume for each.
  \item State the result: the side and the strip of cortex where the
        lesion lies, the sum of inputs that makes a motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} fire,
        and the property of the cortex that gave her back her leg.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
