\chapter{A Look at Human Evolution}\label{ch:g12:human-evolution}

In a layer of volcanic ash three and a half million years old, two
trails of footprints run side by side for twenty-seven metres. They
were left by creatures walking upright, heel first, with an arched foot
and a big toe in line with the others --- feet like ours --- at a time
when no brain larger than an ape's existed. Upright walking came
first, by two million years; the brain, the tools and the speech came
after, and not in a straight line but along a bush of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, most of
which left no descendants. This chapter applies the tools of the last
two --- shared characters, trees, dates, \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} --- to the lineage that
leads to us.

\section{Humans among the primates}

\begin{proposition}[Our place in the tree]\label{prop:g12:human-evolution:place}
Humans are primates: mammals with grasping hands, nails instead of
claws, forward-facing eyes and a large brain for their size. Within the
primates, the great apes --- orangutan, gorilla, chimpanzee, bonobo,
human --- form a \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}, and within it the chimpanzee and the bonobo
are the humans' sister group: our \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} differs from theirs at about
1.2\% of positions, and the common ancestor of the two lineages lived
some six to seven million years ago, in Africa. That ancestor was
neither a chimpanzee nor a human; both lineages have evolved since.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The character matrices and the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} comparisons of
\cref{ch:g12:phylogenetic-trees}: every \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} sequenced places the
chimpanzee closer to the human than to the gorilla, and the human
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} 2 is the fusion, end to end, of two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} that
remain separate in the chimpanzee --- which is why we have 23 pairs
and they 24. The molecular clock, calibrated on the fossil dates of
older primate splits, gives the human--chimpanzee node its date.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[The human lineage]\label{def:g12:human-evolution:lineage}
The \emph{human lineage}\index{human lineage} is the \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade} of all
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} closer to modern humans than to the chimpanzee: everything
descended from the human side of the six-million-year node. It
contains one living \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, \emph{Homo sapiens}, and a score of
fossil ones --- the australopithecines and the several \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} of the
genus \emph{Homo}. A fossil belongs to it if it shows derived
characters of the lineage: upright walking, a shortened face, small
canines, and later a large brain and tools.
\end{definition}

\section{The characters of the lineage}

\begin{proposition}[Bipedalism came first]\label{prop:g12:human-evolution:bipedalism}
The earliest derived character of the \omterm{def:g12:human-evolution:lineage}{human lineage} is habitual
walking on two legs. It shows in the skeleton: the opening at the base
of the skull through which the spinal cord passes sits under the skull
rather than behind it, so the head balances on a vertical spine; the
spine has a double curve; the pelvis is short and bowl-shaped; the
femurs angle inwards to the knee, bringing the feet under the body's
centre; the foot has an arch and a big toe in line with the others,
made for pushing off, not for grasping. All of these are present, with
a few ape-like traits, in fossils four million years old with brains
no larger than a chimpanzee's.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The footprint trails in volcanic ash, 3.6 million years old, show a
striding gait with heel strike and an arched foot. The skeleton of a
female australopithecine 3.2 million years old, 40\% complete, has a
human-like pelvis and knee and a brain of about \qty{400}{cm^3}. Skull
bases of the same age have the spinal opening forward, under the
skull. The characters appear in the order: walking, then, more than a
million years later, the growth of the brain.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Then the brain, the tools, the face]\label{prop:g12:human-evolution:brain}
From about 2.5 million years ago the lineage shows a second set of
derived characters: a brain growing from \qty{450}{cm^3} to
\qty{1350}{cm^3}, a face and jaws that shrink beneath it, a chin, and
stone tools --- first pebbles chipped to an edge, then shaped hand-axes,
then blades, points and hafted tools. Fire is controlled by a million
years ago, the dead are buried by a hundred thousand, and images are
painted by forty thousand. Brain and tools grow together, each making
the other useful, over two million years.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Reading a skull]\label{ex:g12:human-evolution:skull}
A fossil skull with a spinal opening underneath, a braincase of
\qty{450}{cm^3}, large jaws and a projecting face is an
australopithecine: upright, small-brained. One with \qty{900}{cm^3}, a
heavy brow ridge, a receding forehead and no chin is \emph{Homo
erectus}. One with \qty{1400}{cm^3}, a high forehead, a small face
tucked under the braincase and a chin is a modern human. The order of
these characters on the tree is the order in which they appear in the
rocks.
\end{example}

\section{A bush, not a ladder}

\begin{proposition}[Several species at once]\label{prop:g12:human-evolution:bush}
The \omterm{def:g12:human-evolution:lineage}{human lineage} is not a single line of ancestors and descendants
but a bush of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, several of which lived at the same time: two or
three kinds of australopithecine two million years ago beside the first
\emph{Homo}; \emph{Homo erectus} spread over three continents while
other \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} arose in Africa; and as recently as fifty thousand years
ago, modern humans shared the Earth with the Neanderthals in Europe,
another \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} in Asia, and a small-bodied \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} on an island.
Most branches of the bush ended in extinction; ours is the one that
remains.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Fossils of distinct \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} are found in layers of the same age at the
same sites. Neanderthal and modern human remains overlap in Europe for
several thousand years. The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} of Neanderthals and of the Asian
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}, recovered from their bones, is distinct from ours --- and
present in us: people outside Africa carry about 2\% of Neanderthal \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA},
and some Pacific \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} several per cent of the Asian
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}'s, the trace of interbreeding when the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} met. The
branches touched before they ended.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Modern humans]\label{prop:g12:human-evolution:sapiens}
\emph{Homo sapiens} appeared in Africa about 300\,000 years ago,
recognisable by its high, rounded skull, small face and chin. Small
groups left Africa some 60\,000 years ago and, within 50\,000 years,
had reached every continent but Antarctica, meeting and partly
absorbing the older \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} they found. All living humans descend
from that African \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}: the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic diversity} of the whole
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} is smaller than that of the chimpanzees of one African region,
and it is greatest in Africa, decreasing with distance from it along
the routes of dispersal --- the signature of successive founding
groups.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{What made us}

\begin{proposition}[Genes, and then culture]\label{prop:g12:human-evolution:culture}
The characters of the lineage arose as the previous chapters describe:
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}, including changes in the regulation of \omterm{prop:g12:diversification-of-life:development}{developmental genes}
(a longer growth of the brain, a shorter face), sorted by selection in
the environments of African savannas, and by drift in small
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}. But the lineage added a second mode of inheritance ---
the transmitted behaviour of \cref{ch:g12:diversification-of-life},
raised to a scale no other \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} reached: tools, fire, language,
cooperation, teaching. Culture accumulates within a lifetime and passes
to all who learn it, not only to descendants; for the last hundred
thousand years the changes that mattered most to humans have been
cultural, and the environment the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} evolves in is largely one
it made.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Placing a fossil]\label{met:g12:human-evolution:placing}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Date the layer (radioactive decay of volcanic minerals, or the
        known age of the strata).
  \item Read the gait: position of the spinal opening, shape of the
        pelvis and femur, the foot.
  \item Measure the braincase; note the face, the brow ridge, the
        chin, the teeth.
  \item List the derived states present and absent, and place the
        fossil on the tree by \cref{ch:g12:phylogenetic-trees}'s rules:
        as a side branch near the node where its combination of
        characters fits, not as "the ancestor".
  \item Where bone is preserved well enough, read the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} and compare
        it with living \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[No missing link]\label{rem:g12:human-evolution:link}
The phrase supposes a chain with one gap; the record is a bush with
many branches, most of them known from a few bones, and the "link"
between us and the chimpanzee is a node --- an extinct \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} that
was neither --- not a creature half-way between. What the fossils do
show, in the order the rocks preserve them, is each derived character
appearing in its turn: feet, then brains, then tools, then the
chin --- and in the last fifty thousand years, the works of a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
whose evolution is now mostly of its own making.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Walkers in the Ash}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- two trails of footprints read for
gait, height and speed; a row of skulls measured; and the DNA of a
living student traced to three populations}]
\label{pb:g12:human-evolution:1}
Two trails of footprints, 3.6 million years old, run parallel for
\qty{27}{m}. Trail A: footprint length \qty{21.5}{cm}, stride (from one
print to the next of the same foot) \qty{0.95}{m}. Trail B: length
\qty{18.5}{cm}, stride \qty{0.80}{m}. In modern humans the foot is
about 15\% of standing height, and a walking stride of about 0.8 times
the height corresponds to a slow walk.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The footprints.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Estimate the height of walker A and of walker B.
  \item Compare each stride with 0.8 times the height. Were they
        walking or running?
  \item The prints show a deep heel, an arch, and a big toe parallel
        to the others. What does each feature show about the foot and
        the gait?
  \item A chimpanzee walking upright leaves prints with a flat sole and
        a divergent big toe, and cannot keep it up for long. Which
        characters of \cref{prop:g12:human-evolution:bipedalism} do
        the trails establish for their makers, and which do they leave
        unknown?
  \item The nearest fossils of the same age have brains of
        \qty{400}{cm^3}. What does the combination --- these feet, that
        brain --- settle about the order of the lineage's characters?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The skulls.}
Five skulls, with braincase volume, age and features:
1: \qty{450}{cm^3}, 3.0 million years, projecting face, no chin.
2: \qty{650}{cm^3}, 1.9 million years, smaller face.
3: \qty{950}{cm^3}, 1.0 million years, heavy brow, no chin.
4: \qty{1450}{cm^3}, 60\,000 years, heavy brow, no chin, long low
skull.
5: \qty{1350}{cm^3}, 30\,000 years, high forehead, chin.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Assign each skull to a group of the timeline figure.
  \item Plot, or describe, brain volume against age for the five. Over
        which interval does the volume grow fastest?
  \item Skull 4 is larger than skull 5. Does brain volume alone
        identify a modern human? Which characters do?
  \item Skulls 4 and 5 overlap in time. What does the chapter say
        about the relation between their two \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}, and what
        evidence supports it?
  \item Skull 1 and the footprints of Part I could belong to the same
        \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}. Explain why that \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} is an ancestor-like

\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The student's genome.}
A student of European and Melanesian ancestry has her genome compared
with those of Neanderthals and of the extinct Asian \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}: 1.6\%
matches the Neanderthal genome, 1.8\% the Asian one.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Explain what a 1.6\% match means and what event it records.
  \item Compare her figures with the chart. Which of her ancestral
        \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} contributed each share?
  \item If interbreeding occurred 50\,000 years ago and a generation is
        25 years, how many generations separate her from the event?
        Why has the share not been diluted to nothing?
  \item Two lineages contributed \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} to hers although they are
        called \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}. What does this say about the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} boundary
        of \cref{ch:g12:selection-drift-speciation} at that time?
  \item Her mitochondrial \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is of the modern human type, as is every
        living person's. What does that show about the direction or
        the fate of the interbreeding?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The clock and the bush.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Human and chimpanzee \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} differ by 1.2\%; the primate clock of

  \item The oldest known fossils of the \omterm{def:g12:human-evolution:lineage}{human lineage} are 6 to 7
        million years old. Is that consistent with your date?
  \item List the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} of the timeline that were alive one million
        years ago, and state how many of them have living descendants.
  \item Explain why the last hundred thousand years of the lineage are
        better described by cultural change than by the characters of

  \item State the result: the height and the gait of walker A, the
        order in which feet, brain and chin appeared, and the number
