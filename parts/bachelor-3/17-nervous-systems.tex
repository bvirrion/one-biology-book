\chapter{Organization of Nervous Systems}\label{ch:b3:nervous-systems}

A jellyfish has a few thousand neurons in a net with no centre and can
swim, feed and right itself. An octopus has half a billion, two thirds
of them in its arms, each of which can solve a problem the brain has
not been told about. You have eighty-six billion, joined by a hundred
trillion synapses, running on twenty watts --- a fifth of your energy
for two per cent of your mass --- and Santiago Ram\'on y Cajal, drawing
them cell by cell in the 1890s from silver-stained slices, saw that
they were separate cells that touched without fusing and that signals
flowed through them in one direction. The previous volume treated the
neuron: its resting potential, its action potential, its synapse. This
chapter treats what neurons are wired into: the passive cable that
limits how far a signal spreads and how fast, the circuits --- reflex,
oscillator, inhibitory surround --- from which behaviour is assembled,
the plan of the vertebrate nervous system and of the \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{glia} that keep it
running, its energy budget, and the ways different animals have
distributed their neurons.

\section{Neurons and glia}

\begin{definition}[The cells of the nervous system]\label{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}
\index{neuron!types}\index{glia}\index{astrocyte}\index{oligodendrocyte}\index{myelin}\index{microglia}\index{interneuron}
Neurons come in a few architectures that recur across the brain:
\emph{pyramidal} cells, the excitatory projection neurons of the
cortex, with a long apical dendrite and an axon that may travel a
metre; \emph{Purkinje} cells of the \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{cerebellum}, whose flat dendritic
tree receives two hundred thousand synapses; sensory neurons with one
process to the periphery and one to the cord; and \emph{interneurons},
short-axon cells that stay within a region, most of them inhibitory.
About four in five cortical neurons release glutamate and excite; one
in five release GABA and inhibit. The \emph{glia}, as numerous as the
neurons, do the rest. \emph{Astrocytes} wrap synapses and blood vessels:
they take up the potassium that firing neurons release and the
glutamate that synapses spill, supply lactate, regulate blood flow
locally, and induce and maintain the blood--brain barrier.
\emph{Oligodendrocytes} in the central nervous system and \emph{Schwann
cells} in the periphery wrap axons in \emph{myelin}, dozens of layers
of membrane that insulate the axon between the nodes where the channels
sit. \emph{Microglia} are the brain's resident \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{macrophages}, pruning
synapses in development and clearing debris. Ependymal cells line the
ventricles and make the \omterm{prop:b3:nervous-systems:barrier-budget}{cerebrospinal fluid}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Golgi's silver chromate stain (1873) blackened, for unknown reasons,
about one neuron in a hundred, completely, leaving the rest invisible
--- so that a single cell could be seen whole in a tangle of millions.
Golgi read his own preparations as a continuous reticulum; Cajal, from
1888, using the same stain on embryonic tissue where the cells are
simpler, saw that each cell ended freely, that dendrites and axon
differed, and that axons ended on the dendrites and bodies of other
cells without joining them. From the arrangement of sensory and motor
cells he inferred the direction of flow --- dendrite to body to axon ---
the \emph{law of dynamic polarisation}. Sherrington named the gap the
\emph{synapse} in 1897; the electron microscope showed it in 1954.
\end{proof}

\section{The neuron as a cable}

\begin{theorem}[The steady-state cable equation]\label{thm:b3:nervous-systems:cable}
\index{cable equation}\index{length constant}\index{time constant!membrane}\index{conduction velocity}
Model a dendrite or unmyelinated axon as a cylinder of diameter $d$
whose cytoplasm has resistivity $R_{i}$ and whose membrane has specific
resistance $R_{m}$ (resistance times area) and capacitance $C_{m}$ per
area. Per unit length the axial resistance is $r_{i} = 4R_{i}/\pi d^{2}$
and the membrane resistance $r_{m} = R_{m}/\pi d$. A steady voltage
$V_{0}$ applied at $x = 0$ spreads along the fibre as
\[
V(x) = V_{0}\,e^{-x/\lambda}, \qquad
\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{r_{m}}{r_{i}}} = \sqrt{\frac{R_{m}\,d}{4R_{i}}},
\]
where $\lambda$ is the \emph{\omterm{thm:b3:nervous-systems:cable}{length constant}}: the distance over which
a passive signal falls to $1/e$, growing with the square root of the
diameter. The membrane charges with the \emph{time constant} $\tau =
R_{m}C_{m}$, independent of geometry. An action potential, which
regenerates itself, travels along an unmyelinated axon at a speed
proportional to $\lambda/\tau$ and hence to $\sqrt{d}$; along a
myelinated axon, where it jumps from node to node, at a speed
proportional to $d$ --- about \qty{6}{m/s} per micrometre of diameter.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $I(x)$ be the axial current. Ohm's law along the core gives
$\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}x = -r_{i}I$; conservation of charge at steady
state says that the axial current lost between $x$ and $x + \mathrm{d}x$
leaks through the membrane, $\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}x = -V/r_{m}$.
Differentiating the first and substituting the second,
$\mathrm{d}^{2}V/\mathrm{d}x^{2} = (r_{i}/r_{m})V = V/\lambda^{2}$, a
linear second-order equation whose solution bounded as $x \to \infty$ is
$V_{0}e^{-x/\lambda}$. Substituting $r_{m} = R_{m}/\pi d$ and $r_{i} =
4R_{i}/\pi d^{2}$ gives $\lambda^{2} = R_{m}d/4R_{i}$. The time constant
is that of a resistor and capacitor in parallel, $r_{m}c_{m} =
(R_{m}/\pi d)(C_{m}\pi d) = R_{m}C_{m}$. For the velocities: a
regenerating wave must charge the membrane a \omterm{thm:b3:nervous-systems:cable}{length constant} ahead in
about a time constant, so $v \sim \lambda/\tau \propto \sqrt{d}$;
between nodes of a myelinated axon the internodal membrane has a
resistance raised and a capacitance lowered by the hundred-odd layers of
\omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{myelin}, the internode length scales with $d$, and the result is
$v \propto d$. The proportionality constants are admitted.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Numbers for a dendrite and two axons]\label{ex:b3:nervous-systems:numbers}
A dendrite of $d = \qty{2}{\micro m}$ with $R_{m} = \qty{2e4}{\ohm.cm^2}$
and $R_{i} = \qty{100}{\ohm.cm}$: $\lambda = \sqrt{2\times 10^{4}\times
2\times 10^{-4}/400}\ \unit{cm} = \qty{0.1}{cm} = \qty{1}{mm}$ --- a
synaptic potential at the tip of a millimetre-long dendrite reaches the
cell body at a third of its size, which is why the geometry of a
dendritic tree is part of its computation. $\tau = 2\times 10^{4}\times
10^{-6}\,\unit{F/cm^2}\cdot\unit{\ohm.cm^2} = \qty{20}{ms}$, the window
within which inputs must arrive to sum. A squid giant axon of
\qty{500}{\micro m}, unmyelinated, conducts at about \qty{25}{m/s}; to do
the same a vertebrate uses a myelinated axon of \qty{4}{\micro m},
fifteen thousand times smaller in cross-section --- the reason a
vertebrate nerve can carry ten thousand fast fibres in the space of one
squid axon. A \qty{20}{\micro m} motor axon conducts at
\qty{120}{m/s}; when multiple sclerosis strips its \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{myelin} the velocity
falls tenfold and conduction may fail altogether at the bare stretches.
\end{example}

\section{Circuits}

\begin{definition}[Elementary circuits]\label{def:b3:nervous-systems:circuits}
\index{reflex arc}\index{reciprocal inhibition}\index{feedforward inhibition}\index{feedback inhibition}\index{convergence}\index{divergence}
A \emph{reflex arc} is the shortest path from a sensor to an effector:
in the knee jerk, a muscle-spindle afferent enters the cord and
synapses directly on the motor neurons of the same muscle, which
contract it about \qty{30}{ms} after the tap --- one synapse, no brain.
The same afferent excites an inhibitory \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{interneuron} to the motor
neurons of the opposing muscle (\emph{reciprocal inhibition}), so that
the extensor's contraction is not fought by the flexor. Most circuits
are built from a few \omterm{def:b3:bioinformatics:motif}{motifs}: \emph{divergence}, one neuron driving
many, and \emph{convergence}, many onto one, which sums evidence;
\emph{feedforward inhibition}, in which an input excites both a target
and an \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{interneuron} that inhibits the target a millisecond later,
sharpening the response in time; \emph{feedback inhibition}, in which
the target's own output excites an \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{interneuron} that inhibits it,
limiting firing and, spread to neighbours, producing the lateral
inhibition that sharpens contrast (\cref{ch:b3:sensory-systems}); and
\emph{disinhibition}, inhibiting an inhibitor, the basal ganglia's way
of releasing a movement.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Central pattern generators]\label{prop:b3:nervous-systems:cpg}
\index{central pattern generator}\index{half-centre oscillator}
Rhythmic movements --- breathing, walking, swimming, chewing --- are
produced by circuits in the \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{brainstem} and \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{spinal cord} that oscillate on
their own, \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:nervous-systems:cpg}{central pattern generators}}, which sensory feedback
adjusts but does not create: a cat's \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{spinal cord} cut from the brain and
deprived of its sensory nerves still produces the alternating pattern
of stepping. The simplest design is Brown's \emph{half-centre} (1911):
two groups of neurons that each inhibit the other. Whichever fires
silences its partner; but a firing group tires --- its channels
inactivate, its inhibition of the partner weakens --- and the partner,
released, fires and in turn silences the first: alternation, with a
period set by the time constant of the fatigue, not by any clock. The
respiratory rhythm arises in a cluster of a few thousand neurons in the
medulla (the pre-B\"otzinger complex), which fires in bursts from birth
to death; the lamprey's swimming is a chain of half-centres along its
cord, each delayed on the last so that a wave travels tailward.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Tracing a circuit]\label{met:b3:nervous-systems:tracing}
\index{circuit tracing}\index{optogenetics}
To establish that neuron A drives neuron B and that the pathway
matters for a behaviour: (1) anatomy --- inject a tracer that travels
along axons (a dye, a modified rabies \omterm{def:b3:virology:virus}{virus} that crosses one synapse
backward) and find the connected cells; (2) physiology --- record from B
while stimulating A, and measure the latency (a monosynaptic connection
gives a fixed delay of about a millisecond) and the sign (excitation or
inhibition); (3) necessity --- silence A, by lesion, by cooling, or by
expressing in it a light-driven chloride pump and shining light
(\emph{optogenetics}), and see whether the behaviour fails; (4)
sufficiency --- activate A alone, with a light-driven cation channel,
and see whether the behaviour appears; (5) in an intact animal, image
the activity of the whole population with a calcium indicator while
the behaviour is performed. A circuit is established when all five
agree; most published circuits have two or three.
\end{method}

\section{The plan of the vertebrate nervous system}

\begin{definition}[Central and peripheral divisions]\label{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}
\index{spinal cord}\index{brainstem}\index{cerebellum}\index{thalamus}\index{hypothalamus}\index{cerebral cortex}\index{autonomic nervous system}\index{enteric nervous system}
The \emph{central nervous system} is the brain and spinal cord; the
\emph{peripheral} is everything else. The \emph{spinal cord} receives
sensory axons through its dorsal roots and sends motor axons through
its ventral roots, its grey matter a butterfly of cell bodies, its
white matter the myelinated tracts running up and down. The
\emph{brainstem} --- medulla, pons, midbrain --- holds the nuclei of
the cranial nerves and the centres that keep breathing, heart rate and
blood pressure going without thought; a brainstem death is death. The
\emph{cerebellum}, with more neurons than the rest of the brain, tunes
the timing and coordination of movement and learns from error. The
\emph{thalamus} relays every sense but smell to the cortex and the
cortex's answers back; the \emph{hypothalamus} beneath it runs
homeostasis --- temperature, hunger, thirst, the clock, the endocrine
axes of \cref{ch:b3:endocrinology}. The \emph{basal ganglia} select
among possible actions; the \emph{hippocampus} makes memories
(\cref{ch:b3:learning-memory}); and the \emph{cerebral cortex}, a sheet
of six layers of cells two to four millimetres thick and a quarter of a
square metre in area, folded to fit, does the rest, in areas mapped for
each sense and movement and in association areas between them. The
peripheral system's \emph{autonomic} division runs the organs: the
\emph{sympathetic} chain, releasing noradrenaline, mobilises (heart up,
gut down, pupils wide); the \emph{parasympathetic} nerves, releasing
acetylcholine, conserve and digest; and the \emph{enteric} nervous
system, half a billion neurons in the gut wall, runs digestion on its
own.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The blood--brain barrier and the brain's budget]\label{prop:b3:nervous-systems:barrier-budget}
\index{blood-brain barrier}\index{cerebrospinal fluid}\index{brain energy budget}
The capillaries of the brain are sealed by tight junctions between
their endothelial cells, wrapped in \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{astrocyte} endfeet, and equipped
with transporters that admit glucose and amino acids and pump out
much else: the \emph{blood--brain barrier}, which keeps the ionic
composition of the brain's extracellular fluid constant for the sake of
the neurons' electrical behaviour, and which excludes most drugs
(\qty{98}{\%} of small molecules, all antibodies) --- the central
problem of neuropharmacology. The brain floats in \qty{150}{mL} of
\emph{\omterm{prop:b3:nervous-systems:barrier-budget}{cerebrospinal fluid}}, made at half a millilitre a minute and
renewed four times a day, which cushions it and carries away waste,
especially during sleep. The brain's cost: some \qty{20}{W} in an adult,
\qty{20}{\%} of the resting metabolism, almost all as glucose burnt to
ATP, and most of that ATP spent by the sodium pump restoring the
gradients that action potentials and synaptic currents run down. At
\qty{50}{kJ} per mole of ATP, \qty{20}{W} is $4\times 10^{-4}$ mol
of ATP per second, $2.4\times 10^{20}$ molecules; divided among
$8.6\times 10^{10}$ neurons, about $3\times 10^{9}$ ATP per neuron per
second. A cortical action potential with its synaptic consequences costs
of the order of $10^{9}$ ATP, so the budget allows an average firing
rate of a few spikes per second per neuron --- and cortical neurons do
fire at about that rate. The brain is built to a strict energy limit,
which is why its signals are sparse, its axons thin, and its
information coded by few spikes in many cells.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Nervous systems across animals}

\begin{definition}[Nerve nets, ganglia and brains]\label{def:b3:nervous-systems:comparative}
\index{nerve net}\index{ganglion}\index{cephalisation}\index{encephalisation}
Cnidarians (jellyfish, hydra) have a \emph{nerve net}: neurons
distributed through the body wall, connected in every direction, with
ring-shaped condensations around the bell margin that coordinate
swimming --- no centre, and conduction in both directions along each
cell. Bilaterian animals concentrate neurons into \emph{ganglia} ---
clusters with a shared neuropil --- arranged along a ventral cord in
arthropods and annelids, with a head ganglion enlarged where the sense
organs are: \emph{cephalisation}. Molluscs vary from the simple ganglia
of a clam to the octopus, whose half-billion neurons are the most of
any invertebrate and two thirds of which lie in the arms, each arm's
cord controlling its own reaching and grasping. Vertebrates build a
dorsal hollow tube, expanded at the front into the brain of the
previous section, protected by bone and a barrier, and myelinated. The
same signalling molecules, channels, transmitters and many of the same
developmental genes (the Hox code of the hindbrain, the eye-patterning
factors) run all of them: the parts are ancient and shared, the
arrangements diverse.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Counting neurons]\label{ex:b3:nervous-systems:counts}
A nematode has $302$ neurons, every one named and every synapse
mapped; a fruit fly $1.4\times 10^{5}$; a honeybee $10^{6}$; a mouse
$7\times 10^{7}$; an octopus $5\times 10^{8}$; a human $8.6\times
10^{10}$; an elephant $2.6\times 10^{11}$, most of them in its
\omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{cerebellum}. Brain mass scales with body mass across mammals roughly as
$M_{\text{brain}} \propto M_{\text{body}}^{0.75}$, so a mouse's brain
is a larger fraction of its body than an elephant's; species above the
line for their size --- dolphins, primates, humans by a factor of
seven --- are said to be encephalised. What tracks cognition best is
not brain mass but the number of neurons in the \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{cerebral cortex}: about
$1.6\times 10^{10}$ in a human, $5.6\times 10^{9}$ in the elephant with
its three-times-larger brain, $2\times 10^{9}$ in a chimpanzee. The
human brain is not special in its cells or its plan; it has more
cortical neurons than any other, packed more densely, and it is the
number, not the weight, that seems to count.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[What a nervous system is for]\label{rem:b3:nervous-systems:for}
Plants, fungi and sponges have none; an animal that moves needs one,
and the elaboration of nervous systems tracks the demands of movement,
of sensing at a distance, and of predicting what will happen next. The
\omterm{thm:b3:nervous-systems:cable}{cable equation} sets the physics --- how far and how fast a signal
travels for a given diameter and insulation; the energy budget sets the
economics; and within those limits the same neurons, arranged as nets,
ganglia or a cortex, produce a jellyfish's pulse, an octopus's
camouflage or this sentence. The next two chapters take the input end
and the storage: how the senses turn the world into spikes, and how
synapses change so that the world, once met, is remembered.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

What did Golgi's stain make possible, what did Cajal conclude from it,

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:3}
Describe the knee-jerk \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:circuits}{reflex arc} and explain why it takes only
\qty{30}{ms} while a voluntary kick takes ten times longer.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:4}
List the sympathetic and parasympathetic effects on the heart, the
pupil, the gut and the airways, and the transmitter each division uses
at the target.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:5}
Compute the \omterm{thm:b3:nervous-systems:cable}{length constant} of an axon of \qty{1}{\micro m} with $R_{m}
= \qty{1e4}{\ohm.cm^2}$ and $R_{i} = \qty{100}{\ohm.cm}$, and the
fraction of a signal remaining after \qty{2}{mm}. What diameter would
double $\lambda$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:6}
A sensory axon of \qty{12}{\micro m} runs \qty{1.2}{m} from the toe to
the cord, and a motor axon of \qty{15}{\micro m} runs back. With
\qty{6}{m/s} per micrometre and \qty{0.5}{ms} per synapse, estimate the
minimum latency of a spinal withdrawal reflex with one \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{interneuron}.
How long would it take with unmyelinated axons of the same diameter at
$v = 1.1\sqrt{d}$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:7}
Using the brain's budget from
\cref{prop:b3:nervous-systems:barrier-budget}, estimate how many ATP a
neuron can spend per second, and, if a spike with its synapses costs
$2\times 10^{9}$ ATP, the mean firing rate the budget allows. What does
this predict about the code?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:8}
Explain why a \omterm{prop:b3:nervous-systems:cpg}{half-centre oscillator} needs fatigue (or another slow
process) to oscillate, and what would happen with two mutually
inhibitory neurons that never tire.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:9}
The blood--brain barrier excludes L-dopa's product dopamine but admits

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:10}
Derive the \omterm{thm:b3:nervous-systems:cable}{cable equation}'s time-dependent form, $\lambda^{2}\,\partial^{2}
V/\partial x^{2} = \tau\,\partial V/\partial t + V$, by adding the
capacitive current to the membrane current, and show that the
steady-state solution of the theorem follows. (State the form; solving

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:11}

Using $v \propto \sqrt{d}$, what diameter of unmyelinated axon would be

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:nervous-systems:12}

\section{Problem: Wiring a Body}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a body's wiring costed in
length constants, milliseconds and watts: the cable numbers of a
dendrite and two axons, the latency of a reflex from toe to cord and
back, the energy the brain can afford per spike, and the scaling of
brains across animals, ending on the length constant, the reflex
latency and the mean firing rate a brain can pay for}]
\label{pb:b3:nervous-systems:1}
Data: $R_{m} = \qty{2e4}{\ohm.cm^2}$, $R_{i} = \qty{100}{\ohm.cm}$,
$C_{m} = \qty{1}{\micro F/cm^2}$. Myelinated velocity \qty{6}{m/s} per
micrometre; unmyelinated $v = 1.1\sqrt{d}$ m/s with $d$ in
micrometres; synaptic delay \qty{0.5}{ms}. Brain: \qty{20}{W},
$8.6\times 10^{10}$ neurons, $10^{14}$ synapses, ATP \qty{50}{kJ/mol};
a spike costs $4\times 10^{8}$ ATP and each synaptic transmission
$2\times 10^{4}$ ATP; a neuron has \num{7000} synapses. Scaling:
$M_{\text{brain}} = a\,M_{\text{body}}^{0.75}$ with a \qty{30}{g} mouse
having a \qty{0.4}{g} brain.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Cables.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute $\lambda$ for dendrites of \qty{1}{\micro m} and
        \qty{4}{\micro m}, and $\tau$.
  \item A synapse \qty{300}{\micro m} from the soma on the
        \qty{1}{\micro m} dendrite produces \qty{5}{mV} locally. What
        arrives at the soma? On the \qty{4}{\micro m} dendrite?
  \item Explain why a neuron can weight its inputs by where on the
        tree they land, and why inputs must arrive within about $\tau$
        to sum.
  \item Compute the velocity of a \qty{10}{\micro m} myelinated axon,
        and of an unmyelinated axon of the same diameter. What diameter
        of unmyelinated axon would match the myelinated one?
  \item Cross-sectional areas of the \qty{10}{\micro m} axon and of the
        unmyelinated equivalent. How many myelinated axons fit in the
        space of one equivalent unmyelinated axon?
  \item A demyelinating disease removes the \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{myelin} from a
        \qty{10}{\micro m} axon over \qty{2}{cm}. Estimate the extra
        delay over that stretch if it conducts as an unmyelinated axon
        of that diameter, and say why conduction may fail altogether.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Latencies.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A toe touches a hot surface. The sensory axon (\qty{10}{\micro m},
        \qty{1.2}{m}) reaches the cord, one \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{interneuron}, and a motor axon
        (\qty{15}{\micro m}, \qty{1.1}{m}) returns to the leg. Minimum
        latency of the withdrawal?
  \item The signal also travels up a \qty{10}{\micro m} axon
        \qty{0.6}{m} to the brain and through two more synapses before
        pain is felt. When is the pain felt relative to the withdrawal?
  \item A pain fibre is unmyelinated, \qty{1}{\micro m}. How long does
        its signal take to travel the \qty{1.8}{m} to the brain? Explain
        the two phases of pain from a stubbed toe.
  \item A giraffe's leg is \qty{2}{m} longer. Recompute the reflex
        latency and comment on the problems of size.
  \item The knee jerk is monosynaptic, \qty{30}{ms} in an adult. Using
        \qty{0.8}{m} each way at \qty{80}{m/s}, one synapse and a
        neuromuscular junction (\qty{1}{ms}), and \qty{5}{ms} for the
        muscle to develop force, account for the \qty{30}{ms}.
  \item A newborn's axons are unmyelinated. Explain why its reflexes are
        slow and its movements uncoordinated, and what myelination
        during the first two years changes.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The budget.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Convert \qty{20}{W} into ATP per second, and into ATP per neuron
        per second.
  \item A neuron fires one spike: it costs $4\times 10^{8}$ ATP itself
        and drives \num{7000} synapses at $2\times 10^{4}$ each. Total
        cost of one spike with its consequences?
  \item What mean firing rate can the budget sustain if all of it went
        on spikes? If half goes to resting costs?
  \item Cortical neurons fire at about \qty{1}{Hz} on average. What
        fraction of the budget is that, and what does it imply about
        how information is coded?
  \item A brain that fired every neuron at \qty{50}{Hz} would need how
        many watts? Compare with the whole body's \qty{100}{W}.
  \item Glucose supplies the brain at \qty{5}{g/h}. How many watts is
        that (\qty{16}{kJ/g})? Is it enough, and what happens to
        consciousness within seconds of the supply failing?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Scaling.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Find $a$ from the mouse, then predict the brain mass of a
        \qty{70}{kg} mammal and of a \qty{5000}{kg} elephant on the
        line.
  \item Actual values: human \qty{1350}{g}, elephant \qty{4800}{g}.
        Compute each species' ratio to the prediction (the
        \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:comparative}{encephalisation} quotient).
  \item The elephant's brain has $2.6\times 10^{11}$ neurons but
        $5.6\times 10^{9}$ in the cortex; the human $8.6\times 10^{10}$
        and $1.6\times 10^{10}$. Where are the elephant's neurons, and
        what does that suggest about what cortex does?
  \item A neuron's volume grows with its axon's length, so bigger
        brains have bigger, sparser neurons. Explain why doubling brain
        mass does not double neuron number, and why primates, whose
        neurons stay small as the brain grows, gain more neurons per
        gram than rodents.
  \item An octopus has $5\times 10^{8}$ neurons, two thirds in its arms.
        Propose what an arm can and cannot do without the brain, and
        one experiment to test it.
  \item The nematode \emph{C.~elegans} has $302$ neurons and about
        \num{7000} synapses in all, every one mapped. Compare its
        synapses per neuron with the human figure, and say what a
        complete wiring diagram can and cannot tell us about behaviour.
  \item Summarise: the \omterm{thm:b3:nervous-systems:cable}{length constant} of the \qty{1}{\micro m} dendrite
        (question~1), the withdrawal reflex latency (question~7), and
        the mean firing rate the brain's budget allows with half its
        energy on spikes (question~15).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
