\chapter{DNA Replication and Mitosis}\label{ch:b1:replication-mitosis}

Every eight hours or so, a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} of the intestinal lining copies six
billion \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base pairs} with about one error in a billion, then sorts the
two copies into two daughters so that each gets exactly forty-six
\omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} --- not forty-five, not forty-seven. The copying is done
by a machine that reads one strand and builds its complement at fifty
letters a second from tens of thousands of starting points at once;
the sorting is done by a \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{spindle} of \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:cytoskeleton}{microtubules} that pulls the two
copies of every \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} apart with an accuracy of one mistake in
a hundred thousand divisions. This chapter describes the \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:fork}{replication
fork} and its \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzymes}, the problem of the ends, the \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:cycle}{cell cycle} that
times replication and division, and \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{mitosis}, the division itself.

\section{Semi-conservative replication}

\begin{proposition}[Each strand is a template]\label{prop:b1:replication-mitosis:semiconservative}
\index{semi-conservative replication}
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} is replicated \emph{semi-conservatively}: the two strands of the
helix separate, and each serves as a template on which a new
complementary strand is built, so that every daughter molecule is one
old strand paired with one new one.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Meselson and Stahl (1958; recalled from the High School volume) grew
\emph{E. coli} on heavy nitrogen, transferred it to light nitrogen,
and separated the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} by density: after one generation all the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}
was of intermediate density, after two, half intermediate and half
light --- exactly the prediction of one old and one new strand per
molecule, and of no other scheme. Cairns (1963) labelled replicating
bacterial \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} with tritium and photographed them by
autoradiography: circles with a replicating ``bubble'' whose two forks
moved in opposite directions from one origin. Kornberg (1956) purified
an \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme} that synthesised \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} in the test tube from the four
nucleoside triphosphates and a template, in the template's sequence.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[The replication fork and its enzymes]\label{def:b1:replication-mitosis:fork}
\index{replication fork}\index{DNA polymerase}\index{Okazaki fragment}
Replication begins at an \emph{origin} --- one on the bacterial
\omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome}, tens of thousands on a eukaryotic \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genome} --- where the
helix is opened and two \emph{replication forks} move away in
opposite directions. At each fork: a \emph{helicase} unwinds the helix
(one \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} per \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base pair}), \emph{single-strand binding \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins}} keep the
separated strands apart, and a \emph{topoisomerase} ahead of the fork
relieves the twist that unwinding builds up. \emph{\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} polymerase}
adds \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} only to the $3'$ hydroxyl of an existing chain, so
that every new strand grows $5' \to 3'$ and none can start from
nothing: a \emph{primase} first lays down a short \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} \emph{primer}
that the polymerase extends. On the template read $3' \to 5'$ the new
strand grows continuously toward the fork: the \emph{leading strand}.
On the other template the new strand must grow away from the fork, in
pieces of \qtyrange{1000}{2000}{} \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} in bacteria and
\qtyrange{100}{200}{} in eukaryotes, the \emph{Okazaki fragments},
each with its own primer: the \emph{lagging strand}. A second
polymerase replaces the primers with \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} and a \emph{ligase} seals the
fragments into one strand.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Fidelity]\label{prop:b1:replication-mitosis:fidelity}
\index{proofreading}
Base pairing alone would give about one error in $10^5$; the
polymerase \emph{proofreads} --- a $3' \to 5'$ exonuclease activity
removes a mispaired \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide} before the next is added --- bringing
the error to one in $10^7$; and after the fork has passed, a
\emph{mismatch repair} system finds the remaining mispairs, identifies
the new strand, and corrects it: one error in $10^9$ to $10^{10}$ \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base
pairs} per replication. A human \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} copying $\num{6.4e9}$ pairs makes
a handful of mutations each time it divides; a bacterium, one in a
thousand divisions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Speed and starting points]\label{ex:b1:replication-mitosis:speed}
A bacterial fork moves at \qty{1000}{} \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base pairs} a second: two forks
copy the \qty{4.6}{Mb} of \emph{E. coli} in \qty{40}{min}, and in rich
medium, where the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} divides every twenty, a new round starts before
the last has finished. A eukaryotic fork, slowed by chromatin, moves
at \qty{50}{} a second: copying a human \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} of \qty{250}{Mb}
from one origin would take a month, so the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genome} is copied from some
\num{40000} origins in eight hours, in an order fixed for each \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}
type.
\end{example}

\section{The ends}

\begin{proposition}[The end-replication problem and telomerase]\label{prop:b1:replication-mitosis:telomerase}
\index{telomerase}
On a linear \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} the lagging strand cannot be completed to the
very end: when the last primer is removed there is no $3'$ hydroxyl
upstream from which to fill the gap, and each replication would
shorten the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} by \qtyrange{50}{100}{} \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides}. Eukaryotes
end their \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} with \emph{\omterm{def:b1:genomes:karyotype}{telomeres}}, thousands of copies of a
short repeat (TTAGGG in vertebrates) that carry no \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes}, and germ
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} and stem \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} carry \emph{telomerase}, an \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme} with its own
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} template that adds repeats to the $3'$ end, restoring what
replication loses. Most somatic \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} lack it: their \omterm{def:b1:genomes:karyotype}{telomeres} shorten
at every division, and after some fifty divisions in culture they stop
dividing. Bacteria, with circular \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes}, have no ends.
\end{proposition}

\section{The cell cycle}

\begin{definition}[The cell cycle]\label{def:b1:replication-mitosis:cycle}
\index{cell cycle}\index{interphase}
The \emph{\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} cycle} of a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{eukaryotic cell} has four phases.
\emph{G$_1$} (gap 1): the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} grows and makes the \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzymes} of
replication; \emph{S} (synthesis): it replicates its \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}, so that each
\omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} becomes two identical \emph{sister \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{chromatids}} joined at
the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:karyotype}{centromere}; \emph{G$_2$}: it grows further and checks the copies;
\emph{M}: \emph{\omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{mitosis}}, the division of the nucleus, followed by
\emph{cytokinesis}, the division of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cytoplasm}. G$_1$, S and G$_2$
together are \emph{interphase}, in which the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} are extended
and active; a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} that stops dividing \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} the cycle from G$_1$
into a resting state, G$_0$. A dividing human \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} takes about
\qty{24}{h}: G$_1$ \qty{10}{h}, S \qty{8}{h}, G$_2$ \qty{4}{h}, M
\qty{1}{h}; a yeast, \qty{90}{min}; an early frog embryo, thirty
minutes with no gaps at all.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Checkpoints]\label{prop:b1:replication-mitosis:checkpoints}
\index{checkpoint}
The cycle is driven forward by a family of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} kinases, activated
in turn by \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} called \emph{cyclins} whose levels rise and fall
through the cycle, and it is held at \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:replication-mitosis:checkpoints}{checkpoints}} until
conditions are met: at the end of G$_1$, the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} commits to
replicate only if it is large enough, nourished and signalled to
divide, and if its \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} is undamaged; at the end of G$_2$, it enters
\omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{mitosis} only when replication is complete; in \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{mitosis}, the \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{chromatids}
separate only when every \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} is attached to the \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{spindle} from
both sides. Damage arrests the cycle and, if it cannot be repaired,
triggers the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s death. The molecular machinery of this control
belongs to the Year 3 volume; its failure is cancer.
\end{proposition}

\section{Mitosis}

\begin{definition}[Mitosis]\label{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}
\index{mitosis}\index{spindle}\index{chromatid}
\emph{Mitosis} distributes the replicated \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} so that each
daughter nucleus receives one chromatid of every \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} --- the
same number and the same \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes} as the parent. Its stages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{prophase}: the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} condense into visible rods,
        each of two sister chromatids held together along their length
        by cohesin \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins}; the two \emph{\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:cytoskeleton}{centrosomes}} move apart and
        the \emph{mitotic spindle} of \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:cytoskeleton}{microtubules} grows between them;
  \item \emph{prometaphase}: the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:nucleus}{nuclear envelope} breaks down;
        spindle \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:cytoskeleton}{microtubules} attach to each chromatid's
        \emph{kinetochore}, a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} structure at the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:karyotype}{centromere}, from
        opposite poles;
  \item \emph{metaphase}: the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} are aligned at the equator
        of the spindle, each under tension from both poles;
  \item \emph{anaphase}: the cohesin is cut, the sister chromatids
        separate and are pulled to opposite poles as the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:cytoskeleton}{microtubules}
        shorten (anaphase A) and the poles move apart (anaphase B);
  \item \emph{telophase}: the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} decondense, and a \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:nucleus}{nuclear
        envelope} re-forms around each set.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Cytokinesis} then divides the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cytoplasm}: in animal \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} a
\emph{contractile ring} of actin and myosin pinches the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} in two;
in plant \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} vesicles from the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:endomembrane}{Golgi} fuse at the equator into a
\emph{\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} plate} that grows outward into a new wall.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[What mitosis conserves]\label{prop:b1:replication-mitosis:conserves}
\omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{Mitosis} is a division that conserves the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} number: a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}
with 2n \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} (46 in a human) gives two \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} with 2n. The \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}
content goes from $2q$ (after replication) to $q$ in each daughter;
the number of \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes} never changes, because a \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} is
counted by its \omterm{def:b1:genomes:karyotype}{centromere}, and each \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{chromatid}, once separated, is a
\omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome}. Every \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} of a body is thus genetically identical to the
egg it descends from, apart from the mutations that copying has
introduced. The division that halves the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} number, meiosis,
belongs to the Year 2 volume.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Reading a cell-cycle experiment]\label{met:b1:replication-mitosis:reading}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Measure the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} per \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} (a dye whose fluorescence is
        proportional to \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}, \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} by \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}): \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} at $q$ are in G$_1$,
        at $2q$ in G$_2$ or M, in between in S. The fractions give the
        durations: a phase's share of the cycle is its share of the
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
  \item Give a pulse of labelled \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide} (bromodeoxyuridine): only
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} in S phase take it up; the labelled fraction is the S
        fraction, and following the label into \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{mitosis} gives the length
        of G$_2$.
  \item Count mitotic figures in a stained section: the \emph{mitotic
        index}, the fraction of \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} in M, is the fraction of the
        cycle that M occupies --- \qty{4}{\%} for a \qty{24}{h} cycle with
        a one-hour \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{mitosis}.
  \item Block a step (a drug that depolymerises \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:cytoskeleton}{microtubules} arrests
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} in metaphase; one that blocks \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} synthesis arrests them
        in S) and count what accumulates.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Turnover]\label{ex:b1:replication-mitosis:turnover}
The \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} of the small intestine is renewed every four days,
the skin every month, the red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} every four months from the marrow,
which produces two million a second; a liver \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} divides once a year
or so and a \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:nervous}{neuron} never. A tumour's \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} have lost the \omterm{prop:b1:replication-mitosis:checkpoints}{checkpoints}:
they divide when they should not, tolerate mis-segregated \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosomes},
and accumulate the mutations that let them divide faster still.
Chemotherapy exploits the difference: drugs that poison the \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:mitosis}{spindle} or
the polymerase kill the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} that divide most.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: Copying a Human Genome}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- an S phase of eight hours: forks
timed, origins counted, fragments and primers tallied, errors reckoned,
and a bacterium compared, ending on the minimum number of origins}]
\label{pb:b1:replication-mitosis:1}
A human \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} replicates $\num{6.4e9}$ \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base pairs} in an S phase of
\qty{8}{h}. A eukaryotic fork moves at \qty{50}{bp/s}; a bacterial
fork at \qty{1000}{bp/s}. \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:fork}{Okazaki fragments} are \qty{150}{}
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} in eukaryotes and \qty{1500}{} in bacteria. Each
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide} added costs two \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} equivalents (the triphosphate
precursor). Error rates per base: $10^{-5}$ for the polymerase alone,
$10^{-7}$ with proofreading, $10^{-9}$ with mismatch repair.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Forks and origins.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base pairs} does one fork copy in \qty{8}{h}?
  \item An origin sends out two forks. How many \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base pairs} does one
        origin copy in \qty{8}{h}?
  \item Compute the minimum number of origins needed to copy the
        \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genome} in \qty{8}{h} if all fire at the start.
  \item Compute the mean spacing of these origins along the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}, in
        kilobases and in micrometres.
  \item In reality origins fire throughout S phase and about
        \num{40000} are used. Compute the mean spacing and the mean
        distance one fork actually travels.
  \item How long would the largest \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome} (\qty{250}{Mb}) take to
        copy from a single origin at its centre?
  \item Explain why the number of origins, not the fork speed, is what
        evolution adjusted to make S phase short.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Fragments and primers.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the number of \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:fork}{Okazaki fragments} made in one S phase.
  \item Compute the number of \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} primers laid down, including one
        per leading strand per origin.
  \item Compute the number of ligations needed.
  \item Compute the total \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} polymerised (both strands of
        the whole \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genome}).
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} equivalents spent on polymerisation, and the
        mean rate in \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} per second over S phase.
  \item Compare with a resting \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} turnover of about $10^9$ per
        second: what fraction of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s energy goes to
        polymerisation?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Errors.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the number of errors per S phase at each of the three
        error rates.
  \item A \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}'s coding sequence is \qty{1.5}{kb}. With the full
        machinery, what is the probability that a given \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} acquires
        a mutation in one division?
  \item A person's \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} undergo some $10^{16}$ divisions in a
        lifetime. How many mutations in total, at $10^{-9}$? Why does
        the body tolerate this?
  \item A mutation disables mismatch repair. By what factor does the
        mutation rate rise, and what does this do to the risk of
        cancer over a lifetime?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The bacterium.}
\emph{E. coli}: \qty{4.6}{Mb}, one origin, two forks.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the time to replicate the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{chromosome}.
  \item In rich medium the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} divides every \qty{20}{min}. How many
        rounds of replication are under way at once, and how many
        origins does a newborn \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} carry?
  \item Compute the number of \omterm{def:b1:replication-mitosis:fork}{Okazaki fragments} per round and per
        second.
  \item Compute the errors per round at $10^{-9}$ and the fraction of
        daughter \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} carrying a new mutation.
  \item A culture of $10^9$ \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} per millilitre divides once. How
        many new mutations appear in a millilitre, and how many hit a
        given \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} of \qty{1}{kb}?
  \item Explain why antibiotic resistance can be found in almost any
        large culture of a sensitive strain.
  \item Compare the two \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms}: \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base pairs}, origins, fork speed,
        S-phase duration, and the design that lets the larger \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genome}
        be copied in a comparable time.
  \item State the result: the minimum number of origins for a human
        S phase of \qty{8}{h}, and the real number and spacing.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
