\chapter{How Animals Reproduce}\label{ch:g4:animal-reproduction}

In May the pond is loud with frog song; by June it is black with
\omterm{ex:g2:animals-grow-up:frog}{tadpoles}. The song, it turns out, was the beginning of the
story: reproduction --- how \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animals} make the next generation ---
follows rules that hold from frogs to elephants, with marvellous
variations in the details.

\section{It takes two}

\begin{definition}[Sexual reproduction]\label{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}
In \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animals}, making young usually takes two parents: a
\emph{female}\index{female} and a \emph{male}\index{male}. The
female produces \emph{egg cells}\index{egg cell}; the male
produces \emph{sperm cells}\index{sperm cell}. A young \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animal}
begins when one sperm cell joins one \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{egg} cell --- that joining
is \emph{fertilization}\index{fertilization}. Reproduction with
two parents is called \emph{sexual
reproduction}\index{sexual reproduction}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Why the frogs sing]\label{ex:g4:animal-reproduction:song}
The May chorus is the \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{males} calling the \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{females} to the pond.
Each pair then releases its cells into the water --- the
\omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{female}'s \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{eggs}, the \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{male}'s sperm --- where \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{fertilization}
happens. The black dots in the jelly a few days later are the
young already growing: the song was step one of
\cref{ch:g2:animals-grow-up}'s frog cycle.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Two parents, blended luck]\label{rem:g4:animal-reproduction:mix}
A young \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animal} gets something from each parent --- which is why
a foal can have its mother's color and its father's white
blaze. What exactly each parent hands over, and how, is one of
the great questions ahead in this book; for now, note simply
that every sexually produced \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animal} has two parents and
resembles both.
\end{remark}

\section{Eggs laid, or young born alive}

\begin{definition}[Oviparous and viviparous]\label{def:g4:animal-reproduction:ovivivi}
\Cref{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}'s two ways of being born now
get their scientific names:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animal} is \emph{oviparous}\index{oviparous} if the
        \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{female} lays \emph{\omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{eggs}}, and the young finish growing
        inside the \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{egg}, outside her body: \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{birds}, most \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{fish},
        most \omterm{prop:g4:classifying-animals:classes}{reptiles}, \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{amphibians}, \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{insects}, snails;
  \item an \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animal} is \emph{viviparous}\index{viviparous} if the
        young grows \emph{inside the mother's body}, fed by
        her, and is \emph{\omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{born alive}}: nearly all \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{mammals}.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Two nurseries]\label{ex:g4:animal-reproduction:nurseries}
The hen's chick spends three warm weeks in a shelled \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{egg},
living off the yolk (\cref{rem:g2:animals-grow-up:egg}); the
foal spends eleven months inside the mare, fed continuously by
her body, and is born ready to stand within the hour. Same
goal, two nurseries: one packed lunch in a \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:covering}{shell}, one room
service inside.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Where fertilization happens]\label{prop:g4:animal-reproduction:where}
For water-dwellers like \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{fish} and frogs, \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{fertilization} commonly
happens \emph{in the water}, outside the \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{female}'s body ---
\omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{eggs} and sperm are released together and meet by the
thousands. For land \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animals} --- \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{birds}, \omterm{prop:g4:classifying-animals:classes}{reptiles}, \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{mammals},
\omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{insects} --- the cells would dry out in the open, so the \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{male}
places his sperm \emph{inside the \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{female}'s body} during
\emph{mating}\index{mating}, and \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{fertilization} happens there.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Few young and much care, or many and none}

\begin{proposition}[Two strategies for the next generation]\label{prop:g4:animal-reproduction:strategies}
Compare the counts: a codfish releases \emph{millions} of \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{eggs}
a year and cares for none; an elephant has \emph{one} calf
every few years and guards it for a decade. Between these
extremes, a rule holds across the \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animal} world: the more young
a species produces, the less care each one gets --- and the
fewer it produces, the more each is protected and fed. Both
strategies succeed: on average, enough young survive to
replace the parents.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Counting the pond]\label{ex:g4:animal-reproduction:pondcount}
One frog pair's clutch holds thousands of \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{eggs}. Herons,
sticklebacks and dragonfly larvae (see
\cref{ex:g3:food-chains:three}) eat \omterm{ex:g2:animals-grow-up:frog}{tadpoles} all spring ---
and out of the thousands, a handful of froglets climb the bank
in July. The enormous clutch is not waste: it is the frog's
answer to the pond's dangers, with no parental guard at all.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The other extreme]\label{ex:g4:animal-reproduction:whalecalf}
The whale calf is born alone, swims at once beside its mother,
drinks her rich \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{milk} for many months and is defended against
every danger. One young at a time --- but that one gets
everything. \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{Mammals}, with their \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{milk}
(\cref{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}), are the great
specialists of the few-and-cared-for strategy.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Reading a species' strategy]\label{met:g4:animal-reproduction:read}
Given a species, ask:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many young at a time --- one, a dozen, thousands?
  \item Who guards and feeds them --- both parents, the mother,
        nobody?
  \item Where do they start --- \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{egg} outside, or grown inside?
  \item Check the balance of
        \cref{prop:g4:animal-reproduction:strategies}: many
        young with little care, or few with much.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:1}
What is \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{fertilization}? Which cell comes from each parent?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:2}
Define \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:ovivivi}{oviparous} and \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:ovivivi}{viviparous}, with two \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animals} of each
kind.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:3}
Why were the frogs singing in May? Retell the pond's story in
three steps.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:4}
Where does \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{fertilization} happen for the trout, and where for
the hen? Why the difference, by
\cref{prop:g4:animal-reproduction:where}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:5}
Compare the chick's and the foal's nurseries: where does each
grow, and what feeds it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:6}
State the rule linking the number of young to the care they
receive. Give the two extreme examples of the chapter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:7}
The codfish's millions of \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{eggs} mostly never become adult cod.
Why is the strategy successful anyway?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:8}
Run \cref{met:g4:animal-reproduction:read} on the cat: typical
litter of about five, nursed and guarded by the mother for
weeks, grown inside her. Which strategy side is she nearer to?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:9}
A foal often resembles both its parents at once. What does
this show about what the young receives, by
\cref{rem:g4:animal-reproduction:mix}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:10}
The stickleback \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:sexual}{male} builds a nest, fans the \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{eggs} and chases
off intruders --- rare devotion for a \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{fish}. Using
\cref{prop:g4:animal-reproduction:strategies}, what would you
predict about the size of his clutch, compared with the
codfish's millions?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:animal-reproduction:11}
Sea turtles crawl ashore to bury their \omterm{def:g2:animals-grow-up:born}{eggs} in warm sand, then
return to the sea. \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:ovivivi}{Oviparous} or \omterm{def:g4:animal-reproduction:ovivivi}{viviparous}? Cared for or not?
And why must this reptile, a swimmer all its life, still lay
on \emph{land}? (Use \cref{prop:g4:classifying-animals:classes}'s
reptile features.)
\end{exercise}
