\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:behavioural-ecology} --- Behavioural Ecology}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:1}
Mechanism: the dancer transposes the angle between food and sun,
measured on the flight, onto the angle to gravity on the comb, and
encodes distance from the optic flow of the journey as the run's
duration. Development: largely innate --- bees raised without seeing
dances dance correctly, with small learned adjustments. Function: it
recruits nestmates to a profitable source, raising the colony's intake.
History: derived from ritualised intention movements of departing for
the food; dwarf honeybees still dance on a horizontal surface pointing
directly at the source, the ancestral form.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:2}
A \omterm{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:tinbergen}{fixed action pattern} is a stereotyped sequence released by a simple
cue, the \omterm{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:tinbergen}{sign stimulus}, and completed once begun: the goose's egg
rolling, released by an egg-shaped object; the gull chick's pecking,
released by a red spot on a bill-like shape. The stick with three red
bands, pecked at more than a real head, showed that the releasing
mechanism is a filter tuned to a feature --- red contrast, elongation ---
not a recognition of the parent, and that exaggerating the feature
exaggerates the response.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:3}
Leave a patch when the rate of gain in it has fallen to the average
rate for the habitat as a whole, travel included. Doubling the travel
time lowers that average, so the forager stays longer in every patch,
depletes each more thoroughly, and ends with a lower overall rate.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:4}
An ESS is a strategy that, once nearly everyone plays it, no rare
alternative can outscore. All-Dove is not one: a lone Hawk wins every
contest and earns $V$ against the Doves' $V/2$. In the mixture each
strategy earns $V(1 - V/C)/2$, less than the $V/2$ all-Dove would give,
because a fraction of contests are fights that injure; no individual
can improve on it alone, so everyone is stuck with the loss.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:5}
$V = 6$, $C = 10$: $p^{*} = 0.6$; payoff $6(1 - 0.6)/2 = 1.2$ to each,
against $3$ for all-Dove. $V = 12 > C$: Hawk is the pure ESS, payoff
$(12 - 10)/2 = 1$, against $6$ for all-Dove --- the contest destroys
five sixths of the value.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:6}
$\tau = T_{0}$: $t^{*} = 1.15\times 2 = \qty{2.3}{min}$, fraction $1 -
\mathrm{e}^{-1.15} = 0.68$, $R = 0.68A/4.3 = 0.16A$ per minute. $\tau =
4T_{0}$: $t^{*} = 2.0\times 2 = \qty{4}{min}$ (more precisely $3.9$),
fraction $0.86$, $R = 0.86A/12 = 0.072A$ per minute.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:7}
Half-siblings $1/4$; grandparent--grandchild $1/4$; first cousins $1/8$.
A haplodiploid worker and her brother: he is haploid and has only
their mother's genes; of the worker's genes, half came from the mother
and half of those are the copies he received, so $r = 1/4$ from her
point of view (from his it is $1/2$: relatedness is not symmetric when
ploidy differs). Queen to son: all his genes are hers, but only half
of hers are in him: $r = 1/2$ from her side, $1$ from his.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:8}
$n\,r\,b > c$ with $b = 0.01$, $c = 0.02$: $n\,r > 2$. Full siblings, $r
= 1/2$: $n \ge 5$. Nieces, $r = 1/4$: $n \ge 9$. Unrelated: never ---
which is why dispersing males do not call.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:9}
Matings double per \qty{25}{cm}, survival falls a fifth: $L = 25$:
$0.5\times 1.2 = 0.6$; $50$: $1$; $75$: $2\times 0.8 = 1.6$; $100$:
$4\times 0.6 = 2.4$; $125$: $8\times 0.4 = 3.2$; $150$: $16\times 0.2 =
3.2$. On these terms the product keeps rising far beyond the natural
\qty{50}{cm}, so the model is missing costs: the survival penalty of a
long tail in flight is steeper than linear, the female response
saturates, growing the feathers costs energy, and Andersson's doubling
of nests was measured over one season, not a lifetime. The natural tail
is where the real costs bite.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:10}
Retaliator: against Hawk $(V - C)/2$, against Dove $V/2$, against
Retaliator $V/2$. In an all-Retaliator population a rare Hawk meets
Retaliators, who fight back, and earns $(V - C)/2 < V/2$: it cannot
invade when $V < C$. A rare Dove earns $V/2$, exactly the Retaliators'
payoff, so Doves are neutral and can drift in; once common, they let
Hawks in (a Hawk earns $V$ against a Dove), and the three-strategy game
has no simple ESS --- a first example of how adding an option can
unsettle a stable mixture.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:11}
If everyone persisted exactly $t_{0}$, a mutant persisting $t_{0} +
\varepsilon$ would win every contest for a negligible extra cost, and a
population of such mutants would be beaten by $t_{0} + 2\varepsilon$, and
so on: no fixed time is stable, so the ESS must be a random one. With
an exponential distribution the probability of persisting a further
$s$, given persistence so far of $t$, is $\mathrm{e}^{-s/m}$ whatever $t$
is --- the memoryless property --- so an opponent still displaying gives
away nothing about how much longer it will go on, and no strategy can
exploit the elapsed time.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:12}
Workers value a sister at $3/4$ and a brother at $1/4$, so under worker
control the colony should invest three times as much in females as in
males, a $3{:}1$ ratio by investment; the queen, equally related to both,
prefers $1{:}1$. Trivers and Hare (1976) found investment ratios near
$3{:}1$ in ants with a single, singly mated queen: the workers win.
Multiple mating lowers a worker's average relatedness to her sisters
toward $1/4$ to $1/2$ and pushes the workers' optimum toward $1{:}1$;
colonies of species with many-mated queens do invest more evenly, as
the argument predicts.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:behavioural-ecology:1}
\textbf{1.} $R(t) = A(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/T_{0}})/(\tau + t)$; leave when
$g'(t) = R(t)$: $(A/T_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-t/T_{0}} = A(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/T_{0}})/
(\tau + t)$.
\textbf{2.} Multiply by $(\tau + t)\mathrm{e}^{t/T_{0}}/A$: $(\tau + t)/T_{0}
= \mathrm{e}^{t/T_{0}} - 1$. With $\tau = T_{0}$ and $x = t/T_{0}$: $2 + x =
\mathrm{e}^{x}$; $x = 1.15$ gives $3.15$ against $3.16$.
\textbf{3.} $\tau = 4T_{0}$: $5 + x = \mathrm{e}^{x}$; $x = 1.94$ gives
$6.94$ against $6.96$; $t^{*} \approx 1.94\,T_{0}$, call it $2.0$.
\textbf{4.} Dense: $t^{*} = \qty{3.5}{min}$, $60(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-1.15}) =
\qty{41}{kJ}$, \qty{68}{\%}, $R = 41/6.5 = \qty{6.4}{kJ/min}$. Sparse:
$t^{*} = \qty{5.8}{min}$, \qty{51}{kJ}, \qty{86}{\%}, $R = 51/17.8 =
\qty{2.9}{kJ/min}$.
\textbf{5.} Five minutes: $g(5) = 60(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-5/3}) = \qty{49}{kJ}$.
Dense: $49/8 = \qty{6.1}{kJ/min}$ (\qty{96}{\%} of optimum); sparse:
$49/17 = \qty{2.9}{kJ/min}$ (\qty{99}{\%}). A crude rule loses a few per
cent --- good enough for selection to have settled for it.
\textbf{6.} The interval between captures lengthens as the patch
depletes, so a fixed giving-up time is a fixed marginal capture rate
--- the theorem's criterion, which is the same in every patch. It fails
because the right threshold depends on the habitat: with a fixed
\qty{20}{s} the animal leaves too early where travel is long and too
late where it is short, unless the giving-up time is itself adjusted
to recent experience --- which foragers do.
\textbf{7.} Hawk vs Hawk $(10 - 30)/2 = -10$, Hawk vs Dove $10$, Dove vs
Hawk $0$, Dove vs Dove $5$; $p^{*} = V/C = 1/3$.
\textbf{8.} $W_{H} = 10 - 20p$, $W_{D} = 5(1 - p)$. $p = 0.1$: $8$ against
$4.5$, Hawks spread. $p = 1/3$: $3.33$ each, balance. $p = 0.6$: $-2$
against $2$, Doves spread.
\textbf{9.} $3.33$ at the ESS against $5$ for all-Dove: a third of the
value of every contest is lost to the possibility of fighting.
\textbf{10.} $V = 40 > C$: Hawk is the pure ESS; every contest is a fight.
Hard years should see more fighting and more injuries --- as they do.
\textbf{11.} A Hawk meeting a Bourgeois finds it resident half the time
(a fight, $(V - C)/2$) and intruding half the time (a walkover, $V$):
average $(3V - C)/4$. Bourgeois earns $V/2$ among its own. Hawk invades
only if $(3V - C)/4 > V/2$, i.e.\ $V > C$. The butterfly's ``resident
wins'' is Bourgeois: a cheap convention, stable because fighting costs
more than a sunspot is worth --- and when both believe themselves
resident, both play Hawk.
\textbf{12.} The best thing to do depends on what everyone else does,
so the population settles where strategies pay equally rather than at
any individual's best.
\textbf{13.} $n\bar r\,b > c$: $n\bar r > c/b = 3$.
\textbf{14.} Eight full siblings: $n\bar r = 4 > 3$, duty pays. Eight
strangers: $0$, never. Eight half-siblings: $2 < 3$, no.
\textbf{15.} $c = 0.01$: threshold $n\bar r > 1$ --- half-siblings and
even a few full siblings now suffice. The individual with the smallest
cost is the one for whom the rule is satisfied, so the well-fed stand
guard while the hungry dig.
\textbf{16.} $k$ sisters are worth $\tfrac{3}{4}k$, $k$ daughters
$\tfrac{1}{2}k$: prefer sisters, by $3{:}2$.
\textbf{17.} A random sister is a full sister ($3/4$) with probability
$1/3$ and a half-sister ($1/4$) with probability $2/3$: $\bar r = 0.25 +
0.17 = 0.42 < 0.5$. The preference reverses: daughters now beat sisters,
so relatedness alone cannot explain sterile workers in polyandrous
species --- ecology and colony-level policing must be added.
\textbf{18.} The rule needs only that $r$ be higher on average among
those helped than in the population; any cue correlated with kinship
serves --- staying near the natal burrow, helping those grown up with.
A ground squirrel's rule may be ``call when near home,'' which picks out
kin without recognising any.
\textbf{19.} $W = (L/20)(1 - L^{2}/10^{4})$: $L = 20$: $0.96$; $40$:
$1.68$; $60$: $1.92$; $80$: $1.44$.
\textbf{20.} $W' = \tfrac{1}{20}(1 - 3L^{2}/10^{4}) = 0$: $L^{*} = 100/\sqrt{3}
= \qty{58}{cm}$, $W = 2.89\times\tfrac{2}{3} = 1.92$.
\textbf{21.} $L^{*} = 70/\sqrt{3} = \qty{40}{cm}$: shorter. The optimum
sits where the marginal mating gain equals the marginal survival loss,
so a heavier predation cost pulls the tail in, and populations under
different predation should differ in ornament --- as guppies and
swordtails do.
\textbf{22.} Both. Runaway predicts the preference outruns the trait,
which stops at the survival optimum; handicap predicts females prefer
longer because only a fit male carries it, again beyond what most can
grow. The experiment shows a preference beyond the optimum and does not
decide between the accounts.
\textbf{23.} If the tail were free, every male could grow the longest
one, it would say nothing about his quality, and a preference for it
would be exploited and decay. A cost that weighs more heavily on a
poor-condition male makes the long tail affordable only to the fit,
and the preference then picks fitness: the cost is what makes the
signal honest.
\textbf{24.} With eight times the return per mating, a male's fitness
rises steeply with matings and a female's barely at all; males are
selected to compete for matings --- the Hawk--Dove contests and their
weapons --- and females, whose few offspring each matter, are selected
to choose, which makes male ornaments pay. One asymmetry in the gain
per mating produces both the fighting and the finery.
\textbf{25.} $t^{*} \approx \qty{3.5}{min}$ in the dense habitat; $p^{*}
= 1/3$; sentinel duty pays when $n\bar r > 3$.
\end{solution}
