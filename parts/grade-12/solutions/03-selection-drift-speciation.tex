\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g12:selection-drift-speciation} --- Selection, Drift and Speciation}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:1}
The fraction of all copies of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} in a \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} that are a given
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}. With $p = 0.8$, $q = 0.2$: $AA$ 64\%, $Aa$ 32\%, $aa$ 4\%.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:2}
Selection: a difference in reproduction between carriers of different
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, set by the environment, changing \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{frequencies} in a direction.
Drift: random fluctuation of \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{frequencies} from the sampling of a finite
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}, with no direction. Selection depends on what the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}
does; drift does not.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:3}
About 75\% in 1880, when soot had blackened the trunks; about 30\% in
1980, after clean-air laws had let the trunks pale again.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:4}
The next generation's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} are a sample of the previous one's; a
small sample deviates more from the source than a large one, so the
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{frequencies} fluctuate more, and an \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} can be lost by chance.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:5}
An interruption of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} exchange between two \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}, long enough
for them to diverge until they can no longer interbreed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:6}
Copies of $a$: $160 + 40 = 200$ of 1000, so $q = 0.2$, $p = 0.8$.
Expected: $0.64 \times 500 = 320$, $0.32 \times 500 = 160$, $0.04
\times 500 = 20$ --- exactly the census: the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} is at the
reference state.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:7}
$q^2 = 0.0001$: one in ten thousand shows the trait; $2pq \approx
0.02$: one in fifty carries it. Ninety-nine per cent of the copies sit
in heterozygotes, on whom selection against the trait has no hold.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:8}
Both reached an end at generation 12, one fixed at 100\%, the other
lost. The third wandered without touching either boundary in 20
generations --- chance again; given more time it would.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:9}
Pale moths kept breeding in unpolluted countryside, where they were
favoured, and moths fly: migrants carried the pale \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} back into
the sooty region every generation, so it never disappeared.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:10}
Twenty individuals carry at most forty copies of each \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, a tiny
sample of the original \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}; most were lost in the bottleneck, and
drift in the small \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} that followed lost more. Numbers came
back; the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} cannot, except by new \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:11}
Ten \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} moving the same way, after the same environmental
change, with a plausible mechanism: selection. One tiny \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} of
thirty, with no reported cause: drift is the default explanation until
a mechanism is shown.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:12}
$AA$ $0.85^2 \approx 72\%$, $Aa$ $2 \times 0.85 \times 0.15 \approx
26\%$, $aa$ $0.15^2 \approx 2\%$. The $aa$ die, removing copies of
$a$; but the $Aa$, a quarter of the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}, survive malaria better
than the $AA$ and pass on their copies of $a$. The loss from the $aa$
is balanced by the advantage of the $Aa$, and $a$ stays at 15\%.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:13}
Mate choice by colour: a behavioural barrier. In turbid water the
barrier fails and the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} hybridise, so no genetic
incompatibility has yet accumulated: the separation is recent and
still reversible.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:14}
Around the ring every \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} interbreeds with its neighbours,
with only small differences between adjacent ones; the differences
add up around the circle until the ends, meeting, do not interbreed.
The whole gradient is visible at once: speciation is a matter of
accumulated small steps. The two ends are called \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} because,
where they meet, they behave as \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}: no \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} exchange.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:selection-drift-speciation:15}
Selection makes carriers of some \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} reproduce more \emph{here
and now}: better at leaving descendants in this environment, compared
with the other members of the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} --- not better in any
absolute sense. When the environment changes, the direction reverses
(the moths); drift makes \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} differ for no reason of
adaptation; sexual selection favours ornaments that hinder survival.
Evolution has no direction of improvement, only local sorting.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g12:selection-drift-speciation:1}
\textbf{1.} Copies of $D$: $2 \times 720 + 960 = 2400$; of $d$: $960 +
2 \times 320 = 1600$; out of 4000: $p = 0.6$, $q = 0.4$.

\textbf{2.} Expected $0.36 \times 2000 = 720$, $0.48 \times 2000 =
960$, $0.16 \times 2000 = 320$: exactly the census. Yes.

\textbf{3.} $960/1680 \approx 57\%$.

\textbf{4.} Most copies of $d$ are in the dark carriers. After removal:
1680 mice, $D$ copies 2400, $d$ copies 960: $q = 960/3360 \approx
0.29$ --- a fall from 0.40, but far from elimination.

\textbf{5.} Large size: a small island \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} drifts, so
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{frequencies} wander by chance (and migration from the mainland is
absent, \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} negligible).

\textbf{6.} $DD$ 360, $Dd$ 480, $dd$ 320: 1160 breeders.

\textbf{7.} $D$: $720 + 480 = 1200$; $d$: $480 + 640 = 1120$; of 2320:
$p \approx 0.52$, $q \approx 0.48$.

\textbf{8.} Next generation (per 2000): $DD$ $0.52^2 \approx 27\%$
(535), $Dd$ $\approx 50\%$ (999), $dd$ $\approx 23\%$ (466). After
the owls: 267, 500, 466. $D$: $534 + 500 = 1034$; $d$: $500 + 932 =
1432$; $q \approx 0.58$.

\textbf{9.} With $q = 0.58$: $DD$ 18\% (353), $Dd$ 49\% (974), $dd$
34\% (673); survivors 176, 487, 673; $d$: $487 + 1346 = 1833$ of 2672:
$q \approx 0.69$. Trend: 0.40, 0.48, 0.58, 0.69 --- rising by about
0.1 per generation.

\textbf{10.} Every carrier of $D$ is dark and exposed to the owls, so
selection acts on every copy of $D$ at every generation; $q$ rises
steadily. A \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} would hide in heterozygotes once rare,
but a \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{dominant} one never hides: $D$ can be driven to zero.

\textbf{11.} $D$: $4 + 3 = 7$; $d$: $3 + 2 = 5$; $q = 5/12 \approx
0.42$, close to the island's 0.40 by luck of the draw.

\textbf{12.} Among the breeders $D$ has 5 copies and $d$ 1: $q$ in the
next generation is $1/6$ on average, but the single $d$ copy may be
passed to none of the young ($q = 0$) or to several; the $dd$
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} has already vanished, and with it much of the variation.

\textbf{13.} With so few breeders, which \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} are passed on is a
matter of chance at every generation; a rare \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} can be lost in a
single unlucky litter.

\textbf{14.} $d$ will drift, and within some generations be fixed or
lost --- either outcome by chance. A neighbouring islet, from the same
start, may end with the opposite \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}: the two islands differ in
coat colour for no adaptive reason.

\textbf{15.} \omterm{prop:g12:selection-drift-speciation:drift}{Genetic drift}; the tiny size of the colony.

\textbf{16.} Yes: they rarely interbreed with island mice and the
hybrids are sterile --- reproductive isolation.

\textbf{17.} The sea, a geographic barrier; selection in a different
environment (no owls, other conditions) and \omterm{prop:g12:selection-drift-speciation:drift}{genetic drift} in a small
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}.

\textbf{18.} Mice that breed at different seasons never meet as
partners: even without any barrier of geography, no \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} flow, and
the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} continue to diverge.

\textbf{19.} Not now: they are isolated by behaviour and sterility and
would remain two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} side by side. After only 100 years they would
still have interbred freely and merged back into one \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}.

\textbf{20.} $q$ from 0.40 to about 0.69 after three generations of
owls; on the islet $d$ was fixed or lost by \omterm{prop:g12:selection-drift-speciation:drift}{genetic drift}; two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
in the end.
\end{solution}
