\chapter{Membranes and Membrane Transport}\label{ch:b1:membranes-transport}

Drop a red blood \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} into pure water and it swells and bursts within a
second; drop it into strong salt and it shrivels. Put it back into
plasma and it keeps its biconcave shape for four months, pumping sodium
out and potassium in every second of that time. The membrane that does
this is five nanometres thick --- a film of lipid two molecules deep,
threaded with proteins --- and it is the boundary across which every
exchange of \cref{ch:b1:organism-environment} finally happens. This
chapter describes the membrane's structure, the physics of what crosses
it unaided, the proteins that carry, pump and \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channel} the rest, the
electrical potential this traffic produces, and the vesicles that move
what no protein can.

\section{The fluid mosaic}

\begin{definition}[The plasma membrane]\label{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}
The \emph{plasma membrane}\index{plasma membrane} --- and every
membrane of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} --- is a \emph{lipid bilayer}\index{lipid bilayer}
about \qty{5}{nm} thick: two sheets of phospholipids
(\cref{ch:b1:lipids}) with their hydrophobic tails facing each other
and their polar heads facing the two aqueous sides, with cholesterol
between the tails, and \emph{membrane proteins}\index{membrane protein}
embedded in it or attached to it. In the \emph{fluid mosaic}\index{fluid mosaic model} model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972) the bilayer is a
two-dimensional fluid in which lipids and proteins diffuse laterally,
and the proteins are a mosaic of independent units, not a continuous
coat. The two faces differ: sugars attached to lipids and proteins face
outward only (the \emph{glycocalyx}), and some phospholipids are
confined to one leaflet.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Membranes are bilayers and fluids]\label{prop:b1:membranes-transport:evidence}
The \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{plasma membrane} is exactly two lipid molecules thick, and its
components move within its plane.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Gorter and Grendel (1925) extracted the lipids of a known number of
red blood \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} and spread them as a monolayer on water: the area was
twice the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}' total surface, so the membrane is a bilayer.
Electron microscopy shows every membrane as two dark lines
(the stained heads) around a pale core (the tails), \qty{5}{nm} in all.
Frye and Edidin (1970) fused a mouse \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} with a human \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} whose
surface proteins had been labelled with dyes of two colours: after
forty minutes at \qty{37}{\celsius} the two colours were completely
intermixed over the hybrid \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, and not at \qty{4}{\celsius}, where
the lipid is nearly solid. Bleaching a spot of a fluorescent \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{membrane
protein} with a laser and watching the fluorescence return as unbleached
molecules diffuse in (FRAP) measures the lateral diffusion:
$\qty{1}{\micro m^2}$ in a few seconds for lipids, slower for proteins,
and zero for proteins anchored to the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:cytoskeleton}{cytoskeleton}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Membrane proteins]\label{def:b1:membranes-transport:proteins}
\emph{Integral}\index{integral protein} \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{membrane proteins} span the
bilayer with one or more hydrophobic helices and can only be extracted
with detergents; \emph{peripheral} proteins are bound to one face. By
function: \emph{transporters} (\omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels}, carriers, pumps),
\emph{receptors} that bind a signal outside and act inside,
\emph{enzymes}, \emph{anchors} linking the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:cytoskeleton}{cytoskeleton} to the
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{extracellular matrix}, and \emph{recognition} proteins bearing the
sugars by which \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} identify one another. Proteins are half the mass
of a typical membrane and nearly all of its function.
\end{definition}

\section{Crossing the membrane without help}

\begin{proposition}[Permeability of the bilayer]\label{prop:b1:membranes-transport:permeability}
\index{permeability}
A pure \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{lipid bilayer} is freely permeable to small non-polar molecules
($\mathrm{O_2}$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{N_2}$, steroids), fairly
permeable to small uncharged polar molecules (water, urea, ethanol),
poorly permeable to larger polar molecules (glucose, amino acids), and
practically impermeable to ions ($\mathrm{Na^+}$, $\mathrm{K^+}$,
$\mathrm{Cl^-}$, $\mathrm{H^+}$) and to macromolecules. The
permeability spans twelve orders of magnitude, from
$\qty{e-2}{cm/s}$ for water to $\qty{e-14}{cm/s}$ for $\mathrm{Na^+}$:
a hydrophobic core \qty{3}{nm} thick lets through what dissolves in
oil and stops what carries a charge.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Fick's law of diffusion]\label{thm:b1:membranes-transport:fick}
\index{Fick's law}\index{diffusion}
The net flux $J$ of a solute across a membrane of area $S$ (moles
per second) is proportional to the concentration difference across it:
\[
J = P\,S\,(c_{\text{out}} - c_{\text{in}}),
\]
where the \emph{permeability coefficient} $P$ (in \unit{m/s}) lumps
together the solute's diffusion coefficient in the membrane, its
solubility in the lipid and the membrane's thickness ($P = DK/\ell$).
Diffusion needs no energy and always runs down the gradient; it is
\emph{passive transport}\index{passive transport}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Inside the membrane the solute diffuses down a linear concentration
profile from $Kc_{\text{out}}$ to $Kc_{\text{in}}$ ($K$ the partition
coefficient between lipid and water); Fick's first law in the bulk,
$J/S = -D\,\dd c/\dd x$, gives $J/S = DK(c_{\text{out}} -
c_{\text{in}})/\ell$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Osmosis, water potential]\label{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}
\emph{Osmosis}\index{osmosis} is the net diffusion of water across a
membrane that lets water through but not the solutes, from the
solution where water is more concentrated (fewer solutes) to the one
where it is less. It is described by the \emph{water
potential}\index{water potential} $\Psi$, the chemical potential of
water expressed as a pressure, zero for pure water at atmospheric
pressure:
\[
\Psi = \Psi_s + \Psi_p, \qquad \Psi_s = -RTc_s ,
\]
where $\Psi_s$, the \emph{solute potential}\index{solute potential},
falls with the total solute concentration $c_s$ (in osmoles per litre,
the van 't Hoff law) and $\Psi_p$, the \emph{pressure
potential}\index{pressure potential}, is the hydrostatic pressure above
atmospheric. Water moves from higher to lower $\Psi$. At
\qty{25}{\celsius}, $RT = \qty{2.48}{MPa.L/mol}$: a \qty{0.3}{osmol/L}
solution has $\Psi_s = \qty{-0.74}{MPa}$. A solution is
\emph{isotonic}\index{isotonic} to a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} when no net water moves,
\emph{hypotonic} when water enters, \emph{hypertonic} when it \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[A red cell in three solutions]\label{ex:b1:membranes-transport:redcell}
Plasma is \qty{0.3}{osmol/L}: $\Psi_s = \qty{-0.74}{MPa}$ on both sides,
no net flow. In pure water ($\Psi = 0$) the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, at
\qty{-0.74}{MPa} inside, takes up water until its membrane, which can
stretch only a few percent, ruptures. In \qty{0.6}{osmol/L} salt it
loses water until its inside is as concentrated, at half its volume. A
plant \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} in pure water does not burst: as water enters, the wall is
stretched and $\Psi_p$ rises until $\Psi_p = -\Psi_s$, the \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water
potential} inside is zero, and the flow stops with the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} turgid at
\qty{0.74}{MPa}.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Water-potential bookkeeping]\label{met:b1:membranes-transport:psi}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Convert every solute concentration to osmoles (a salt that
        dissociates into two ions counts twice) and compute $\Psi_s =
        -RTc_s$.
  \item Add the pressure term: $\Psi_p = 0$ for a solution in an open
        vessel or an animal \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, positive for a turgid plant \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell},
        negative for water under tension in a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} vessel.
  \item Water flows toward the lower $\Psi$. Equilibrium is reached
        when the two $\Psi$ are equal: by dilution of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s
        contents (animal \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}), or by a rise of pressure (plant \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}).
  \item Read the sign of the result as the direction of flow, and its
        size as the driving force; the flow rate also depends on the
        membrane's water permeability, raised a hundredfold by
        \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{aquaporin} \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Transport proteins}

\begin{definition}[Channels, carriers, pumps]\label{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}
\emph{Channels}\index{channel} are \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:proteins}{integral proteins} with a
water-filled pore through which a specific ion or small molecule
diffuses down its gradient at up to $10^8$ per second; many are
\emph{gated}, opening in response to a voltage, a ligand or a
mechanical force. \emph{Aquaporins}\index{aquaporin} are water
channels. \emph{Carriers}\index{carrier} bind their solute on one face,
change conformation, and release it on the other, a thousand times a
second at most; the glucose transporter of the red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} is one.
Channels and carriers working down a gradient perform \emph{facilitated
diffusion}\index{facilitated diffusion}, passive but selective and
saturable. \emph{Pumps}\index{pump} are carriers that couple the
transport of a solute against its gradient to the hydrolysis of ATP:
\emph{primary active transport}\index{active transport}. The
\emph{sodium--potassium pump}\index{sodium--potassium pump} of animal
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} exports three $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and imports two $\mathrm{K^+}$ per
ATP; the \emph{proton pump} of plant, fungal and bacterial membranes
exports $\mathrm{H^+}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Secondary active transport]\label{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}
\index{secondary active transport}
A gradient built by a pump is a store of energy that other carriers
spend: a \emph{symporter}\index{symporter} moves a solute uphill by
coupling it to an ion moving downhill in the same direction (the
sodium--glucose transporter of the intestine and kidney; the
proton--sucrose transporter of the phloem), an
\emph{antiporter}\index{antiporter} couples it to an ion moving the
opposite way (the sodium--calcium exchanger). In animal \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} the
currency is the $\mathrm{Na^+}$ gradient; in plants, fungi and bacteria
the $\mathrm{H^+}$ gradient.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Kinetics tell the mechanism]\label{ex:b1:membranes-transport:kinetics}
The flux of glucose into a red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} rises with the outside
concentration, then levels off at a maximum, like an enzyme
(\cref{ch:b1:enzymes}): a carrier, saturable, with a $K_m$ of about
\qty{1.5}{mmol/L}; a similar sugar, mannose, competes for it. The flux
of urea rises in proportion to its concentration without limit: simple
diffusion. The flux of glucose into an intestinal \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} stops when
sodium is removed from the lumen, or when the pump is poisoned with
ouabain: \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{secondary active transport}.
\end{example}

\section{The membrane potential}

\begin{definition}[Membrane potential]\label{def:b1:membranes-transport:potential}
Every living \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} holds an electrical potential difference across its
\omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{plasma membrane}, the \emph{membrane potential}\index{membrane potential} $V_m = V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}}$, negative inside:
\qty{-70}{mV} in a \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:nervous}{neuron}, \qty{-90}{mV} in a muscle fibre,
\qty{-120}{mV} or more in a plant \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}. It arises because the membrane
is selectively permeable to ions whose concentrations differ across it.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The Nernst equation]\label{thm:b1:membranes-transport:nernst}
\index{Nernst equation}\index{equilibrium potential}
An ion of charge $z$ at concentrations $c_{\text{out}}$ and
$c_{\text{in}}$ is at equilibrium across the membrane --- no net flux,
though the membrane is permeable to it --- when the potential equals
its \emph{\omterm{thm:b1:membranes-transport:nernst}{equilibrium potential}}
\[
E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\,\ln\frac{c_{\text{out}}}{c_{\text{in}}}
= \frac{\qty{61.5}{mV}}{z}\,\log_{10}\frac{c_{\text{out}}}{c_{\text{in}}}
\quad (\qty{37}{\celsius}).
\]
For $\mathrm{K^+}$ at \qty{5}{mmol/L} outside and \qty{140}{mmol/L}
inside, $E_K = 61.5\log(5/140) = \qty{-89}{mV}$; for $\mathrm{Na^+}$ at
\qty{145}{} outside and \qty{12}{} inside, $E_{Na} = \qty{+67}{mV}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Moving one mole of the ion from outside to inside changes the free
energy by the sum of a concentration term and an electrical term:
\[
\Delta G = RT\ln\frac{c_{\text{in}}}{c_{\text{out}}} + zFV_m .
\]
At equilibrium $\Delta G = 0$, which gives $V_m = (RT/zF)\ln(c_{\text{out}}/c_{\text{in}})$.
Converting to base-ten logarithms and inserting $R = \qty{8.314}{J.mol^{-1}.K^{-1}}$,
$T = \qty{310}{K}$, $F = \qty{96485}{C/mol}$ gives the numerical form.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Origin of the resting potential]\label{prop:b1:membranes-transport:resting}
The resting membrane is far more permeable to $\mathrm{K^+}$ (through
open potassium \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels}) than to $\mathrm{Na^+}$; $\mathrm{K^+}$ leaks
out down its concentration gradient, leaving the inside negative, until
the electrical pull back nearly balances the leak. The resting
potential therefore lies close to $E_K$, pulled a little toward
$E_{Na}$ by the small sodium leak (the Goldman equation weights each
ion's Nernst term by its permeability). The pump sustains the
gradients that the leaks would otherwise dissipate; it also contributes
a few millivolts directly, since it moves three charges out for two in.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
In a squid axon the resting potential follows $E_K$ when the outside
potassium is varied over a wide range (a straight line of slope
\qty{58}{mV} per decade at high concentrations), and departs from it
at low concentrations exactly as a small sodium permeability predicts.
Blocking the pump with ouabain \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} the potential almost unchanged
for minutes, then lets it decay over hours as the gradients run down:
the potential is a diffusion potential, the pump its long-term
support.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Where the pump's energy goes]\label{ex:b1:membranes-transport:pumpcost}
Pumping one $\mathrm{Na^+}$ out against \qty{12}{} to \qty{145}{mmol/L}
and \qty{-70}{mV} costs $RT\ln(145/12) + F\times 0.070 = 6.4 + 6.8 =
\qty{13.2}{kJ/mol}$; three of them, \qty{40}{kJ}, plus two
$\mathrm{K^+}$ in at $RT\ln(140/5) - F\times 0.070 = 8.6 - 6.8 =
\qty{1.8}{kJ/mol}$ each: \qty{43}{kJ} per cycle against the
\qty{50}{kJ} of one ATP under cellular conditions. The pump runs near
its thermodynamic limit, and it consumes a third of a resting animal's
ATP, two thirds in the brain.
\end{example}

\section{Bulk transport}

\begin{proposition}[Vesicular transport]\label{prop:b1:membranes-transport:vesicles}
Macromolecules and particles cross the \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{plasma membrane} without passing
through it: by \emph{exocytosis}, a vesicle fuses with the membrane and
empties outward; by \emph{\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:endocytosis}{endocytosis}}, the membrane engulfs material
into a vesicle (\cref{ch:b1:eukaryotic-cell}). Both require ATP, both
move membrane as well as contents, and both preserve the sidedness of
the membrane. The three routes across a membrane --- through the lipid,
through a protein, inside a vesicle --- are selective in three ways: by
solubility, by molecular recognition, by the choice of what is
engulfed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Cholesterol delivery]\label{ex:b1:membranes-transport:ldl}
Cholesterol travels in the blood inside protein-coated particles too
large for any carrier. A \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} needing cholesterol displays receptors
for the particle; particle and receptor gather in a coated pit, which
pinches off, and the vesicle delivers the particle to a \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:endomembrane}{lysosome} where
the cholesterol is freed. A person whose receptors are defective
cannot clear the particles: blood cholesterol doubles and arteries
clog in early adulthood. One \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{membrane protein}, one disease.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Enterocyte}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the cell that pulls glucose out of
the gut: pump, symporter, carrier and the Nernst equation, ending on
the highest glucose ratio the cell can build}]
\label{pb:b1:membranes-transport:1}
An intestinal epithelial \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} (enterocyte) is \qty{25}{\micro m} tall
and \qty{5}{\micro m} wide; its apical membrane faces the lumen, its
basolateral membrane the blood. Its \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol} holds \qty{12}{mmol/L} of
$\mathrm{Na^+}$ and \qty{140}{mmol/L} of $\mathrm{K^+}$; the lumen and
the blood hold \qty{145}{mmol/L} of $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and \qty{5}{mmol/L}
of $\mathrm{K^+}$. The \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:potential}{membrane potential} is \qty{-60}{mV}. Take
$T = \qty{310}{K}$, $R = \qty{8.314}{J.mol^{-1}.K^{-1}}$,
$F = \qty{96500}{C/mol}$, $RT/F = \qty{26.7}{mV}$, and
$\Delta G_{\text{ATP}} = \qty{-50}{kJ/mol}$ in the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The gradients.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the Nernst potentials of $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and
        $\mathrm{K^+}$.
  \item Which ion is nearer equilibrium at \qty{-60}{mV}? What does
        this say about the resting permeabilities?
  \item Compute the free energy change for one mole of $\mathrm{Na^+}$
        entering the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, and for one mole of $\mathrm{K^+}$ leaving.
  \item Compute the free energy cost of one pump cycle ($3\,\mathrm{Na^+}$
        out, $2\,\mathrm{K^+}$ in) and compare with the energy of one
        ATP. What is the efficiency?
  \item The pump is on the basolateral membrane only. Explain why this
        placement is necessary for the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} to move glucose from lumen
        to blood.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Glucose uphill.}
The apical membrane carries a \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} taking in one glucose with two
$\mathrm{Na^+}$; the basolateral membrane carries a glucose carrier
(\omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{facilitated diffusion}).
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the free energy released by two $\mathrm{Na^+}$
        entering the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}.
  \item Write the free energy needed to move one glucose from the
        lumen (concentration $c_L$) to the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol} ($c_C$).
  \item At the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter}'s limit the two are equal. Compute the maximum
        ratio $c_C/c_L$.
  \item If the lumen falls to \qty{0.1}{mmol/L} of glucose late in
        absorption, what cytosolic concentration can the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter}
        still maintain?
  \item Blood glucose is \qty{5}{mmol/L}. Explain how glucose \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}
        the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} into the blood without any further energy.
  \item Why is the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} placed on the apical and the carrier on
        the basolateral membrane, and not the reverse?
  \item Oral rehydration solutions for cholera contain glucose and
        salt. Explain, from the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter}, why the glucose makes the
        salt --- and the water --- be absorbed.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Counting the traffic.}
The \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} absorbs \qty{2e-15}{mol} of glucose per second.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the $\mathrm{Na^+}$ entering per second through the
        \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporters}, and the number of pump cycles per second needed to
        export it.
  \item Compute the ATP spent per second on this export, and the
        fraction of the glucose absorbed that would have to be
        respired to pay for it (about 30 ATP per glucose).
  \item Each \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} cycles \qty{50}{} times per second. How many
        \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporters} does the apical membrane need? The apical membrane
        is \qty{25}{\micro m^2} with microvilli multiplying it by
        \qty{20}{}: compute the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} density per square
        micrometre.
  \item The $\mathrm{Na^+}$ that enters brings water by \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{osmosis}. Two
        $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and one glucose, with the accompanying
        $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, are four osmoles; the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} keeps its
        osmolarity at \qty{0.3}{osmol/L}. Compute the volume of water
        that must follow the solutes per second, and the time it would
        take to double the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s volume if the water did not leave
        through the basolateral membrane.
  \item Compute the number of water molecules absorbed per glucose
        molecule (\qty{18}{g/mol}, density \qty{1}{g/mL}).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Charging the membrane.}
The membrane is a capacitor of \qty{1}{\micro F/cm^2}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the area of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s membrane (take a box
        \qty{25}{\micro m} by \qty{5}{\micro m} by \qty{5}{\micro m}
        without microvilli) and its capacitance.
  \item Compute the charge needed to hold \qty{-60}{mV} and the number
        of monovalent ions it represents.
  \item Compute the number of excess ions per square micrometre of
        membrane that this represents.
  \item Compute the number of $\mathrm{K^+}$ ions in the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, and the
        fraction that must leave to charge the membrane. Comment.
  \item The pump moves a net charge out on each cycle. Using question
        13, compute the current it carries and the change in potential
        it would produce in one second if nothing else moved. Why does
        the potential not in fact run away?
  \item A drug blocks the pump. Predict, in order, the changes in the
        sodium gradient, the glucose absorption, the \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:potential}{membrane
        potential} and the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} volume.
  \item A drug blocks the potassium \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels} instead. Predict the
        change in the resting potential.
  \item State the result: the maximum glucose concentration ratio the
        enterocyte can build, and the two \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{membrane proteins} that make
        it possible.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
