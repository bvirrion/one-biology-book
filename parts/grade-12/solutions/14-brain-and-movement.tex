\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g12:brain-and-movement} --- From Intention to Movement}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:1}
A strip over the top of each hemisphere, in front of the groove
between frontal and parietal lobes. Its map assigns each part of the
strip to one part of the opposite side of the body, with area
proportional to the fineness of that part's movements.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:2}
Axons of \omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{motor cortex} \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} descend through the brainstem, where
most cross to the other side, then down the spinal cord to the level
of the leg, where they end on the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} (or neighbouring
interneurons) whose axons run to the leg muscle.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:3}
Area follows the precision of control, not size: the hand makes many
fine, independent movements that need many cortical \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}; the
trunk makes few and coarse ones.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:4}
Every command to a muscle --- from the cortex, the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex arcs}, the
interneurons --- must pass through its motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}, which sum all
the inputs and fire only if the sum reaches threshold; there is no
other route to the muscle.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:5}
The enlarged finger area of musicians (and of volunteers after days of
practice); the takeover of an amputated limb's area by neighbours; the
recovery after a stroke commanded by regions around the lesion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:6}
About \qty{4}{mV}; four inputs in quick succession reach the
\qty{15}{mV} threshold; an inhibitory input arriving with an
excitatory one cancels it and the voltage barely moves.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:7}
Face and arm on the left, leg less: the right hemisphere's \omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{motor
cortex}, in its lower and middle part (face and arm areas), with the
leg area at the top only partly touched --- above the crossing, since
the deficit is on one side with the face included.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:8}
After a stroke the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex arc} is intact but the cortex's inhibitory
influence on the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} is lost, so the spindle's input meets
no damping: an exaggerated jerk. A cut nerve breaks the arc itself:
no signal reaches the muscle, no \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:9}
Voluntary movement of both legs and the lower trunk is lost (the
tract is interrupted); the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes} of the legs remain, exaggerated,
since their arcs lie below the lesion; the face's fibres leave the
tract in the brainstem, above the lesion, and are untouched.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:10}
Clenching sends a general excitation from the cortex down the cord,
including some to the leg's motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}; their voltage sits closer to
threshold, and the spindle's inputs reach it more easily and in more
\omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:11}
Years of practice enlarged the finger area by plasticity; starting
before seven, during the period when the cortex reorganises most
readily, produced a larger change than the same practice later.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:12}
Circuits are strengthened by use: if the good arm does everything,
the surviving circuits to the paralysed arm are never activated and
are not selected; forcing their use makes them fire, and the ones that
succeed are strengthened. As in the kitten, the connections that are
used take the territory; those unused lose it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:13}
The sensory area of the hand, deprived of input, was invaded by the
neighbouring face area, so touching the cheek activates \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} that
still "mean" the hand. The motor map lies beside it and reorganises
the same way: the former hand area is expected to be taken over by the
arm and face.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:14}
Motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} destroyed: paralysis, \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes} absent, muscles wasting
(no nerve input); the cortex forms intentions that cannot reach any
muscle --- an interface reading the cortex could drive a prosthesis
or a stimulator of the muscles. Cortex destroyed: paralysis, \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes}
exaggerated, muscles maintained by the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflexes}; the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} are
intact --- an interface could stimulate the cord or the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}
directly, but the intention itself is what is lost, and plasticity of
surrounding cortex is the main hope.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:brain-and-movement:15}
Muscles grow with training, but a learnt movement is a change in the
nervous system: the cortical area commanding it enlarges and its
connections sharpen (plasticity), and the pattern of inputs reaching
each motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} --- which fire, how many, in what order --- is
refined so that the sums at the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} produce the right
contraction at the right moment. The same muscles, better commanded.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g12:brain-and-movement:1}
\textbf{1.} The corticospinal fibres cross in the brainstem: the left
cortex commands the right side.

\textbf{2.} The whole strip's lower and upper parts: face and arm (the
side of the strip) and leg (the top) --- a lesion across the left
\omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{motor cortex}.

\textbf{3.} The sensory strip, just behind the motor one across the
central groove, is spared: sensation reaches it normally.

\textbf{4.} The \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex arc} in the cord is intact, and the cortex's
damping inputs to its motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} are gone: the spindle's input
fires the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} more easily.

\textbf{5.} The motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} or their nerves on the right: the arc is
broken (no \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex}) and the muscles, without nerve input, waste. A
lesion below the \omterm{prop:g12:brain-and-movement:integration}{final common pathway}, not above it.

\textbf{6.} $12 \times 1 + 3 \times 2 = \qty{18}{mV}$: above threshold,
it fired.

\textbf{7.} $18 - 4 = \qty{14}{mV}$: just below threshold, no firing
--- the inhibition modulated the step.

\textbf{8.} $0 + 6 = \qty{6}{mV}$: no firing; the leg does not move.

\textbf{9.} Before: $20 - 6 = \qty{14}{mV}$, below threshold or just at
it --- a modest jerk. After: \qty{20}{mV}, well above --- a strong jerk:
the damping is what kept the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} modest.

\textbf{10.} $8 + 6 = \qty{14}{mV}$: just short; with a little more
spindle input (or effort) it fires. The movement is weak and
hesitant, firing on some attempts and not others.

\textbf{11.} From surviving cortex around the lesion and from the
other hemisphere, whose \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} form new or strengthened connections
to the leg's motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}.

\textbf{12.} Plasticity strengthens the circuits that are activated:
an attempted movement fires the surviving cortical \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} and their
pathways, and those that produce a contraction are selected; a leg
moved passively activates only sensory circuits, not the commanding
ones.

\textbf{13.} The small fraction of corticospinal fibres that do not
cross, and pathways through the brainstem, can carry a command from
the right hemisphere to the right leg; the reorganisation recruits
them.

\textbf{14.} The hand occupies a large, specialised area whose fine
control is hard for neighbouring regions to replace, and its movements
demand far more precision than a step; the leg's coarser commands are
easier to rebuild.

\textbf{15.} Same: recovery depends on use, and unused circuits are
lost. Different: the adult cortex reorganises far more slowly and less
completely than the kitten's within its critical period --- which is
why her recovery took months and remained partial.

\textbf{16.} \omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{Motor cortex}, \omterm{prop:g12:brain-and-movement:pathway}{corticospinal tract}, crossing in the
brainstem, motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} of the cord (meeting point with the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex}
input), acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} from the
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} of the \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{muscle fibres}.

\textbf{17.} \omterm{prop:g12:glucose-and-diabetes:hormones}{Glucagon}; the \omterm{prop:g12:glucose-and-diabetes:hormones}{islets of the pancreas}.

\textbf{18.} Anticipation by the brain (the command copied to the
cardiac centres, with \omterm{prop:g10:heart-lungs-effort:control}{adrenaline}) and the \omterm{def:g11:hormones-and-reproduction:hormone}{feedback} from sensors of the
muscles and blood.

\textbf{19.} Plasticity: the chapters on vision and the brain, and
this one; the blocked artery: the chapters on the heart and on
\omterm{def:g10:sport-and-health:training}{physical activity} and health; immunity: the two chapters on innate and
\omterm{def:g12:innate-immunity:immunity}{adaptive immunity}.

\textbf{20.} The left hemisphere's \omterm{def:g12:brain-and-movement:cortex}{primary motor cortex}; a sum of
inputs reaching \qty{15}{mV} above rest within a few milliseconds;
the plasticity of the cortex.
\end{solution}
