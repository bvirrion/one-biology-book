\chapter{Genome Damage and Cancer}\label{ch:g11:cancer}

Under the microscope, a slice of healthy gut lining is a picture of
order: \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} in neat layers, each with a modest \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus}, dividing at
the bottom of the layer and dying at the top, in step. A few
millimetres away, in the same slice, a patch of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} has broken ranks:
crowded, oddly shaped, with huge dark nuclei, dividing anywhere and
pushing into the tissue beneath. Every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} of the patch descends from
one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} that, some years earlier, acquired a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} letting it
divide when it should not have --- and then, over the years, a few
more. This chapter is about how the controls of the \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:cycle}{cell cycle} fail,
what damages them, and what can be done before and after.

\section{Dividing when one should not}

\begin{definition}[Cancer]\label{def:g11:cancer:cancer}
A \emph{cancer}\index{cancer} is a population of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that divide
without the controls that limit the division of normal \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, and
that invade the surrounding tissues. It begins with one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} whose
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} has accumulated \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} in the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} controlling the \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:cycle}{cell
cycle}; the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s descendants form a \emph{tumour}\index{tumour}. A
tumour that stays confined is \emph{benign}; one whose \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} invade
neighbouring tissue and travel through the blood or lymph to found
new tumours elsewhere (\emph{metastases}) is \emph{malignant}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Cancer is a disease of the genome]\label{prop:g11:cancer:genome}
The \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} of a \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} carry \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} that its owner's other \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} do
not: somatic \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}, arisen in one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} and inherited by its
descendants. The \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} is a \emph{clone}, and it evolves --- \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
that divide faster or survive better take over, and later \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}
add the ability to invade and to spread.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Sequencing the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} of \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} beside that of the patient's normal
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} finds thousands of differences, all present in the \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour}, none
in the healthy tissue; the same few \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} are found mutated in \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour}
after \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour}. Every \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} of a woman carries the same one of her
two X \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} switched off --- the signature of descent from a
single \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}. And \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutagen}{mutagens} are \omterm{def:g11:cancer:carcinogen}{carcinogens}: the agents that raise the
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} rate (\cref{ch:g11:mutations}) raise the \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer} rate, with a
delay of decades.
\end{proof}

\section{Accelerators and brakes}

\begin{proposition}[Two kinds of gene control the cycle]\label{prop:g11:cancer:genes}
The decision of a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} to divide is taken by \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} that act like
the accelerator and the brakes of a vehicle.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Proto-oncogenes}\index{proto-oncogene} encode \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins}
        that push the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} into the cycle when a growth signal
        arrives --- receptors for growth signals, and the relays that
        carry the signal to the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus}. A \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} that leaves such a
        \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} permanently active turns the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} into an
        \emph{oncogene}: the accelerator is jammed down. One mutant
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is enough.
  \item \emph{Tumour suppressor genes}\index{tumour suppressor gene}
        encode \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} that halt the cycle --- at the checkpoints
        before S and before \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:cycle}{mitosis} --- when the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is damaged, and
        that trigger repair or, if the damage is beyond repair, the
        \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s self-destruction. A \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} that inactivates such a
        \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} removes a brake; since the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} has two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, both
        must be lost.
\end{itemize}
The best-known suppressor, the \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} p53, is inactivated in half of
all human \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Two familiar genes]\label{ex:g11:cancer:genes}
A receptor for a growth signal sits in the membrane of many \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells};
one substitution in its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} makes it signal continuously, without
any signal outside --- an oncogene found in a third of colon and lung
\omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers}. The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} of p53, on the other hand, is inactivated by
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} in every possible way: substitutions that alter its shape,
deletions, a premature stop. A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} that has lost p53 does not stop
to repair its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} before copying it, so it accumulates further
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} faster: losing that brake accelerates the whole process.
\end{example}

\section{Several steps, many years}

\begin{proposition}[Cancer is a multi-step process]\label{prop:g11:cancer:multistep}
A single \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} does not make a \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer}. A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} must accumulate
several --- typically an oncogene activated and two or more
suppressors lost --- and each successive \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}, by increasing the
clone's proliferation, increases the number of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} in which the next
can occur. The sequence takes years to decades, which is why the
incidence of most \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers} rises steeply with age, and why a
\omterm{def:g11:cancer:carcinogen}{carcinogen}'s effect appears twenty or thirty years after exposure.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
In the colon, the stages can be seen: a small benign polyp carrying
one suppressor \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}; a larger polyp with an oncogene added; an
invasive \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} with p53 lost as well --- a sequence of some four to
six \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} over ten to twenty years, each stage found in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
of the next. \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{Cancer} incidence rises roughly as the fifth power of age,
the curve expected if about five or six independent rare events must
all occur in one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}. People who inherit one mutant \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of a
suppressor \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} develop the corresponding \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer} earlier and more
often, having one step already taken in every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}.
\end{proof}

\section{What damages the genome}

\begin{definition}[Carcinogen]\label{def:g11:cancer:carcinogen}
A \emph{carcinogen}\index{carcinogen} is an agent that raises the
risk of \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer}. Most are \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutagen}{mutagens}: the ultraviolet of sunlight (skin
\omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers}), the tars of tobacco smoke (lung, mouth, bladder), ionising
radiation, asbestos fibres, alcohol, some moulds. Some act otherwise:
certain viruses carry \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} that jam the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s brakes --- human
papillomaviruses cause nearly all \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers} of the cervix, hepatitis
viruses many \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers} of the liver --- and chronic inflammation or
hormones that keep a tissue dividing raise the number of divisions in
which \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} can occur.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Tobacco]\label{ex:g11:cancer:tobacco}
Smoke carries some seventy \omterm{def:g11:cancer:carcinogen}{carcinogens}; benzopyrene, once activated by
the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}' own \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes}, attaches to guanines and causes substitutions
at those positions. The p53 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} of a smoker's lung \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} typically
carries exactly such a substitution, at one of a few positions where
benzopyrene binds most readily --- the \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutagen}{mutagen} leaves its signature in
the sequence. Lung \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer} was a rarity before cigarettes; smoking
causes nine cases in ten, and stopping halves the risk within ten
years, because the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that carried the early steps are gradually
replaced.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Inherited predisposition]\label{prop:g11:cancer:inherited}
\omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{Cancer} itself is not inherited --- it is a disease of somatic
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} --- but a mutant \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of a suppressor \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} can be. A person
who inherits one non-working \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of such a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} has taken one step
in every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} of the body; a single somatic \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} of the other
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} completes the loss in any \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, instead of the two required in
other people. The result is a high risk of the corresponding \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers},
earlier in life: some 70\% of women carrying a mutant \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of one
particular \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{breast-cancer} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} develop the disease, against 12\% in
general.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Prevention, detection, treatment}

\begin{method}[Reducing the risk]\label{met:g11:cancer:prevention}
Since most of the \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} behind \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers} come from identifiable
exposures, and since the process takes years, \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer} is largely
preventable and detectable.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Avoid the \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutagen}{mutagens}}: no tobacco (a third of all \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer}
        deaths), little alcohol, sun protection, no unnecessary
        radiation.
  \item \emph{Vaccinate} against the cancer-causing viruses: the
        papillomavirus vaccine prevents nearly all cervical \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers}
        when given before exposure.
  \item \emph{Screen}: find the \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} at the polyp stage --- smear
        tests of the cervix, colonoscopy, mammography --- when
        removing it is simple and curative.
  \item \emph{Know the family}: an inherited predisposition calls for
        earlier and closer surveillance.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Treatments and what they target]\label{ex:g11:cancer:treatments}
Surgery removes a \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} that has not spread. Radiotherapy breaks the
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} in the beam beyond repair, killing dividing \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
preferentially. Chemotherapy uses drugs that block replication or the
spindle (\cref{ch:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis}): \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, dividing
constantly, die faster than normal \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} --- but the fast-dividing
normal tissues (bone marrow, gut lining, hair roots) suffer too,
which is where the side effects come from. Newer treatments target
the specific mutant \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} of a \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour}, or unleash the immune system
on it.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Why we all carry the beginnings]\label{rem:g11:cancer:everyone}
Every adult carries clones of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} with cancer-related \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} ---
in the skin, the gut, the blood --- that never become \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers}: the
remaining brakes hold, the immune system removes the worst, and the
person dies of something else first. \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{Cancer} is the occasional outcome
of a process that runs in everyone; the aim of prevention is to keep
the number of steps taken, in any one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, below the number needed.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Signature in the Gene}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a lung tumour sequenced: the
mutations counted, the smoker's signature read, the years reckoned, and
the risk a cigarette carries}]
\label{pb:g11:cancer:1}
A lung \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} and healthy tissue from the same patient, a smoker of 20
cigarettes a day for 35 years, are sequenced. The \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} carries
$\num{5000}$ somatic substitutions not found in the healthy tissue;
75\% of them replace a G by a T, the change benzopyrene causes. Among
them: one in the p53 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, creating a premature stop, on one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele};
the other \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of p53 is deleted; and one in a growth-signal
receptor \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, making the \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} permanently active.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Reading the sequence.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Why are the $\num{5000}$ substitutions called somatic? Where
        would they be found if they were germline?
  \item In a non-smoker's \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour}, substitutions are of all kinds in
        roughly equal proportions. What does the 75\% of G-to-T
        changes tell you?
  \item Classify the p53 \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} (with the vocabulary of
        \cref{ch:g11:gene-expression}) and its effect on the \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}.
  \item Explain why both \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of p53 had to be lost, but one
        mutant \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of the receptor \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} was enough.
  \item Which of the two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} is an accelerator and which a brake?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The years.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The patient smoked about $20 \times 365 \times 35$
        cigarettes. Compute the number.
  \item If each cigarette produces, on average, one lasting
        substitution in each of the lung's lining \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, how many
        substitutions would a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} carry after 35 years? Compare with
        the $\num{5000}$ found.
  \item The \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour}'s \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} are not all in the same \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}' order:
        the p53 and receptor \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} are in every \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, the
        other thousands only in subsets. What does this say about
        when the two key \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} occurred?
  \item The \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} is \qty{3}{cm} across, about $10^{10}$ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, and
        \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} double every 100 days. How many doublings from
        one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, and how many years, at least?
  \item Combine questions 8 and 9: roughly when in the 35 years did
        the last of the key \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} occur?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The risk of a cigarette.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item From the tobacco figure, the patient's relative risk was
        about 17. If 1\% of non-smokers develop lung \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer}, what
        fraction of smokers like him do?
  \item Among 100 such smokers, how many \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancers} are attributable to
        smoking?
  \item Had he stopped at 40 (after 20 years), his risk would have
        been about a third of the continuing smoker's. Explain, with
        the multi-step model and the renewal of the lung lining, why
        the risk falls after stopping.
  \item Why does a smoker's \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer}, when it comes, usually come after
        thirty years and not after five?
  \item A student argues that since a cigarette causes "only one
        \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} per \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}", it is harmless. Answer with the arithmetic
        of question 7 and the number of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Prevention and the population.}
In a country of 60 million, 25\% of adults smoke; lung \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer} kills
$\num{30000}$ people a year, 90\% of them smokers.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the yearly \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{lung-cancer} deaths among smokers and among
        non-smokers, and the death rate per 100\,000 in each group
        (take 45 million adults).
  \item By what factor is the rate higher among smokers? Compare with
        the figure's relative risk and comment.
  \item If smoking disappeared today, what would the yearly number of
        \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{lung-cancer} deaths become in the long run? Why not
        immediately?
  \item Screening by low-dose scans finds \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumours} earlier and lowers
        deaths among heavy smokers by about 20\%. Compare, in deaths
        avoided per year, screening every smoker with stopping
        smoking.
  \item State the result: what the G-to-T signature proved about the
        origin of the \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{tumour}'s \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}, the three genetic events
        that made the \omterm{def:g11:cancer:cancer}{cancer}, and the one decision that would have
        prevented it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
