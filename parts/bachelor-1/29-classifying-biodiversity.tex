\chapter{Classifying the Living World}\label{ch:b1:classifying-biodiversity}

About \num{2} million \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} have been named, and every year some
\num{18000} are added; the number that exist is estimated at nearly
\num{9} million for the eukaryotes alone, and nobody knows how many
bacteria. A name is only the beginning: a biologist wants to know
which \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} is closest to which, and the answer is a tree, because
the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} are related by descent. This chapter gives the rules of
naming, the methods by which relationships are inferred from
characters --- and the criterion, \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:parsimony}{parsimony}, by which competing trees
are judged --- the shape of the tree of life as it is now understood,
and the state of the inventory of what lives.

\section{Species and names}

\begin{definition}[Species]\label{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}
\index{species}\index{biological species concept}
A \emph{species} is, under the \emph{biological species concept}, a
group of \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} whose members can interbreed and produce fertile
offspring and are reproductively isolated from other such groups.
The concept cannot be applied to \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} that do not reproduce
sexually (most bacteria, many protists and some plants and animals),
to fossils, or to \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} that never meet; there a species is
recognised by its morphology (the \emph{morphological} concept), by
its distinct lineage on a tree (the \emph{phylogenetic} concept), or
by a threshold of genetic divergence. All the concepts try to name the
same thing: a lineage evolving separately from others.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Nomenclature and ranks]\label{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}
\index{binomial nomenclature}\index{taxon}\index{genus}\index{type specimen}
Every \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} has a \emph{binomial} name: the name of its
\emph{genus} (capitalised) followed by a specific epithet, both in
italics --- \emph{Homo sapiens}, \emph{Quercus robur},
\emph{Escherichia coli} --- often followed by the author and year of
its description. The system, fixed by Linnaeus in 1753 (plants) and
1758 (animals), nests \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} in a hierarchy of \emph{ranks}: genus,
family, order, class, phylum (or division), kingdom, domain. A group
at any rank is a \emph{taxon}. Each name is tied to a \emph{type
specimen}, deposited in a museum or herbarium, which fixes what the
name refers to; the oldest valid name has priority; and the rules are
codified so that one \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} has one name worldwide. The ranks are
conventions --- there is no property that all families share and no
genus does; only the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} is a natural unit.
\end{definition}

\section{Reconstructing relationships}

\begin{definition}[Systematics, phylogeny, cladistics]\label{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}
\index{systematics}\index{phylogeny}\index{cladistics}\index{clade}
\emph{Systematics} is the study of the diversity of \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} and of
the relationships among them; a \emph{phylogeny} is the tree of those
relationships, a hypothesis about the order in which lineages split.
\emph{Phenetics} groups \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} by overall similarity, counting all
characters equally; \emph{cladistics} groups them by shared derived
characters only, so that every group is a \emph{clade} --- an ancestor
and all of its descendants. Modern classification aims to make every
named \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{taxon} a clade.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Characters: homology, homoplasy, polarity]\label{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}
\index{homology}\index{homoplasy}\index{synapomorphy}\index{outgroup}
A \emph{character} is any heritable feature with two or more
\emph{states} (limb present or absent; a given base in a \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} A, C, G
or T). Two \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms}' characters are \emph{homologous} when inherited
from a common ancestor, \emph{homoplastic} when similar by convergence
or reversal (the wings of birds and bats; the streamlined bodies of
dolphins and sharks). A character state is \emph{ancestral}
(plesiomorphic) if present in the group's ancestor, \emph{derived}
(apomorphic) if it arose within the group; the derived states shared
by a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clade}, its \emph{synapomorphies}, are the only evidence for it.
Polarity is determined by comparison with an \emph{outgroup}, a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{taxon}
known to lie outside the group being studied: the state it shows is
taken as ancestral.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Monophyly, paraphyly, polyphyly]\label{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:monophyly}
\index{monophyly}\index{paraphyly}\index{polyphyly}
A group is \emph{monophyletic} (a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clade}) if it contains an ancestor
and all its descendants; \emph{paraphyletic} if it contains an
ancestor and only some of its descendants (the "reptiles", which
exclude the birds descended from them; the "fish", which exclude the
tetrapods; the "invertebrates"); \emph{polyphyletic} if it groups
descendants of different ancestors by a convergent character (the
"warm-blooded animals", birds and mammals; the "algae"). Paraphyletic
groups are useful descriptions of grades of organisation but are not
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clades} and are no longer given formal names.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Parsimony]\label{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:parsimony}
\index{parsimony}\index{tree length}
Among all the trees that could relate a set of taxa, the \emph{most
parsimonious} is the one that requires the fewest evolutionary
changes of character state --- the smallest \emph{tree length}, the
sum over characters of the minimum number of changes each needs on
that tree. A character that fits the tree with one change is
consistent with it; one that needs two or more is a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homoplasy} on
that tree. Parsimony is not a claim that evolution is economical; it
is the rule that a hypothesis should not multiply convergences
beyond what the data force.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Building a cladogram from a character matrix]\label{met:b1:classifying-biodiversity:cladogram}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Choose the taxa and an \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup}; list characters with their
        states, coding the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup}'s state 0 (ancestral) and the
        derived state 1.
  \item Group taxa by shared derived states, starting from the
        character shared by the most taxa (the deepest \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clade}) and
        nesting inward; each 1 shared by a set of taxa is a candidate
        \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{synapomorphy} for that set.
  \item Where characters conflict (two groupings cannot both be
        \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clades}), draw each alternative tree and count its length:
        for each character, the fewest changes that place its states
        on the tree.
  \item Keep the shortest tree; the characters that needed extra
        changes on it are the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homoplasies}. If two trees tie, the
        data do not decide and more characters are needed.
  \item Read the tree: name the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clades}, note which traditional groups
        are paraphyletic, and check that it agrees with independent
        data (molecular sequences, fossils).
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Two trees, counted]\label{ex:b1:classifying-biodiversity:count}
Four taxa: lizard, bird, mammal, frog (\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup}). Characters:
amniotic egg (lizard, bird, mammal: 1), diapsid skull (lizard,
bird), endothermy (bird, mammal). Tree A, (lizard, bird) then mammal:
egg 1 change, skull 1, endothermy 2 (arising twice) --- length 4.
Tree B, (bird, mammal) then lizard: egg 1, endothermy 1, skull 2 ---
length 4. A tie; add feathers on the leg scales and epidermal
scales (lizard, bird): tree A length 5, tree B length 6. Tree A is
preferred, and endothermy is a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homoplasy}: it evolved twice.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Molecular characters]\label{rem:b1:classifying-biodiversity:molecular}
A \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} sequence supplies hundreds of characters at once, each site a
character with four states, comparable across \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} that share
no visible feature --- a bacterium and an oak. Sequences are aligned,
homologous sites compared, and trees built by \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:parsimony}{parsimony} or by
statistical methods that model the rate of substitution; the
molecular clock, the rate at which neutral differences accumulate,
dates the splits. The methods, and the pitfalls of long branches and
unequal rates, are treated in the Year 2 volume.
\end{remark}

\section{The tree of life}

\begin{proposition}[Three domains]\label{prop:b1:classifying-biodiversity:domains}
\index{domain}\index{Archaea}\index{Bacteria}\index{Eukarya}
Life divides into three domains: \emph{Bacteria}, \emph{Archaea} and
\emph{Eukarya}. Bacteria and Archaea are both prokaryotic in \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}
organisation (\cref{ch:b1:cell-unit-of-life}) but differ in their
membrane \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{lipids}, \omterm{prop:b1:carbohydrates:wall}{cell walls}, \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:transcription}{RNA polymerases} and \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes}; the
Archaea's transcription and translation machinery is closer to the
eukaryotes' than to the bacteria's, and the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{eukaryotic cell} arose
from an archaeal lineage that acquired a bacterium as its
\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:mitochondrion}{mitochondrion} (\cref{ch:b1:eukaryotic-cell}). Within the Eukarya the
old kingdoms --- animals, plants, fungi and a "protist" remainder ---
are replaced by a handful of major lineages, of which the animals,
the fungi and the land plants are three small twigs, and the
"protists" a paraphyletic assortment of everything else.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Woese and Fox (1977) compared the sequence of the small-subunit
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{ribosomal RNA} --- a molecule every \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} has, of the same function and
alignable across all of life --- among bacteria, methanogens and
eukaryotes, and found that the methanogens differed from the other
bacteria as much as either differed from the eukaryotes. The
prokaryotes were two groups, not one; the tree drawn from this one
\omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} has been confirmed by whole \omterm{def:b1:genomes:genome}{genomes} and by the distinct
biochemistry of the Archaea (ether-linked \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{lipids}, no \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{peptidoglycan}).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The main eukaryote lineages]\label{prop:b1:classifying-biodiversity:eukaryotes}
The eukaryotes fall into a few great groups: the \emph{opisthokonts}
(animals, fungi and their unicellular relatives, whose \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} swim
with one rear flagellum); the \emph{Archaeplastida} (land plants,
green and red algae, with the primary chloroplast); the
\emph{Amoebozoa}; the \emph{Excavata} (flagellates such as
\emph{Trypanosoma} and \emph{Euglena}); the \emph{SAR} group
(stramenopiles such as diatoms and brown algae; alveolates such as
ciliates, dinoflagellates and the malaria \omterm{def:b1:species-interactions:symbiosis}{parasite}; rhizarians such
as foraminifera and radiolarians). Animals and fungi are thus closer
to each other than either is to plants; the "algae" are scattered
through four groups; and the microscopic majority of eukaryote
diversity lies outside the three kingdoms with common names.
\end{proposition}

\section{The inventory of life}

\begin{definition}[Biodiversity]\label{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:biodiversity}
\index{biodiversity}\index{extinction}
\emph{Biodiversity} is the variety of life at every level: genetic
diversity within \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}, the number and abundance of \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}, and
the variety of \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{communities} and \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystems}. Its inventory is
unfinished: some \num{2} million \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} have been described, of
which a million are insects, \num{380000} plants, \num{150000} fungi,
\num{70000} vertebrates and a few tens of thousands each of molluscs,
crustaceans, protists and prokaryotes; the number of eukaryote \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}
is estimated, from the rate at which new higher taxa are still being
found, at close to \num{9} million, so that most \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} are unnamed,
and most of those are small, tropical, or both. \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{Species} have always
gone \emph{extinct} --- the background rate, from fossils, is about
one \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} per million per year --- and the present rate, driven by
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{habitat} loss, exploitation, invasive \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} and climate, is estimated
at a hundred to a thousand times that.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Estimating how many species there are]\label{met:b1:classifying-biodiversity:estimate}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Count the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} described in a group and the rate at which
        new ones are still being added; if the rate is not slowing,
        the inventory is far from complete.
  \item Extrapolate from a well-known group to a poorly known one by
        a ratio: if the temperate faunas hold two insect \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} per
        plant \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}, and the tropics have \num{250000} plants, the
        tropics hold at least half a million insects.
  \item Sample intensively a small area (fogging a tropical tree
        canopy with insecticide, sequencing the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} of a litre of sea
        water) and scale up from the fraction of \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} that are new.
  \item Use the pattern across ranks: the numbers of phyla, classes,
        orders and families are nearly complete and rise regularly
        toward the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} level; extrapolating the regularity gives
        the total (about \num{8.7} million eukaryotes).
  \item Report the estimate with its range; the honest answer for the
        prokaryotes is a range of several orders of magnitude.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:1}
Write the name of the house cat correctly, identify its \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{genus} and
epithet, and place it in its family, order, class and phylum.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:2}
Define \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homology} and \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homoplasy} with an example of each, and say which
is evidence for a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clade}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:3}
State whether "fish", "birds" and "warm-blooded animals" are
monophyletic, paraphyletic or polyphyletic, with the reason.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:4}
Name the three domains and give two features that separate the
Archaea from the Bacteria.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:5}
Why cannot the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{biological species concept} be applied to bacteria or
to fossils, and what is used instead?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:6}
Three taxa X, Y, Z and an \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup}. Character 1 is derived in X and Y;
character 2 in Y and Z; character 3 in X and Y; character 4 in X and
Y. Draw the two possible trees, compute their lengths, and give the
most parsimonious one and its \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{homoplasy}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:7}
Why is an \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup} needed to build a cladogram, and what goes wrong
if the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup} chosen actually belongs inside the group?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:8}
Explain why animals and fungi are considered closer relatives than
animals and plants, and what character the opisthokonts share.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:9}
Woese found that methanogens differ from other bacteria as much as
from eukaryotes. Why did this justify a new domain rather than a new
kingdom within the bacteria?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:10}
A tropical tree canopy fogged with insecticide yields 1200 beetle
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}, of which \qty{20}{\%} are specific to that tree \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species};
beetles are \qty{40}{\%} of the canopy's insects, and the canopy
holds two thirds of the tree's insects; there are \num{50000}
tropical tree \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}. Estimate the number of tropical insect
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} and list the assumptions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:11}
A \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} is described from a single museum specimen; later, living
\omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} are found that differ slightly. Explain the role of the
\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{type specimen} in deciding what the name applies to, and why priority
matters.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:classifying-biodiversity:12}
"\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:parsimony}{Parsimony} assumes that evolution takes the shortest path." Discuss:
what \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:parsimony}{parsimony} does assume, when it fails (convergence, fast-evolving
lineages), and how molecular data and fossils are used to check a
tree.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: Eight Vertebrates and a Tree}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a character matrix of eight
vertebrates, its cladogram built by hand, two rival trees scored by
parsimony, the traditional groups tested for monophyly, and a
species-count estimate, ending on the most parsimonious tree and its
length}]
\label{pb:b1:classifying-biodiversity:1}
The matrix scores ten characters (1 = derived state present) for a
lamprey (\omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup}), a shark, a trout, a lungfish, a frog, a lizard, a
mouse and a pigeon.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Reading the matrix.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which characters are shared by all taxa but the \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup}?
        What do they tell about the tree?
  \item List the characters in order of the number of taxa that share
        the derived state, and say which \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clade} each defines.
  \item Which characters are present in a single \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{taxon}? Are they of
        any use in placing that \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{taxon}?
  \item Which pair of characters conflict with each other, and among
        which three taxa?
  \item State the polarity of character 3 (\omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lungs}) and explain how the
        \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{outgroup} fixes it.
  \item How many distinct rooted trees can relate the three amniotes
        to one another, once the rest of the tree is fixed? Which
        characters bear on the choice?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Building the tree.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Draw the cladogram implied by characters 1 to 5: the nested
        \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clades} from jaws to the amniotic egg.
  \item Within the amniotes, draw tree A, in which the lizard and the
        pigeon are sister taxa, and tree B, in which the mouse and the
        pigeon are sisters.
  \item Compute the length of tree A: for each of the ten characters,
        the minimum number of changes it requires.
  \item Compute the length of tree B likewise.
  \item Which tree is most parsimonious, and by how much?
  \item Name the homoplastic character on the preferred tree and
        describe its evolutionary interpretation.
  \item If characters 8 and 10 were removed, what would the two
        lengths be, and what would you conclude?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Groups on the tree.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item On the preferred tree, is the group (shark, trout, lungfish)
        --- the "fish" --- monophyletic? Which \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:nomenclature}{taxon} would have to be
        added?
  \item Is (lizard, mouse, pigeon), the amniotes, monophyletic? Name
        its \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:characters}{synapomorphy}.
  \item Is (lizard) alone plus the crocodiles and turtles --- the
        traditional "reptiles" --- monophyletic once the pigeon is
        placed? What kind of group is it?
  \item Is (mouse, pigeon), grouped by endothermy, a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{clade}? What kind
        of group is it?
  \item The lungfish is often called a fish. On the tree, is it closer
        to the trout or to the frog? Which character decides?
  \item Give the ranks that the three amniotes would occupy in a
        Linnaean classification, and explain why the ranks convey no
        information about the depth of their splits.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The inventory.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item About \num{70000} vertebrate \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} are described and the
        rate of new descriptions is \num{300} a year for fishes and
        \num{20} for mammals. Which inventory is nearer completion?
  \item A survey of the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} in \qty{1}{L} of seawater finds
        \num{2000} distinct ribosomal sequences, of which
        \qty{90}{\%} match no described \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism}. What does this
        imply for the prokaryote inventory?
  \item If the background \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:biodiversity}{extinction} rate is one \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} per million
        per year and there are \num{9} million eukaryote \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species},
        how many should go extinct a year? The observed rate for
        well-studied groups is about \num{1000} times higher: how
        many per year does that give for all eukaryotes?
  \item Why is a \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species} that goes extinct before being described a
        loss for \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:systematics}{systematics} as well as for the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem}?
  \item Explain why the number of described families is a better
        guide to the total than the number of described \omterm{def:b1:classifying-biodiversity:species}{species}.
  \item State the result: the most parsimonious tree of the eight
        vertebrates, written in nested parentheses, and its length.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
