\chapter{Enzymes and the Phenotype}\label{ch:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype}

A Siamese cat is cream-coloured over most of its body and dark brown on
its ears, muzzle, paws and tail --- the cold parts. Shave a patch on its
back, keep the patch cool under an ice pack while the fur regrows, and
the new fur comes in dark. The cat's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} have not changed; its
pigment-making \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} simply works below \qty{35}{\celsius} and not
above. Between a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} and a visible trait stands, almost always, an
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}; and between the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} and the trait stands the environment
in which it works. This chapter is about those two intermediaries.

\section{The phenotype at three scales}

\begin{definition}[Genotype and phenotype]\label{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}
The \emph{genotype}\index{genotype} of an individual is the set of
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} it carries; its \emph{phenotype}\index{phenotype} is the set
of its observable characteristics. The phenotype can be described at
three scales:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{molecular} scale: which \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} are present, in
        which form, with what activity;
  \item the \emph{cellular} scale: the shape, contents and behaviour
        of the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells};
  \item the \emph{organismal} scale: the visible traits, from eye
        colour to the symptoms of a disease.
\end{itemize}
Each scale is the consequence of the one below it.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Sickle-cell disease at three scales]\label{ex:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:sickle}
Molecular: one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid} of the haemoglobin chain (glutamate, the
sixth) is replaced by valine; the changed molecules stick to one
another into long fibres when oxygen is low. Cellular: the fibres
deform the red blood \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} into a rigid crescent that blocks small
vessels and breaks easily. Organismal: anaemia, crises of pain when
vessels block, damage to the spleen and kidneys. One substitution in
one \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:code}{codon} of one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, and three descriptions of its consequence.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Proteins make the phenotype]\label{prop:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:proteins}
Every level of the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype} is produced by \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins}: an \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}
determines a \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} (\cref{ch:g11:gene-expression}); the \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}'s
activity determines the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s chemistry and structure; the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
determine the organism's traits. Most of those \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} are
\emph{\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes}}, and most traits are therefore the outcome of chains
of enzyme-run reactions.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Enzymes}

\begin{definition}[Enzyme, substrate, active site]\label{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}
An \emph{enzyme}\index{enzyme} is a \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} that catalyses one
chemical reaction: it makes the reaction run millions of times faster
without being consumed. The molecule it transforms is its
\emph{substrate}\index{substrate}; the reaction takes place in a
pocket of the enzyme, the \emph{active site}\index{active site}, whose
shape and chemistry fit that substrate and no other. This
\emph{specificity} follows from the enzyme's three-dimensional fold,
hence from its \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid} sequence, hence from its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Lactase]\label{ex:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:lactase}
Milk sugar, lactose, is split into two simple sugars by the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}
lactase in the lining of the small intestine; without the split it
passes undigested to the colon, where bacteria ferment it, with gas,
cramps and diarrhoea as the result. All infants make lactase; in most
of humanity its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is switched off after weaning, and adults are
lactose intolerant. In populations with a long history of dairying, an
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} that keeps the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} active for life is common --- 90\% in
northern Europe, 10\% in eastern Asia. The lactase drops sold to the
intolerant contain the same \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}, made by fungi.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Conditions of activity]\label{prop:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:conditions}
An \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}'s activity depends on its surroundings. It rises with
temperature until the \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} begins to unfold, then collapses ---
human \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes} work best near \qty{37}{\celsius} and are destroyed
above about \num{45}; each \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} works within a range of acidity
suited to its place (pepsin in the acid of the stomach, trypsin in the
neutral intestine); and its rate rises with the concentration of its
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{substrate} up to a maximum, when every \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{active site} is busy.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Chains of reactions}

\begin{proposition}[Metabolic pathways]\label{prop:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:pathway}
A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} rarely makes a product in one step: a starting molecule is
transformed by a succession of \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes}, each acting on the product of
the previous one, along a \emph{metabolic pathway}\index{metabolic pathway}.
Each \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} is encoded by its own \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}. A non-working \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of any
one of them blocks the pathway at that step: the product is not made,
and the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{substrate} of the blocked \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} may accumulate. Two different
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} can thus produce the same visible defect, and one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}'s defect
can produce several.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Phenylketonuria, and how a diet changes a phenotype]\label{ex:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:pku}
About one newborn in ten thousand carries two non-working \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of
the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} for \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} 1. Phenylalanine from food is not converted;
its concentration in the blood rises twentyfold and it poisons the
developing brain. Untreated, the child suffers severe intellectual
disability. Detected at birth by a drop of blood on a card --- every
newborn is tested --- and fed a diet almost free of phenylalanine,
the same child develops normally. The \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} is unchanged; the
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype}, at every scale above the molecular one, is that of a
healthy person. The environment, here the diet, decided.
\end{example}

\section{Genotype, environment, phenotype}

\begin{proposition}[The environment shapes the phenotype]\label{prop:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:environment}
The \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype} is the result of the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} \emph{and} the
environment. The environment acts on the molecular scale (temperature
and acidity setting \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} activity, the presence of a \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{substrate}
setting whether a pathway runs), on the cellular scale (\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
responding to signals, nutrients, light) and on the organismal scale
(diet, exercise, disease). Individuals of identical \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} ---
identical twins, cuttings of one plant --- differ in \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype}
according to their histories; and individuals of different \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotypes}
can converge on the same \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype} under suitable conditions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The Siamese cat's temperature-sensitive tyrosinase: pigment forms
only in the cool extremities, and a cooled shaved patch regrows dark.
Phenylketonuria controlled by diet. Identical twins raised apart
differing in height by several centimetres and in body mass by more.
A plant of the same clone grown at sea level and at altitude
differing in height tenfold, and reverting when transplanted. In each
case the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} is fixed and the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype} moves with the
conditions.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Reasoning from gene to trait]\label{met:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:reason}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}: which \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}, in which pathway, and
        what it does.
  \item Identify the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}'s effect on the \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}: normal, reduced,
        absent, altered in its conditions of activity.
  \item Follow the consequence up the scales: what the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} lacks or
        accumulates, then what the organism shows.
  \item Ask how many working \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} are needed: for most \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes} one
        suffices, so the defect appears only with two non-working
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} (a recessive trait).
  \item Ask what the environment adds: \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{substrate} present or not,
        temperature, a treatment.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Why one working allele is usually enough]\label{rem:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:recessive}
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{Enzymes} are catalysts: a small quantity turns over a large amount of
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{substrate}. Half the normal amount of tyrosinase still makes enough
melanin, half the normal lactase still digests a glass of milk. A
carrier of one non-working \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is therefore usually
indistinguishable from a non-carrier --- the "recessive" of the
Primary volume, now explained --- and the trait appears only when
both \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} fail. \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{Proteins} that are not \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes}, needed in
quantity or in exact shape, are often the exception: half-normal
haemoglobin makes a difference.
\end{remark}

"Since the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype} depends on the environment, the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} does
not matter much." Discuss with phenylketonuria, albinism and the
Siamese cat: in each case, what the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} fixes and what the
environment can and cannot change.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Cat with Cold Ears}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a Siamese cat's coat explained
enzyme by enzyme: the temperature-sensitive tyrosinase, the map of body
temperatures, the shaved patch, and a diet that rewrites a phenotype}]
\label{pb:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:1}
The Siamese \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of the tyrosinase \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} encodes an \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} whose
activity, measured in the laboratory, is: \qty{100}{\percent} at
\qty{30}{\celsius}, \qty{80}{\percent} at \qty{33}{\celsius},
\qty{40}{\percent} at \qty{35}{\celsius}, \qty{5}{\percent} at
\qty{37}{\celsius}, \qty{0}{\percent} at \qty{39}{\celsius}. The
ordinary \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}'s \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} keeps \qty{100}{\percent} up to
\qty{40}{\celsius}. Skin temperatures of a cat: trunk
\qty{38}{\celsius}, legs \qty{36}{\celsius}, paws
\qty{33}{\celsius}, ears and muzzle \qty{31}{\celsius}. Melanin is
visible in the fur when the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} works at more than about
\qty{30}{\percent}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The map.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For each body region, read the activity of the Siamese \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}
        and say whether the fur is pigmented.
  \item Draw, or describe, the resulting coat. Does it match a Siamese?
  \item Do the same for a cat with the ordinary \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}.
  \item A Siamese kitten is born uniformly cream. Explain, knowing
        that the womb is at \qty{38}{\celsius}.
  \item At what skin temperature does the boundary between dark and
        pale fur lie? Which part of the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}'s curve fixes it?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The experiment.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A patch on the trunk is shaved and kept at \qty{30}{\celsius}
        under a cold pack while the fur regrows. Predict its colour.
  \item A patch on a paw is shaved and kept warm at \qty{38}{\celsius}
        with a bandage. Predict.
  \item After the packs are removed, the regrown fur keeps its colour
        for months, then is gradually replaced. Explain both facts.
  \item Explain why these experiments show that the environment acts
        at the molecular scale, not on the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype}.
  \item A student proposes that the dark extremities are due to a
        different \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} in those \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. Give two arguments against.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The three scales.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Describe the Siamese \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype} at the molecular, cellular and
        organismal scales.
  \item The Siamese \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} differs from the ordinary one by a single
        substitution changing one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid}. Explain how one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino
        acid} can make an \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} temperature-sensitive.
  \item A cat carrying one Siamese and one ordinary \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is fully
        pigmented. Explain with
        \cref{rem:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:recessive}.
  \item An albino cat has two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of the same \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} that encode no
        working \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} at any temperature. Compare its \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype} with
        the Siamese's at the three scales.
  \item Himalayan rabbits and certain mice show the same pattern.
        What does this suggest about their tyrosinase \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, and what
        would you expect of the \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- A diet that rewrites a \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype}.}
A newborn is found to have phenylketonuria: blood phenylalanine
\qty{1200}{\micro mol/L} instead of \num{60}. The safe level for
brain development is below \qty{360}{\micro mol/L}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item By what factor is the phenylalanine raised, and which \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}
        of the pathway figure is missing?
  \item Explain why the child's brain, and not, say, its skin, is the
        organ at risk.
  \item The diet reduces phenylalanine intake to a fifth of normal;
        the blood level falls in proportion. Is the child safe? What
        does this say about the relation between \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{substrate} supply and
        the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype} when the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} is absent?
  \item Some phenylalanine is nevertheless indispensable, since the
        body cannot make it. Explain why the diet must be controlled
        rather than phenylalanine simply eliminated.
  \item State the result: for the Siamese cat and for the child, what
        the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} fixed, what the environment set, and the single
        sentence that relates \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype}, environment and \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{phenotype}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
