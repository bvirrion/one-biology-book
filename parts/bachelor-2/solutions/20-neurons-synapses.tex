\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b2:neurons-synapses} --- Neurons, Action Potentials and Synapses}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:1}
\omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{Dendrites} receive \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:synapse}{synapses}; the soma holds the nucleus and
integrates; the \omterm{prop:b2:neurons-synapses:integration}{axon hillock} decides; the \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{axon} conducts; the
terminals release transmitter. \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{Myelin} is the wrapped membrane of a
glial cell, nearly free of cytoplasm; it insulates the \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{axon} so that
the impulse jumps between nodes and travels a hundred times faster
at lower cost.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:2}
Depolarisation to threshold; rising phase --- voltage-gated sodium
channels open, positive feedback; peak near $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$ ---
sodium channels inactivate; falling phase --- voltage-gated potassium
channels open; undershoot --- potassium channels still open, near
$E_{\mathrm K}$; recovery as they close. All-or-none because the
positive feedback either runs to completion or not at all; one-way
because the membrane just traversed is refractory (sodium channels
inactivated).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:3}
Impulse reaches the terminal; voltage-gated calcium channels open;
calcium triggers vesicle fusion; transmitter diffuses across the
cleft; binds postsynaptic receptors; channels open (or a \omterm{prop:b2:cell-signalling:gpcr}{G protein}
switches); \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:synapse}{postsynaptic potential}; transmitter removed by enzyme or
transporter; vesicle membrane retrieved.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:4}
Electrical: no delay, usually both directions, no amplification, no
inhibition (current only passes). Chemical: half a millisecond,
one-way, amplification (one impulse releases hundreds of quanta),
inhibition possible (channels for chloride or potassium), and
modifiable.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:5}
From $E = (61.5/z)\log_{10}(c_{\text{out}}/c_{\text{in}})$:
potassium \qty{-89}{mV}; sodium \qty{+61}{mV}; chloride, with $z =
-1$, $-61.5\log_{10} 11 = \qty{-64}{mV}$; calcium, with $z = 2$,
$30.75\times 4.3 = \qty{+132}{mV}$. At \qty{-70}{mV}:
potassium leaves, sodium enters, chloride leaves slightly (the
membrane is below its equilibrium), calcium enters strongly.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:6}
$20 : 1$: $(20\times(-89) + 61)/21 = \qty{-82}{mV}$, the resting
state near $E_{\mathrm K}$. $1 : 20$: $(-89 + 20\times 61)/21 =
\qty{+54}{mV}$, the peak, near $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$. $50 : 1$: $(50\times
(-89) + 61)/51 = \qty{-86}{mV}$, the undershoot with extra potassium
channels open.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:7}
$\sqrt{500} = 22$: \qty{22}{m/s}. To reach \qty{100}{m/s} an
unmyelinated \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{axon} would need $d = 10^{4}$ \unit{\micro m}, a
centimetre. One metre takes \qty{10}{ms} in the myelinated fibre,
\qty{45}{ms} in the squid \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{axon}, a full second in a \qty{1}{\micro m}
fibre.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:8}
$m = \ln(200/74) = 1.0$; $P(1) = e^{-1} = 0.37$, $P(2) = 0.18$, $P(3)
= 0.06$. Mean response $1.0\times 0.5 = \qty{0.5}{mV}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:9}
$1/\qty{2}{ms} = 500$ impulses a second. The intensity of the touch
is coded by the frequency of impulses (and by how many \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neurons} are
recruited); the impulses themselves are identical.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:10}
$\lambda = \sqrt{R_m d/4R_i}$: \qty{0.5}{mm}, \qty{1.6}{mm},
\qty{11}{mm}. With \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{myelin}, \qty{10}{\micro m}: $1.6\times\sqrt{200} =
\qty{22}{mm}$. An internode of \qty{1}{mm} loses only $1 - e^{-1/22}
= \qty{4}{\%}$ of the signal, so the next node is easily brought to
threshold. Across a demyelinated \qty{3}{mm} the signal falls to
$e^{-3/1.6} = \qty{15}{\%}$ --- of a \qty{100}{mV} impulse, about the
\qty{15}{mV} threshold: conduction fails or becomes unreliable.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:11}
Tetrodotoxin: no sodium current, no impulse, no transmission.
Tetraethylammonium: no potassium current, prolonged impulse, more
transmitter released per impulse. No calcium: impulses normal,
no release, no transmission. Curare: release normal, receptors
blocked, no end-plate potential --- paralysis. Cholinesterase
inhibitor: acetylcholine persists, receptors stay open, the muscle
depolarises and then cannot repolarise --- paralysis by excess.
Botulinum toxin: vesicles cannot fuse, no release --- paralysis.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:neurons-synapses:12}
The delay buys: transmission in one direction only; amplification
(a single impulse releases hundreds of quanta and can drive a
larger cell); inhibition, which an electrical junction cannot do;
and plasticity, since the number of quanta and receptors can change
with use, which is how circuits learn. Electrical \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:synapse}{synapses} are used
where synchrony and speed matter more than computation: escape
circuits, the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}'s muscle, some inhibitory networks.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b2:neurons-synapses:1}
\textbf{1.} $E_{\mathrm K} = 61.5\log_{10}(4/140) = \qty{-95}{mV}$;
$E_{\mathrm{Na}} = 61.5\log_{10}(145/12) = \qty{+67}{mV}$.
\textbf{2.} $(25\times(-95) + 67)/26 = \qty{-89}{mV}$.
\textbf{3.} $E_{\mathrm K} = 61.5\log_{10}(8/140) = \qty{-76}{mV}$;
$V = (25\times(-76) + 67)/26 = \qty{-71}{mV}$: closer to threshold,
so more excitable at first --- and, if it persists, less, as sodium
channels inactivate at the depolarised level.
\textbf{4.} The gradients run down over hours as sodium leaks in and
potassium out; the potential drifts toward zero; with the pump
stopped the cell's impermeant anions draw in chloride and water,
and it swells.
\textbf{5.} Surface per millimetre $\pi\times 15\times 10^{-6}\times
10^{-3} = \qty{4.7e-8}{m^2}$; $C = \qty{4.7e-10}{F}$; $Q = CV =
\qty{4.7e-11}{C}$: $2.9\times 10^{8}$ ions.
\textbf{6.} Volume per millimetre $\pi(7.5\times 10^{-6})^{2}\times
10^{-3} = \qty{1.8e-13}{m^3} = \qty{1.8e-10}{L}$, holding $12\times
10^{-3}\times 1.8\times 10^{-10} = 2.1\times 10^{-12}$ mol, $1.3\times
10^{12}$ ions: a change of $2\times 10^{-4}$.
\textbf{7.} $2.9\times 10^{8}/3 \approx 10^{8}$ ATP per millimetre per
impulse.
\textbf{8.} $0.9/90 = \qty{10}{ms}$ each way.
\textbf{9.} Bare: $\sqrt{1\times 15\times 10^{-6}/4} = \qty{1.9}{mm}$;
myelinated: $\times\sqrt{250} = \qty{31}{mm}$.
\textbf{10.} Bare: $e^{-1.5/1.9} = 0.45$, \qty{45}{mV}; myelinated:
$e^{-1.5/31} = 0.95$, \qty{95}{mV}. Both exceed the \qty{15}{mV}
threshold; but the bare \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{axon} must charge its whole membrane along
the way, which is what makes it slow, whereas under \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{myelin} almost
nothing is lost or charged.
\textbf{11.} $\sqrt{15} = \qty{3.9}{m/s}$: \qty{0.23}{s} each way,
nearly half a second for the round trip --- too slow for a reflex
that must catch a stumble.
\textbf{12.} $e^{-4/1.9} = 0.12$: \qty{12}{mV} at the far node, below
threshold --- the impulse is blocked and the reflex is lost.
\textbf{13.} Behind the impulse the sodium channels are inactivated
for a millisecond or two, so the membrane just traversed cannot be
re-excited and the wave can only move forward.
\textbf{14.} $m = \ln(300/111) = 1.0$.
\textbf{15.} $P(1) = 0.37$, $P(2) = 0.18$, $P(3) = 0.06$: about 110, 55
and 18 of the 300 trials.
\textbf{16.} $1.0\times 0.4 = \qty{0.4}{mV}$.
\textbf{17.} $P(0) = e^{-60} \approx 10^{-26}$: never; mean response
\qty{24}{mV}, above the \qty{15}{mV} threshold: one such \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:synapse}{synapse}
fires the motor \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neuron}.
\textbf{18.} Twenty \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:synapse}{synapses} active together add their currents
(spatial summation), sublinearly as the potential approaches the
reversal potential of the channels; even at $m = 1$ each they would
give \qty{8}{mV}, and at normal $m$ far more than threshold.
\textbf{19.} Opening chloride channels at $E_{\mathrm{Cl}} = V_{\text{rest}}$
adds conductance without moving the potential; the excitatory
current now flows through a leakier membrane and produces a smaller
depolarisation --- shunting inhibition.
\textbf{20.} $10 + 0.6 + 10 + 0.6 + 5 = \qty{26}{ms}$, against
\qty{30}{ms} measured.
\textbf{21.} The receptor's own activation in the muscle spindle, the
propagation of the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{muscle fibre}'s \omterm{prop:b2:neurons-synapses:ap}{action potential} along the fibre
(metres per second over centimetres), and the mechanical latency
before force appears.
\textbf{22.} Each \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:synapse}{synapse} adds half a millisecond and an interneuron
adds a cell; but interneurons allow the signal to be inverted
(inhibiting the antagonist), combined with others, and modulated by
the brain --- a reflex that can be shaped.
\textbf{23.} $90 - 6.2 = \qty{84}{ms}$ of conduction for \qty{1.8}{m}:
about \qty{21}{m/s}.
\textbf{24.} Half the inward current at every voltage: the threshold
rises, the impulse is smaller and slower, the safety factor at each
node falls, and the impulse may fail; the reflex weakens or
disappears.
\textbf{25.} \omterm{thm:b2:neurons-synapses:chord}{Resting potential} \qty{-89}{mV}; \qty{10}{ms} each way;
$m = 1.0$; computed latency \qty{26}{ms}.
\end{solution}
