\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b2:speciation} --- Speciation and Macroevolution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:1}
A species is a group of interbreeding natural populations
reproductively isolated from other such groups. It fails for
asexual lineages (bacteria, bdelloid rotifers, dandelions:
morphological or phylogenetic concept, clusters of like forms or
diagnosable lineages); for fossils (morphological concept, since
no cross can be made); and for allopatric populations that never
meet (ecological or phylogenetic concept, since interbreeding
cannot be tested) --- and it is blurred by hybridising oaks and by
ring species.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:2}
Frogs in different months: prezygotic (temporal). Mule: postzygotic
(hybrid sterility). Sperm that cannot bind: prezygotic (gametic).
Vigorous hybrids with sterile \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed}: postzygotic (hybrid sterility,
or hybrid breakdown if the failure is in the next generation).
Crickets with different songs: prezygotic (behavioural).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:3}
Allopatric: a barrier divides a range (snapping shrimps on the two
sides of the Isthmus of Panama). Peripatric: a few founders beyond
the range (Darwin's finches from a mainland ancestor). Sympatric:
no barrier (the apple maggot fly on hawthorn and apple; an
\omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:polyploidy}{allopolyploid} wheat). Parapatric: along a gradient with a \omterm{prop:b2:speciation:reinforcement}{hybrid
zone} in the middle (grasses on and off mine tailings, flowering at
different times).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:4}
Species 1 always wins; species 2 always wins; stable coexistence;
either wins according to initial numbers. Coexistence is stable
when each species can invade the other at its carrying capacity,
that is when each species limits itself more than it limits the
other ($K_1 > \alpha K_2$ and $K_2 > \beta K_1$).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:5}
$\alpha K_2 = 400 < K_1 = 1000$: species 1 invades; $\beta K_1 = 600
< K_2 = 800$: species 2 invades. $N_1^{*} = (1000 - 400)/(1 - 0.3) =
857$, $N_2^{*} = (800 - 600)/0.7 = 286$; at the equilibrium species
1 is below its $K$ by $\alpha N_2^{*} = 143$ and species 2 by $\beta
N_1^{*} = 514$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:6}
$\alpha K_2 = 1200 > K_1$: species 1 cannot invade species 2 at
carrying capacity; $\beta K_1 = 300 < K_2$: species 2 can invade
species 1. Species 2 wins always, because its individuals hurt
species 1 more than species 1's own do while it is itself barely
affected. With $\alpha = \beta = 1.5$: $\alpha K_2 = 1200 > 1000$
and $\beta K_1 = 1500 > 800$: neither can invade the other, each
excludes the other when established, and the founder wins.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:7}
Tetraploid $4n$ makes $2n$ gametes; crossed with the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid}'s $n$
gametes it gives triploids $3n$, whose \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} cannot pair three
sets and yields aneuploid, mostly inviable gametes. The
tetraploid can breed only with itself (or by selfing): isolated in
one step. It usually fails because it is alone --- its only
potential mates are \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploids} and every such cross is wasted ---
and is swamped; it persists when it selfs, propagates vegetatively,
or arises repeatedly.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:8}
$0.01\times 10\times 10 = 1$ incompatibility; with 30 each, $0.01\times
900 = 9$. Isolation grows as the square of divergence time: two
populations diverged for three times as long are nine times as
isolated --- the snowball.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:9}
$w = 2\sqrt{8/0.05} = \qty{25}{km}$; $w = 0.3\sqrt{8/0.4} =
\qty{1.3}{km}$. Reinforcement is likelier in the second: hybrids
are strongly unfit, so a female that refuses the other kind gains
much, and the zone is narrow enough for the two forms to meet often
enough for that refusal to be selected.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:10}
$\alpha K_2 = 450 < 500$, so each invades the other: they coexist,
at $N^{*} = (500 - 450)/(1 - 0.81) = 263$ each --- barely, near the
edge of exclusion, and a small change in $K$ would tip it. With
$\alpha = \beta = 0.4$: $N^{*} = (500 - 200)/(1 - 0.16) = 357$ each.
Displacement rotated each isocline away from the other's ($K/\alpha$
moved outward), so the crossing moved away from the axes and each
species sits nearer its own $K$: a more secure coexistence with
more of each.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:11}
$R = h^{2}S = 0.7\times(-1) = \qty{-0.7}{mm}$: as observed. In the
1977 drought, with no large ground finch present, the same medium
finches were selected for \emph{larger} beaks, since large \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds}
were the food left; in 2004 the large \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} were taken by the
invader, and the medium finches that competed for them lost: the
direction of selection was set by the competitor.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:speciation:12}
Made by accident: the two-locus model shows isolation arising as a
by-product of substitutions fixed for other reasons, or by drift,
and geography --- a barrier, a founding --- supplies the separation
that lets the accident accumulate. Kept by selection: where the
forms meet, selection against unfit hybrids builds prezygotic
barriers (reinforcement), and competition displaces characters so
that the two can coexist. The slogan omits \omterm{prop:b2:speciation:modes}{sympatric speciation} by
disruptive selection, where selection makes as well as keeps the
species, and \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:polyploidy}{polyploidy}, where the accident is the whole story.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b2:speciation:1}
\textbf{1.} Species 1: $N_1 + 0.9N_2 = 1200$, intercepts $N_1 = 1200$
and $N_2 = 1333$. Species 2: $N_2 + 0.3N_1 = 400$, intercepts $N_2 =
400$ and $N_1 = 1333$.
\textbf{2.} $\beta K_1 = 360 < K_2 = 400$: species 2 invades;
$\alpha K_2 = 360 < K_1 = 1200$: species 1 invades. Stable
coexistence.
\textbf{3.} $N_1^{*} = (1200 - 360)/(1 - 0.27) = 1151$; $N_2^{*} =
(400 - 360)/0.73 = 55$.
\textbf{4.} $\mathrm{d}N_1/\mathrm{d}t = 0.5\times 1200\,(1 - 1218/1200)
= -9$ per year: the resident, at its carrying capacity, is pushed
into decline by the newcomers.
\textbf{5.} $K_1 = 600$, $K_2 = 200$: $N_1^{*} = (600 - 180)/0.73 =
575$, $N_2^{*} = (200 - 180)/0.73 = 27$: both halve, and the invader,
at 27 birds, is close to extinction --- the drought is when
competition bites.
\textbf{6.} The invader is the larger bird with the larger beak: it
takes the large \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} the resident also uses, and takes them
faster, while the resident's small \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} are of little use to it.
\textbf{7.} $S = 9.0 - 9.5 = \qty{-0.5}{mm}$; $R = 0.7\times(-0.5) =
\qty{-0.35}{mm}$.
\textbf{8.} $9.5 - 0.35 = \qty{9.15}{mm}$.
\textbf{9.} $N_1^{*} = (1200 - 200)/(1 - 0.15) = 1176$; $N_2^{*} =
(400 - 360)/0.85 = 47$.
\textbf{10.} The $N_2$ intercept of species 1's isocline rose from
$K_1/0.9 = 1333$ to $K_1/0.5 = 2400$: the line rotated away from
the invader's, the resident is now less affected by each invader,
and the equilibrium moved toward the resident's $K$.
\textbf{11.} $2/0.35 \approx 6$ years. It does not because selection
of this kind occurs only in drought years, reverses in wet years
when small \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} abound (or when the invader is scarce), and
because the additive variance would be depleted.
\textbf{12.} Two million years is about $10^{6}$ generations; a shift
of \qty{0.35}{mm} per generation sustained for even a thousandth of
them would change the beak by hundreds of millimetres. Selection is
strong but inconstant; sustained directional change is the rare
exception, and net change over geological time is a tiny residue
of large opposing episodes.
\textbf{13.} Song, learned from the father and used by females in
mate choice, with beak shape itself as a cue. Hybrids with
intermediate beaks handle neither the large nor the small \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds}
best and starve first in droughts.
\textbf{14.} $w = 0.5\sqrt{8/0.2} = \qty{3.2}{km}$.
\textbf{15.} The island is narrower than the zone would be: no
spatial cline can form; hybridisation, where it occurs, affects
the whole population, and only assortative mating keeps the two
apart.
\textbf{16.} Females that mate only with males of their own song and
beak leave more grandchildren than those that hybridise; the
preference and the cue (song, beak) become more distinct where the
two species live together than where they live alone.
\textbf{17.} $0.005\times 15\times 15 = 1.1$ incompatibilities: on
average one, with hybrids probably reduced but not sterile. Not yet
species by the biological criterion; on the way.
\textbf{18.} Biological: not yet (hybrids nearly fit).
Morphological: perhaps, if the beaks or plumage have diverged
visibly. Phylogenetic: yes, if each population has a fixed
diagnostic difference. The concepts disagree exactly during the
process of speciation, which is what makes them useful.
\textbf{19.} $1/(5\times 10^{6}) = 2\times 10^{-7}$ per species per
year; for $10^{7}$ species, two extinctions a year.
\textbf{20.} 200 to 2000 species a year.
\textbf{21.} $0.75\times 10^{7}/10^{5} = 75$ species a year --- lower
than the present rate at the low end. The present episode, if
sustained, is a \omterm{prop:b2:speciation:tempo}{mass extinction} proceeding faster than the big
five.
\textbf{22.} The niches vacated by the losers are empty of
competitors, so any survivor variant that can use one is favoured,
and the survivors diversify into them.
\textbf{23.} \omterm{prop:b2:speciation:tempo}{Punctuated equilibrium}; allopatric (peripatric)
speciation in a small isolated population whose descendant then
spreads into the main population's range.
\textbf{24.} $1.04^{n} = 2$: $n = \ln 2/\ln 1.04 = 18$ generations.
The record shows millions of years because selection reverses,
\omterm{prop:b2:population-genetics:kinds}{stabilising selection} dominates most of the time, and net change is
the small difference between large opposed episodes.
\textbf{25.} Before displacement: stable coexistence at $N_1^{*} =
1151$, $N_2^{*} = 55$; after: 1176 and 47. Beak shift
\qty{-0.35}{mm} in one generation. \omterm{prop:b2:speciation:reinforcement}{Hybrid zone} width \qty{3.2}{km}.
\end{solution}
