\chapter{Carbohydrates}\label{ch:b1:carbohydrates}

A potato and a sheet of paper are made of the same molecule, glucose,
strung into chains. One is a meal and the other is not, because the
chains are joined by two different bonds: the $\alpha$ link of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch}
coils the chain into a helix that our enzymes open, the $\beta$ link of
\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{cellulose} stretches it into ribbons that stack into fibres no mammal
can digest. A liver stores a hundred grams of glucose as \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen}, a
tree holds itself up with \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{cellulose}, a beetle wears \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{chitin}, and every
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} displays sugars on its surface as its identity. This chapter
describes the simple sugars, the bonds that join them, the
\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{polysaccharides} they build, and the sugar-decorated molecules of the
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} surface and the plant wall.

\section{Monosaccharides}

\begin{definition}[Monosaccharide]\label{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}
\emph{Carbohydrates}\index{carbohydrate} are molecules of composition
$(\mathrm{CH_2O})_n$ and their derivatives. A
\emph{monosaccharide}\index{monosaccharide} (simple sugar) is a chain
of three to seven carbons, one of which carries a carbonyl group and
the others hydroxyls: an \emph{aldose}\index{aldose} when the carbonyl
is terminal (an aldehyde: glucose, galactose, ribose), a
\emph{ketose}\index{ketose} when it is internal (a ketone: fructose).
By length: trioses ($n = 3$: glyceraldehyde), pentoses ($n = 5$:
ribose, deoxyribose), hexoses ($n = 6$: glucose, fructose, galactose).
Every carbon bearing four different groups is a \emph{chiral} centre,
so each formula stands for several \emph{stereoisomers}: glucose has
four chiral carbons and sixteen isomers, of which living things use one,
D-glucose. \emph{Epimers} differ at one carbon only (glucose and
galactose at C4).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Ring forms and anomers]\label{prop:b1:carbohydrates:rings}
\index{anomer}\index{Haworth projection}
In water a pentose or hexose is almost entirely in a ring: the carbonyl
carbon reacts with a hydroxyl of the same molecule (C5 in glucose) to
close a six-membered \emph{pyranose} ring or a five-membered
\emph{furanose} ring (fructose, ribose). The carbonyl carbon becomes a
new chiral centre, the \emph{anomeric carbon}, whose hydroxyl can lie
below the ring plane ($\alpha$ anomer) or above it ($\beta$ anomer) in
the Haworth drawing; the two forms interconvert through the open chain
in minutes. In solution, glucose is \qty{36}{\%} $\alpha$, \qty{64}{\%}
$\beta$ and less than \qty{0.1}{\%} open chain. The free anomeric
carbon is what makes a sugar \emph{reducing}: it can open and reduce a
copper or silver reagent.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Reading a Haworth projection]\label{met:b1:carbohydrates:haworth}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Find the ring oxygen; the anomeric carbon (C1 in an \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{aldose}, C2
        in a \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{ketose}) is the ring carbon to its right, bearing both a
        hydroxyl and the ring oxygen.
  \item The carbon carrying the $\mathrm{CH_2OH}$ group outside the
        ring (C5 in glucose) is to the left of the ring oxygen; its
        $\mathrm{CH_2OH}$ points up in a D-sugar.
  \item Anomeric hydroxyl down: $\alpha$; up (on the same side as
        $\mathrm{CH_2OH}$): $\beta$.
  \item Compare the other hydroxyls with those of glucose (down, up,
        down for C2, C3, C4): a single difference identifies an epimer
        (galactose: C4 up).
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Sugars that are not $(\mathrm{CH_2O})_n$]\label{ex:b1:carbohydrates:derivatives}
Deoxyribose lacks the hydroxyl at C2 (\cref{ch:b1:nucleic-acids});
glucosamine and N-acetylglucosamine carry an amine instead of the C2
hydroxyl (\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{chitin}, \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{peptidoglycan}); glucuronic acid has a carboxyl at C6
(the matrix \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{polysaccharides}); sugar phosphates (glucose-6-phosphate,
ribose-5-phosphate) are the forms in which sugars enter metabolism;
sugar alcohols (glycerol, sorbitol) have no carbonyl. The core is the
same: a small polyhydroxylated carbon chain that water loves.
\end{example}

\section{The glycosidic bond}

\begin{definition}[Glycosidic bond, disaccharide]\label{def:b1:carbohydrates:glycosidic}
A \emph{glycosidic bond}\index{glycosidic bond} joins the anomeric
hydroxyl of one sugar to a hydroxyl of another (or of an alcohol, an
amine, a base) by condensation, fixing the anomeric carbon in its
$\alpha$ or $\beta$ configuration; it is named by the configuration and
the carbons joined: $\alpha(1{\to}4)$, $\beta(1{\to}4)$,
$\alpha(1{\to}6)$. Two sugars so joined form a
\emph{disaccharide}\index{disaccharide}: \emph{maltose}
(glucose $\alpha(1{\to}4)$ glucose, from \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} digestion),
\emph{lactose} (galactose $\beta(1{\to}4)$ glucose, the sugar of milk),
\emph{sucrose} (glucose $\alpha(1{\to}2)\beta$ fructose, the sugar of
plant sap and of the kitchen). In sucrose both anomeric carbons are
engaged: it is non-reducing and does not open, which is why plants
transport it (\cref{ch:b1:plant-transport}).
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Testing for a reducing sugar]\label{met:b1:carbohydrates:reducing}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Heat the solution with an alkaline copper(II) reagent
        (Benedict's or Fehling's).
  \item A free anomeric carbon opens to an aldehyde, reduces the blue
        $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ to a brick-red precipitate of $\mathrm{Cu_2O}$;
        the depth of colour scales with the amount of sugar.
  \item Glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose: positive. Sucrose: negative
        --- unless first hydrolysed by acid or by the enzyme invertase,
        after which its glucose and fructose react.
  \item \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{Starch} does not react (its single reducing end is one in
        thousands); it is detected with iodine, which enters the helix
        of amylose and turns blue-black.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Polysaccharides}

\begin{definition}[Polysaccharide]\label{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}
A \emph{polysaccharide}\index{polysaccharide} is a chain of hundreds to
tens of thousands of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{monosaccharides} joined by \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:glycosidic}{glycosidic bonds}, linear
or branched. Storage polysaccharides: \emph{starch}\index{starch} in
plants --- \emph{amylose}, unbranched $\alpha(1{\to}4)$ glucose,
\qty{20}{\%}, and \emph{amylopectin}, $\alpha(1{\to}4)$ with
$\alpha(1{\to}6)$ branches every \qtyrange{24}{30}{} residues,
\qty{80}{\%} --- packed in grains; \emph{glycogen}\index{glycogen} in
animals and fungi, like amylopectin but branched every
\qtyrange{8}{12}{} residues, in cytosolic granules of the liver and
muscles. Structural polysaccharides: \emph{cellulose}\index{cellulose},
unbranched $\beta(1{\to}4)$ glucose, \qtyrange{2000}{15000}{} residues,
in plant walls; \emph{chitin}\index{chitin}, $\beta(1{\to}4)$
N-acetylglucosamine, in the walls of fungi and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{cuticles} of
arthropods; \emph{peptidoglycan}\index{peptidoglycan}, alternating
N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid cross-linked by short
peptides, the one-molecule mesh that is the bacterial wall.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The bond decides the shape, and the shape the function]\label{prop:b1:carbohydrates:alphabeta}
In an $\alpha(1{\to}4)$ chain every glucose is turned the same way as
the last and the chain curls into a helix of six residues per turn, an
open, hydrated coil that enzymes enter easily: the form of a
\emph{store}. In a $\beta(1{\to}4)$ chain each glucose is flipped
$180^\circ$ relative to its neighbour and the chain is a straight,
flat ribbon; ribbons lie side by side and hydrogen-bond into
\emph{microfibrils} of thirty to a hundred chains, crystalline,
insoluble, with the tensile strength of steel per unit mass: the form
of a \emph{fibre}. The enzymes that hydrolyse $\alpha$ bonds (amylases)
cannot touch $\beta$ bonds; \emph{cellulases} exist in bacteria, fungi
and a few animals, and the mammals that live on grass do so through
symbionts (\cref{ch:b1:digestion-absorption}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Why glycogen is branched]\label{ex:b1:carbohydrates:branching}
\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{Glycogen} is degraded from its non-reducing ends, one glucose at a time,
by \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} phosphorylase. An unbranched chain of \num{50000} glucoses
would have one such end and release one glucose per enzyme turnover;
a \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} particle of the same size, branched every twelve residues,
has some \num{2000} ends and releases two thousand at once. Branching
also keeps the particle compact and soluble. The liver's hundred grams
of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} can be mobilised at ten grams an hour; the same glucose
stored as one long chain would take years to unwind.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Why not store glucose itself]\label{ex:b1:carbohydrates:osmotic}
A hundred grams of glucose (\qty{0.55}{mol}) dissolved in the
\qty{1}{L} of water of a liver's \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} would add \qty{0.55}{osmol/L}
to a \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol} at \qty{0.3}{osmol/L}: the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} would swell to three
times their volume and burst. As \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} the same glucose is
$\num{7e18}$ particles, \qty{1e-5}{mol/L}, osmotically invisible. A
polymer is a way of storing a great many molecules as one.
\end{example}

\section{Glycoconjugates and the plant wall}

\begin{definition}[Glycoproteins, proteoglycans, glycolipids]\label{def:b1:carbohydrates:glycoconjugate}
Sugars are attached covalently to proteins and \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{lipids} to form
\emph{glycoconjugates}\index{glycoconjugate}. A \emph{glycoprotein}\index{glycoprotein}
carries short branched chains (a dozen sugars) on some of its
asparagine, serine or threonine residues, added in the ER and \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:endomembrane}{Golgi}
(\cref{ch:b1:eukaryotic-cell}): nearly every secreted and \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{membrane
protein} is one. A \emph{proteoglycan}\index{proteoglycan} is a protein
core bearing long unbranched chains of repeating acidic \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:glycosidic}{disaccharides},
the \emph{glycosaminoglycans}\index{glycosaminoglycan} (hyaluronan,
chondroitin sulfate, heparin), which bind enormous amounts of water and
give cartilage, the vitreous body and the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{extracellular matrix} their
resilience. \emph{Glycolipids}\index{glycolipid} carry sugars on a \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{lipid}
tail in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane. Together with the
sugars of glycoproteins they form the \emph{glycocalyx}\index{glycocalyx},
the sugar coat by which \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} are recognised.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Blood groups]\label{ex:b1:carbohydrates:abo}
The ABO blood groups are three versions of one sugar chain on the red
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s surface \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:glycoconjugate}{glycolipids} and \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:glycoconjugate}{glycoproteins}: the O chain ends in
fucose; A adds an N-acetylgalactosamine to it, B adds a galactose, by
two versions of one enzyme; AB \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} carry both. A single sugar
residue is enough for the immune system to distinguish self from
foreign, and for a transfusion to succeed or kill.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[The plant cell wall]\label{prop:b1:carbohydrates:wall}
\index{cell wall}
The primary wall of a plant \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} is a fibre-reinforced composite:
\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{cellulose} microfibrils (a quarter of the mass, tensile strength) laid
in layers, tied together by \emph{hemicelluloses} (branched
\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{polysaccharides} that hydrogen-bond to the microfibrils and bridge
them) and embedded in a gel of \emph{pectins} (acidic \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{polysaccharides}
that hold water and calcium and cement \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} together in the middle
lamella), with a few percent of protein. It is thin
(\qtyrange{0.1}{1}{\micro m}), permeable to water and small solutes,
and strong enough to hold \qty{1}{MPa} of \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor}. \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{Cells} that stop
growing may add a thick \emph{secondary wall} with more \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{cellulose} and
\emph{lignin}, an aromatic polymer that waterproofs and stiffens it:
wood is secondary walls.
\end{proposition}

\section{Sugars as fuel and carbon}

\begin{proposition}[The place of glucose]\label{prop:b1:carbohydrates:fuel}
\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{Carbohydrates} yield \qty{17}{kJ/g} on oxidation and are the fuel every
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} can use, with or without oxygen (\cref{ch:b1:respiration-fermentation});
glucose is the sugar of the blood (\qty{5}{mmol/L}), sucrose that of
plant sap, and \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} and \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} their stores. Sugars are also the
carbon from which the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} makes everything else: the pentoses of the
nucleotides, the glycerol of \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{lipids}, the carbon skeletons of amino
acids all leave the sugar pathways (\cref{ch:b1:biosyntheses-integration}).
Photosynthesis makes sugar first; the rest follows.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[A day's sugar]\label{ex:b1:carbohydrates:day}
A human eats some \qty{300}{g} of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{carbohydrate} a day, nearly all as
\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} and sucrose; the gut hydrolyses it to glucose, fructose and
galactose, the liver turns the last two into glucose, and glucose
circulates at \qty{5}{g} in the whole blood --- twenty minutes' supply
for the brain, which takes \qty{120}{g} a day, refilled continuously
from the liver's \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen}. The lens of the eye, the red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} and the
kidney medulla live on glucose alone; nothing else in the diet can
replace it, and when it runs out the liver makes it from protein.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Tree of Glycogen}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a glycogen particle counted branch
by branch, the liver's store weighed and its release timed, the osmotic
price that polymerisation avoids, ending on the glucose release rate of
the liver}]
\label{pb:b1:carbohydrates:1}
A \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} particle is a tree of chains: an inner chain of
\qty{13}{} glucose residues bears two branches, each of \qty{13}{}
residues and each bearing two branches in turn, and so on for
\qty{12}{} tiers; the outermost tier's chains are unbranched. Each
glucose residue in the polymer has a mass of \qty{162}{g/mol}. A
resting human liver of \qty{1.5}{kg} holds \qty{100}{g} of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} in
\qty{1.0}{L} of \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} water, and releases glucose into the blood at
\qty{10}{g/h} between meals. \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{Glycogen} phosphorylase removes one glucose
from a non-reducing end per turnover, at \qty{20}{} turnovers per second
per enzyme molecule.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Counting the tree.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many chains does tier $t$ contain ($t = 1$ for the inner
        chain)? Give the number for tiers 1, 2, 3 and 12.
  \item Compute the total number of chains in the particle.
  \item Compute the total number of glucose residues.
  \item Compute the mass of the particle in daltons and in grams.
  \item What fraction of all the chains are in the outermost tier?
        How many non-reducing ends does the particle offer?
  \item An amylose molecule of the same number of residues has how
        many non-reducing ends?
  \item If each tier adds \qty{1.9}{nm} to the radius, compute the
        particle's diameter and compare it with a ribosome
        (\qty{25}{nm}).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The liver's store.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the number of particles in the liver.
  \item Compute the glucose the store represents in moles and in
        grams of free glucose (\qty{180}{g/mol}).
  \item \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{Glycogen} binds \qty{3}{g} of water per gram. What mass of the
        liver is \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} plus its water?
  \item Compute the release rate of \qty{10}{g/h} in molecules of
        glucose per second.
  \item How many hours does the store last at that rate? Which \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ}'s
        demand does the release chiefly serve?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Ends and enzymes.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the total number of non-reducing ends in the liver.
  \item If every end were attacked at once at \qty{20}{} glucoses per
        second, what would the release rate be, in grams per second?
        Compare with the actual rate.
  \item How many phosphorylase molecules, each working at \qty{20}{}
        per second, are needed for the actual rate?
  \item Suppose the same \qty{100}{g} were stored as amylose chains
        of the same size as the particles. Compute the number of ends
        and the maximum release rate.
  \item Explain why the branch points are the key to the liver's
        response to a fall in blood glucose within minutes.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The osmotic price.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item If the \qty{100}{g} were dissolved as free glucose in the
        \qty{1.0}{L} of \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} water, what osmolarity would it add?
  \item Compare with the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol}'s \qty{0.3}{osmol/L} and predict what
        would happen to the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
  \item Compute the osmolarity contributed by the \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} particles
        themselves.
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} the free glucose would create
        ($\Psi_s = -RTc$, $RT = \qty{2.58}{MPa.L/mol}$) and the
        pressure a plant-type wall would need to withstand it.
  \item Muscle stores \qty{400}{g} of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} in \qty{30}{kg} of
        muscle but cannot release glucose into the blood. Propose why,
        given what \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} is for in a muscle.
  \item A potato stores \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} in grains of \qty{50}{\micro m}, not
        \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} particles of \qty{40}{nm}. Relate the difference to
        the timescale of mobilisation each \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} needs.
  \item After a meal the liver rebuilds \qty{100}{g} of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen} in
        four hours. Compute the rate in glucose residues per second and
        the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} cost if adding one residue costs two \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} equivalents.
  \item State the result: the glucose release rate of the liver in
        molecules per second, the number of non-reducing ends that
        make it possible, and the osmolarity the polymer spares the
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
