\chapter{Hormonal Control of Reproduction}\label{ch:b2:reproductive-hormones}

Every month, on a schedule kept to within a day or two for thirty
years, one \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} ripens, one egg is released, the lining of the
uterus is built up and then, unless an embryo has arrived, torn
down. No clock in the body ticks at this rate; the rhythm emerges
from a conversation between three organs --- the hypothalamus, the
pituitary and the ovary --- in which each hormone controls the
release of the next and the last controls the first. This chapter
takes that conversation apart: the axis that runs both sexes, the
tonic control of the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis}, the cyclic control of the ovary with
its remarkable switch from negative to positive feedback, and the
ways the axis is overridden by pregnancy, by drugs, and by the
clinic.

\section{The axis}

\begin{definition}[The hypothalamo-pituitary-gonadal axis]\label{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:axis}
\index{gonadotropin-releasing hormone}\index{luteinising hormone}\index{follicle-stimulating hormone}\index{negative feedback!hormonal}
Neurons of the \emph{hypothalamus} secrete \emph{GnRH}
(gonadotropin-releasing hormone, a peptide of ten amino acids) into
the small portal vessels that run to the \emph{anterior pituitary},
in brief \emph{pulses} every one to two hours. GnRH makes the
pituitary secrete two glycoprotein hormones into the general
circulation, the \emph{gonadotropins} \emph{LH} (luteinising
hormone) and \emph{FSH} (follicle-stimulating hormone). These act on
the \emph{gonads}: LH on the steroid-making cells (\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{Leydig cells},
theca and luteal cells), FSH on the cells that nurse the gametes
(Sertoli and granulosa cells). The gonads answer with steroids ---
\emph{testosterone}, \emph{oestradiol}, \emph{progesterone} --- and
with the peptide \emph{inhibin}, and these feed back on the
hypothalamus and pituitary: the steroids on both, mostly
\emph{negatively}; inhibin on FSH alone. The result is a regulated
loop in which the gonad's output is held at a set point, and the
same three-tier design runs the thyroid and the adrenal cortex. The
mechanisms by which these hormones act on their cells are the
subject of \cref{ch:b2:cell-signalling}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Why the pulses matter]\label{prop:b2:reproductive-hormones:pulses}
\index{pulsatile secretion}\index{receptor desensitisation}
The pituitary responds to GnRH only if it arrives in pulses. A cell
exposed to a hormone continuously removes the hormone's receptors
from its surface (\emph{desensitisation}); if the receptor pool is
replenished at a rate $s$, removed at a basal rate $d$ and, in the
presence of hormone $H$, at an extra rate $iH$, then
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t} = s - (d + iH)\,R, \qquad R^* = \frac{s}{d + iH},
\]
so that a continuous high $H$ drives the receptor number to a low
steady state and the cell falls silent, whereas brief pulses
separated by hormone-free intervals let the pool recover
(time constant $1/d$) between them. A GnRH pulse every ninety
minutes keeps the pituitary responsive; a constant infusion, or a
long-acting GnRH agonist, shuts it down within days --- the basis
of the drugs that suppress the axis in prostate cancer, endometriosis
and precocious puberty. Pulse frequency also encodes information:
fast pulses favour LH, slow pulses favour FSH.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Knobil (1978--1980) destroyed the GnRH neurons of rhesus monkeys,
which abolished LH, FSH and the cycle, and then replaced GnRH by
pump: one pulse an hour restored the gonadotropins and normal
ovulatory cycles; the same daily dose given continuously restored
them for a few days and then LH and FSH collapsed to zero; returning
to pulses restored them again. The pattern of delivery, not the
amount, is the signal.
\end{proof}

\section{The male: tonic control}

\begin{proposition}[Control of the testis]\label{prop:b2:reproductive-hormones:testis}
\index{testosterone}\index{inhibin}
LH acts on the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{Leydig cells}, which make \emph{testosterone}
(\qtyrange{3}{10}{ng/mL} in the blood of an adult man, a hundred
times more inside the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis}). FSH acts on the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{Sertoli cells}, which
--- with the local testosterone --- support \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{spermatogenesis} and
secrete \emph{inhibin}. Testosterone feeds back on the hypothalamus
and pituitary to hold LH down; inhibin feeds back on the pituitary
to hold FSH down. Both loops are \emph{negative} and the system is
\emph{tonic}: hormone levels fluctuate with the pulses and with the
day (highest in the morning) but do not cycle, and sperm are made
continuously. Testosterone also builds and maintains the accessory
glands and ducts, the beard, the voice, muscle and bone, red-cell
production and libido, and in the fetus made the male body
(\cref{ch:b2:mammal-reproduction}). Castration removes it: LH and
FSH rise several-fold for want of feedback, and the secondary
characters regress; injected testosterone restores the characters
--- but not fertility, because it also suppresses LH and so the
intratesticular testosterone that \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{spermatogenesis} requires. This is
why anabolic steroids make men infertile.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Berthold (1849) castrated cockerels: their combs shrank and they
neither crowed nor fought. A \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis} grafted into the abdomen of a
castrate, where it grew a new blood supply but no nerves, restored
comb, crowing and fighting: the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis} acted through the blood ---
the first demonstration of an internal secretion, fifty years before
the word hormone. Testosterone was isolated and synthesised in 1935.
\end{proof}

\section{The female: a cycle with a switch}

\begin{definition}[The ovarian and uterine cycles]\label{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:cycle}
\index{menstrual cycle}\index{follicular phase}\index{luteal phase}\index{LH surge}\index{endometrium}
The cycle is counted from the first day of menstruation and lasts
about 28 days. \emph{Follicular phase} (days 1--14): FSH, freed of
feedback by the previous \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum}'s death, recruits a cohort
of \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicles}; they secrete \emph{oestradiol}, which rises steadily;
oestradiol and inhibin lower FSH, and only the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} with the
most FSH receptors survives the fall --- the dominant follicle. In
the uterus, oestradiol rebuilds the \emph{endometrium} (the
proliferative phase) and thins the cervical mucus. \emph{\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{Ovulation}}
(day 14): when oestradiol has stayed above about \qty{200}{pg/mL}
for a day and a half, its feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary
switches from negative to \emph{positive}; LH rises tenfold in a day
--- the \emph{LH surge} --- and about 36 hours after its onset the
\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} ruptures and releases the oocyte. \emph{Luteal phase} (days
15--28): the emptied \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} becomes the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum}, which
under LH secretes \emph{progesterone} (\qtyrange{10}{20}{ng/mL}) and
oestradiol; progesterone turns the endometrium secretory, thickens
the mucus, raises the basal body temperature by \qty{0.3}{\celsius},
and with oestradiol suppresses LH and FSH. The \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum} has a
lifespan of about 14 days; unless hCG from an embryo rescues it, it
regresses, progesterone and oestradiol fall, the endometrium is shed
(\emph{menstruation}), FSH rises, and the next cycle begins. The
luteal phase is fixed at 14 days; variation in cycle length is
variation in the follicular phase.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The switch from negative to positive feedback]\label{thm:b2:reproductive-hormones:switch}
\index{positive feedback!LH surge}
Oestradiol at the low levels of the early \omterm{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:cycle}{follicular phase}
inhibits LH and FSH; oestradiol above about \qty{200}{pg/mL}, held
for more than about \qty{36}{h}, does the opposite: it makes the
hypothalamus release a burst of GnRH and the pituitary respond to it
with a tenfold rise of LH. The mechanism is a change of sign, not
of magnitude: a high, sustained dose induces in the pituitary a
large store of LH and a heightened sensitivity to GnRH, and in the
hypothalamus (through kisspeptin neurons) a switch of the GnRH
pulse generator into a surge mode. The switch converts a graded
signal --- how much oestradiol the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} makes --- into an
all-or-none event timed to the day, which is what \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation}
requires; and because only a large, mature \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} can sustain
\qty{200}{pg/mL}, the surge fires only when there is an egg ready to
release. Once \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation} has occurred, the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum}'s
progesterone restores the negative feedback and no second surge
follows.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
In women and monkeys with the axis suppressed, an infusion of
oestradiol that raises the plasma level above \qty{200}{pg/mL} for
two days is followed by an \omterm{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:cycle}{LH surge}; the same amount given for one
day, or a lower level for three, is not. Progesterone given before
the infusion blocks the surge; given at the same time it advances
and amplifies it. The threshold in dose and duration reproduces the
timing of the natural surge from the natural oestradiol rise.
\end{proof}

\section{Overriding the cycle}

\begin{proposition}[Pregnancy, lactation, menopause]\label{prop:b2:reproductive-hormones:overrides}
\index{human chorionic gonadotropin}\index{menopause}
An implanting embryo secretes \emph{hCG}, an LH-like hormone that
binds the LH receptor and keeps the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum} alive and
secreting past its fourteen days; by week ten the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} itself
makes enough progesterone and oestrogens to maintain the pregnancy
and the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum} is dispensable. Throughout pregnancy the high
steroids hold LH and FSH at zero: no \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} grows, no \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation}
occurs. After birth, suckling raises prolactin and slows the GnRH
pulses, and \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation} stays suppressed for months. At the
\emph{menopause} the stock of \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicles} is exhausted: oestradiol and
inhibin fall, and, with nothing to feed back, LH and FSH rise to ten
times their earlier level and stay there --- the signature of a
gland that has stopped answering.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Contraception and assisted reproduction]\label{prop:b2:reproductive-hormones:clinic}
\index{contraception!hormonal}\index{in vitro fertilisation}
The combined pill delivers a synthetic oestrogen and a progestin
every day: the constant steroid level keeps FSH and LH low, no
\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} is recruited, no oestradiol rise occurs, and the surge
never fires; the progestin also thickens the cervical mucus. The
seven-day break lets the \omterm{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:cycle}{endometrium} bleed; missing pills for more
than two days in a row lets FSH escape and a \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} may grow.
Emergency contraception delays the surge for a few days with a
large progestin dose. In \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:reproductive-hormones:clinic}{in vitro fertilisation}} the axis is
driven the other way: daily FSH for ten days overrides the
selection of a single dominant follicle and matures ten or more; a
GnRH antagonist prevents a premature spontaneous surge; an injection
of hCG substitutes for the surge, and the oocytes are collected 34
to 36 hours later, just before they would have been released; they
are fertilised in the dish and one or two embryos are transferred,
the rest frozen. In men, exogenous testosterone or a GnRH analogue
suppresses \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{spermatogenesis} by suppressing LH --- the principle of a
male hormonal contraceptive, still not marketed. All of this is the
axis of the first section, read backwards.
\end{proposition}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Reading the cycle.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the follicular and \omterm{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:cycle}{luteal phases} from the table,
        with the hormone that marks each.
  \item On what day did the \omterm{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:cycle}{LH surge} peak? When did \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation} occur?
  \item How long was the \omterm{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:cycle}{luteal phase}? Is it consistent with the
        lifespan of the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum}?
  \item Explain the temperature shift and why it confirms, but
        cannot predict, \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation}.
  \item On which days was oestradiol above \qty{200}{pg/mL}? Relate
        the duration to the surge.
  \item Give the fertile window of this cycle (sperm three days, egg
        one day).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Numbers.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Convert the day-13 oestradiol to \unit{pmol/L}, and the
        day-21 progesterone to \unit{nmol/L}.
  \item By what factor did LH rise from day 12 to day 14? Why does it
        fall so fast afterwards (the half-life of LH is about
        \qty{1}{h})?
  \item If this woman's next cycle had a 21-day \omterm{def:b2:reproductive-hormones:cycle}{follicular phase}, on
        what day would she ovulate and what would the cycle length
        be?
  \item An embryo implants 7 days after \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation}. On what day of
        this cycle would hCG appear, and, doubling every two days
        from \qty{1}{IU/L}, what level would it reach by day 27?
  \item Explain why the day-27 progesterone of \qty{2}{ng/mL} shows
        that no embryo implanted.
  \item What would the day-27 values of progesterone and oestradiol
        have been if one had?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Feedback and pulses.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Explain, with the feedback signs, why FSH is highest at the
        start of the cycle and why only one \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} usually
        survives.
  \item In the receptor model with $s = 100$ per hour, $d =
        \qty{0.5}{h^{-1}}$, $iH = \qty{6}{h^{-1}}$, compute $R^*$
        without hormone and under continuous hormone.
  \item A pulse lasts \qty{2}{min}. Starting from the unstimulated
        $R^*$, how many receptors does one pulse remove (use the
        linear approximation $iHR\,\Delta t$)? What fraction of the
        deficit is recovered in the hour before the next pulse
        (time constant $1/d$), and around what level does the pool
        settle?
  \item Use these numbers to explain Knobil's result.
  \item A man is given a GnRH agonist continuously for prostate
        cancer. Predict his LH and testosterone after one day and
        after one month, and explain the difference.
  \item A woman's granulosa cells fail to make inhibin. Predict her
        FSH and LH, and the consequence for \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{follicle} recruitment.
\end{enumerate}
