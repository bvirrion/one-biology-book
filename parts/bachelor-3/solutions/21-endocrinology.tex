\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:endocrinology} --- Endocrinology and Homeostasis}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:1}
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{Insulin}: peptide, surface receptor (a tyrosine kinase), acts in
minutes, half-life about \qty{5}{min}. Cortisol: steroid, \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{nuclear
receptor}, hours, half-life \qty{80}{min} (mostly bound to a carrier).
Adrenaline: amine, surface G-protein-coupled receptors, seconds,
half-life about \qty{2}{min}. Thyroxine: iodinated amino acid, \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{nuclear
receptor}, days, half-life \qty{7}{days} (bound to carriers).
Vasopressin: peptide, surface G-protein-coupled receptor (cAMP in the
\omterm{def:b3:renal-osmoregulation:nephron}{collecting duct}), minutes, half-life about \qty{15}{min}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:2}
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{Hypothalamus} (TRH) $\to$ \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} (TSH) $\to$ thyroid (T$_{4}$), with
T$_{4}$ inhibiting both upper tiers. Destroyed thyroid: T$_{4}$ low,
TSH high. TSH-secreting \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumour}: TSH high and T$_{4}$ high (the
feedback fails to suppress the \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumour}). Excess thyroxine tablets:
T$_{4}$ high, TSH suppressed. Iodine deficiency: T$_{4}$ low or
low-normal, TSH high, and the gland grows under it into a goitre.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:3}
The brain dies of a low glucose in minutes and is harmed by a high one
only over years; and evolution met famine far more often than feasts.
So the defence against a fall is redundant (\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{glucagon}, adrenaline,
cortisol, growth \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone}) and the defence against a rise is single.
Prediction: too much \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} is acutely lethal (hypoglycaemic coma),
too little is a chronic disease --- which is what the clinic sees.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:4}
A \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:clock}{zeitgeber} is an environmental cue that entrains a clock: light,
food timing, temperature, exercise, social schedule. The master clock
uses light, through the melanopsin \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{ganglion cells} of the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{retina}. After
eight time zones the clock shifts only about an hour a day, so for a
week sleep, cortisol, temperature and appetite run on the old time
while the peripheral clocks, reset by meals, drift at their own pace:
jet lag, worse eastward, since advancing the clock is harder than
delaying it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:5}
Half-maximal response at $250$ receptors occupied: $H = K_{d}\times
250/(\num{20000} - 250) = \qty{1}{nM}\times 0.0127 = \qty{12.7}{pM}$,
eighty times below $K_{d}$. With \num{2000} receptors: $250/1750
\times \qty{1}{nM} = \qty{143}{pM}$, eleven times less sensitive.
Prolonged high \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} down-regulates its receptors, so a given effect
needs more \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}: one component of \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} resistance, which feeds
on itself.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:6}
$T_{4} = 13$: $1.5\,\mathrm{e}^{0.92} = \qty{3.8}{mU/L}$; $11$:
$1.5\,\mathrm{e}^{1.84} = \qty{9.4}{mU/L}$; $9$: $1.5\,\mathrm{e}^{2.76}
= \qty{24}{mU/L}$; $25$: $1.5\,\mathrm{e}^{-4.6} = \qty{0.015}{mU/L}$. At
\qty{11}{pmol/L} the T$_{4}$ is still within \qtyrange{10}{20}{} while
TSH is more than double its upper limit (subclinical hypothyroidism);
at $13$ TSH is just within range; at $9$ both are abnormal; at $25$
both are abnormal the other way.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:7}
$L = s\beta/(a\gamma) = 0.2\times 0.05/(0.02\times 0.1) = 5$. Steady
excess $g^{*} = (u/a)/(1 + L) = (2/0.02)/6 = \qty{16.7}{mg/dL}$; without
feedback $u/a = \qty{100}{mg/dL}$. \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{Insulin} excess $i^{*} = \beta g^{*}/
\gamma = 0.05\times 16.7/0.1 = \qty{8.3}{mU/L}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:8}
Discriminant $(a - \gamma)^{2} - 4s\beta = 0.0064 - 0.04 < 0$:
oscillatory. $\omega = \sqrt{a\gamma + s\beta - (a+\gamma)^{2}/4} =
\sqrt{0.002 + 0.01 - 0.0036} = \qty{0.092}{min^{-1}}$, period $2\pi/
\omega = \qty{68}{min}$; amplitude falls by $\mathrm{e}$ in $2/(a +
\gamma) = \qty{16.7}{min}$. Halving $s$: $s\beta = 0.005$, discriminant
$0.0064 - 0.02 < 0$, still oscillatory, $\omega = \sqrt{0.007 - 0.0036}
= \qty{0.058}{min^{-1}}$, period \qty{108}{min}; the decay time is
unchanged; $L = 2.5$. A weaker loop returns more slowly and holds a
larger steady error.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:9}
For a year the prednisolone suppressed CRH and ACTH; the adrenal
cortex, unstimulated, atrophied. On stopping there is no exogenous
steroid, the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{hypothalamus} and \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} take weeks to resume, and the
shrunken adrenal could not respond to ACTH if it came. An infection
demands ten times the normal cortisol; with none, vessels dilate,
pressure and glucose fall: adrenal crisis. Measured: ACTH low (the
lesion is central), cortisol low, and a poor cortisol rise to injected
ACTH (the gland has wasted) --- unlike Addison's disease, where ACTH is
high. Hence the slow taper.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:10}
One variable: $\dot x = f(x)$ with $f' < 0$ has a single fixed point
$x^{*}$; for $x > x^{*}$, $\dot x < 0$ and $x$ decreases monotonically
toward $x^{*}$, never crossing it (uniqueness of solutions), and
symmetrically below: no oscillation. Two variables: the matrix can have
complex eigenvalues, as in the theorem, when the effector acts through
a second variable with its own time scale. An instantaneous repression
would settle at a steady level; the delay between transcription and
nuclear repression (translation, dimerisation, phosphorylation, import)
lets the protein overshoot before the gene is turned off and undershoot
before it is turned on again, and the period is roughly twice the sum
of the delay and the time to degrade the protein. Faster PER degradation
shortens the period: a destabilising mutation of human PER2 gives a
clock of about 20 hours and a family that falls asleep at 7 p.m.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:11}
Exponent $k(\text{Ca} - 1.2)$: at $1.10$, $\mathrm{e}^{-2}$, PTH $= 1/
(1 + 0.135) = 0.88$; $1.15$: $0.73$; $1.20$: $0.50$; $1.25$: $1/(1 +
2.72) = 0.27$; $1.30$: $1/(1 + 7.39) = 0.12$. Slope at the \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{set point}
$-k/4 = -5$ per mmol/L: \qty{5}{\%} of maximum for each
\qty{0.01}{mmol/L}. Calcium must be held within a few per cent and its
effector acts on a huge buffer (bone) without overshoot, so a steep
response is safe and needed. A glucose loop that steep, acting through
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} with its own clearance time, would have a large \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{loop gain} and
oscillate deeply into hypoglycaemia after every meal.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:endocrinology:12}
Glycation is irreversible and slow, so a cell of age $\tau$ has a
fraction $kG\tau$ glycated; averaged over cells uniformly aged
\numrange{0}{120} days, HbA1c $= kG\times\qty{60}{days}$, proportional
to the mean glucose. Calibration: $5.5\times 60k = 0.05$ gives $k =
\qty{1.5e-4}{}$ per (mmol/L)(day); at \qty{10}{mmol/L}: \qty{9.1}{\%}.
With cells living 60 days the mean age is 30 days and HbA1c is
halved for the same glucose: a patient at \qty{10}{mmol/L} would read
\qty{4.5}{\%}, normal.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:endocrinology:1}
\textbf{1.} \qty{90}{mg/dL} $= \qty{0.9}{g/L} = 0.9/180 = \qty{5.0}{mmol/L}$;
$0.9\times 15 = \qty{13.5}{g}$ in the distribution volume.
\textbf{2.} $75/15 = \qty{5}{g/L}$: a rise of \qty{500}{mg/dL},
\qty{28}{mmol/L}. The real peak is far lower because absorption is
spread over an hour while disposal removes glucose as it arrives, and
the liver takes up a large share on first pass.
\textbf{3.} $\qty{75}{g}/\qty{60}{min} = \qty{1.25}{g/min}$; over
\qty{15}{L}: \qty{8.3}{mg/dL/min}, four times $u_{0}$.
\textbf{4.} Steady excess $u/a = 8.3/0.02 = \qty{417}{mg/dL}$, time
constant $1/a = \qty{50}{min}$; after an hour $417(1 -
\mathrm{e}^{-1.2}) = \qty{291}{mg/dL}$ above fasting: about
\qty{380}{mg/dL}, \qty{21}{mmol/L}.
\textbf{5.} $L = 0.2\times 0.05/(0.02\times 0.1) = 5$; steady excess
$417/6 = \qty{69}{mg/dL}$, six times smaller than without \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}.
\textbf{6.} The brain burns \qty{120}{g} of glucose a day, stores none
and can use little else: a low glucose gives confusion and coma within
minutes. A high glucose glycates proteins and, over years, damages the
small vessels of \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{retina}, kidney and nerves and the large arteries.
\textbf{7.} $\lambda^{2} + (a + \gamma)\lambda + (a\gamma + s\beta) = 0$:
trace $-0.12$, determinant $0.002 + 0.01 = 0.012$, discriminant
$0.0144 - 0.048 = -0.0336$.
\textbf{8.} $\omega = \sqrt{0.0336}/2 = \qty{0.092}{min^{-1}}$; period
\qty{68}{min}; decay time $2/0.12 = \qty{16.7}{min}$.
\textbf{9.} $\dot g(0) = 100(-0.06 + c\,\omega) = -2$, so $c\,\omega =
0.04$, $c = 0.44$.
\textbf{10.} $g = 0$ when $\tan\omega t = -1/c = -2.29$, $\omega t =
\pi - 1.16 = 1.98$, $t = \qty{21.6}{min}$. At the first minimum, near
\qty{32}{min}: $100\,\mathrm{e}^{-1.94}(\cos 2.97 + 0.44\sin 2.97) =
14.4\times(-0.91) \approx -\qty{13}{mg/dL}$, glucose \qty{77}{mg/dL}.
\textbf{11.} At $t = 0$, $\dot i = 100\beta > 0$: \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} rises while
$g > 0$, and peaks when $\beta g = \gamma i$, which requires $g$ still
positive --- so the peak comes before glucose reaches fasting, and after
glucose has begun to fall (it falls from $t = 0$). In the model the peak
is near \qty{12}{min}.
\textbf{12.} With input spread over an hour the peak occurs when
absorption and disposal balance, lower and later (30--60 min); the
return waits for absorption to end, hence two hours; the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}
overshoot is smaller for a gradual input, so the dip is mild.
\textbf{13.} $L = 2.5$. Healthy $g^{*} = (2/0.02)/6 = \qty{16.7}{mg/dL}$;
resistant $100/3.5 = \qty{28.6}{mg/dL}$: a rise of about
\qty{12}{mg/dL} (\qty{0.7}{mmol/L}), fasting glucose near
\qty{102}{mg/dL}, \qty{5.7}{mmol/L} --- the threshold of impaired fasting
glucose.
\textbf{14.} $i^{*} = \beta g^{*}/\gamma$: healthy $8.3$, resistant
\qty{14.3}{mU/L}, ratio $1.7$: compensated \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} resistance,
hyperinsulinaemia with near-normal glucose.
\textbf{15.} $L = 0.1\times 0.01/0.002 = 0.5$; $g^{*} = 100/1.5 =
\qty{66.7}{mg/dL}$, \qty{50}{mg/dL} above the healthy state:
\qty{2.8}{mmol/L}, fasting glucose \qty{7.8}{mmol/L} (\qty{140}{mg/dL}),
above the diabetic threshold.
\textbf{16.} Discriminant $(a - \gamma)^{2} - 4s\beta = 0.0064 - 0.004 =
0.0024 > 0$: monotone return. $\lambda = -0.06 \pm 0.0245$: $-0.0355$
and $-0.0845$ per minute; slowest time constant \qty{28}{min}, against
\qty{16.7}{min} for the healthy decay: the failing loop returns more
slowly and never undershoots.
\textbf{17.} High glucose with high \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}: resistance with partial
compensation --- type~2; the two-hour value of \qty{13}{mmol/L} exceeds
\qty{11.1}{}, so diabetes, not merely impaired tolerance.
\textbf{18.} No C-peptide means no endogenous \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin}: type~1. Without
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} muscle and fat cannot take up glucose, fat is broken down to
fatty acids and ketones, muscle protein is catabolised for
gluconeogenesis, and glucose is lost in the urine with its calories:
the patient starves in the midst of plenty.
\textbf{19.} $5\times 9/5.5 = \qty{8.2}{\%}$.
\textbf{20.} $1.5\,\mathrm{e}^{0.46\times 3} = 1.5\times 3.97 =
\qty{6.0}{mU/L}$. T$_{4}$ within range, TSH above it: subclinical
hypothyroidism, the gland failing and the \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} compensating.
\textbf{21.} $1.5\,\mathrm{e}^{0.46\times 8} = 1.5\times 39.6 =
\qty{59}{mU/L}$. A low T$_{4}$ with a TSH of only $0.3$ means the
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} is not responding: the lesion is central (\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary} or
\omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{hypothalamus}), not in the thyroid.
\textbf{22.} One half-life: the level falls to \qty{50}{\%}, and the
symptoms creep in over weeks. Cortisol, with a half-life of
\qty{80}{min}, is gone within hours of a missed dose, and a stress on
that day meets no defence: the missed week of thyroxine is
uncomfortable, the missed day of cortisol can kill.
\textbf{23.} T$_{4}$ high (the \omterm{def:b3:adaptive-immunity:antibody}{antibody} drives the gland regardless of
feedback); TSH suppressed by the high T$_{4}$; the log-linear relation
drives it to undetectable --- at $T_{4} = 30$, $1.5\,\mathrm{e}^{-6.9}
= \qty{0.0015}{mU/L}$ --- so an undetectable TSH is the first sign.
\textbf{24.} A \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:hormone}{hormone}'s level reports the balance of its command and
its gland: high TSH with low T$_{4}$ is a failed gland and high TSH with
high T$_{4}$ a runaway \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:axes}{pituitary}; high \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} with high glucose is a
resistant body and low \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} with high glucose a destroyed islet.
Read alone, a T$_{4}$ of \qty{7}{pmol/L} or an \omterm{def:b3:endocrinology:glucose}{insulin} of
\qty{30}{mU/L} does not say where the fault lies; read with its
commander, it does.
\textbf{25.} \omterm{thm:b3:endocrinology:loop}{Loop gain} $L = 5$; period \qty{68}{min} and decay time
\qty{17}{min}; fasting glucose of the failing loop \qty{7.8}{mmol/L}.
\end{solution}
