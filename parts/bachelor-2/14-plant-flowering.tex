\chapter{Reproductive Development and Flowering Control}\label{ch:b2:plant-flowering}

A chrysanthemum grower can make a greenhouse \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower} for Christmas
or for Easter by switching the lights; a winter wheat sown in
spring never flowers at all, because it has not been cold; a
cocklebur that has seen a single night longer than its critical
length is committed to flowering, whatever happens afterward. A
plant has no calendar, but it has clocks, thermometers and light
meters, and it decides once a year, on their evidence, to convert a
\omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem} that has been making leaves into one that will make a
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower}. This chapter follows that decision from the leaf that
measures the night to the \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem} that changes its programme, then
the building of the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower} by a code of genes, and the reverse
transition at the other end of the cycle: the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} that will not
germinate until it is told the season is right.

\section{The transition to flowering}

\begin{definition}[Floral transition and induction]\label{def:b2:plant-flowering:transition}
\index{floral transition}\index{floral induction}\index{inflorescence meristem}
A vegetative \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{shoot apical meristem} makes leaves with a bud in each
axil, indefinitely. At the \emph{floral transition} it becomes an
\emph{inflorescence \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem}}, which makes bracts and, in their
axils, \emph{floral \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristems}}, each of which is determinate: it
makes sepals, petals, \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{stamens} and \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{carpels} and then stops. The change
is triggered by \emph{floral induction} --- a signal, arriving from
the leaves or arising in the plant's own physiology, that switches
the \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem}'s identity genes. Annuals flower once and die;
perennials flower each year from some \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristems} and keep others
vegetative. The timing decides whether \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} meets \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen}, whether
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} ripen before frost, and whether the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower} opens when its
pollinator flies: it is under strong selection, and plants have
evolved several ways of reading the season.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Photoperiodism: the plant measures the night]\label{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}
\index{photoperiodism}\index{short-day plant}\index{long-day plant}\index{phytochrome}
Many plants flower in response to day length. \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{Short-day
plants}} (chrysanthemum, soybean, rice, cocklebur) flower when the
day is shorter than a critical length --- in autumn or spring;
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{long-day plants}} (spinach, wheat, clover, \emph{Arabidopsis})
when it is longer --- in early summer; \emph{day-neutral} plants
(tomato, maize) flower when they are big enough. What the plant
measures is in fact the length of the uninterrupted \emph{night}: a
\omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{short-day plant} is a long-night plant, and a flash of light in the
middle of a long night cancels its effect, whereas a period of
darkness in a long day does not. The measurement is made in the
\emph{leaves}, by the pigment \emph{phytochrome} --- a protein that
switches between a red-absorbing form and a far-red-absorbing form
with each photon it catches, so that its state records the light
--- read against an internal \emph{circadian clock} of about
twenty-four hours: flowering is triggered when light coincides with
a particular phase of the clock, which happens only at the right
day length.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Garner and Allard (1920) found that a mutant tobacco flowered only
in the short days of a winter greenhouse, and that soybeans sown at
intervals through the summer all flowered together in September:
day length, not age or temperature, set the date. Hamner and Bonner
(1938) showed with cocklebur that one long night was enough, that
a minute of light in the middle of the night abolished the effect,
and that shortening the day with no change to the night did not
induce. Borthwick and Hendricks (1952) found that the most
effective light was red and that far-red light given immediately
after red cancelled it --- the signature of phytochrome, isolated
in 1959. Grafting a single induced leaf onto an uninduced plant made
the whole plant flower: the leaf exports a signal.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Florigen]\label{def:b2:plant-flowering:florigen}
\index{florigen}\index{FLOWERING LOCUS T}
The signal the leaf exports was named \emph{florigen} in 1937 and
identified seventy years later as a small protein, the product of
the gene \emph{FLOWERING LOCUS T} (FT). In the leaf, the clock and
phytochrome together switch on FT only when the day length is right
(in \emph{Arabidopsis}, a \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{long-day plant}, when light coincides with
the afternoon peak of the clock-controlled activator CONSTANS);
the FT protein enters the phloem, travels with the sugar stream to
the shoot apex, and there, with a partner protein, switches on the
genes that convert the \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem}. The same protein is the florigen
of rice, a \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{short-day plant}, in which the clock wires it the other
way round. Grafting experiments, the transport of the protein in
the phloem, and the flowering of plants in which FT is expressed
from a leaf-specific promoter all agree: the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower} is decided in
the leaf and executed in the bud.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Vernalisation: the plant counts the cold]\label{prop:b2:plant-flowering:vernalisation}
\index{vernalisation}\index{FLOWERING LOCUS C}
Winter cereals, biennials (carrot, cabbage, foxglove) and many
perennials of cold climates will not flower until they have
experienced weeks of cold --- \emph{vernalisation} --- which
ensures that a plant germinating in autumn waits for spring rather
than flowering into the frost. The cold is sensed by the \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem}
itself, not the leaves; it cannot be replaced by any other
treatment; and its effect is remembered through the rest of the
plant's growth, through hundreds of cell divisions, but not through
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} --- every \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} starts unvernalised. In \emph{Arabidopsis}
the memory is a gene, \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:vernalisation}{FLOWERING LOCUS C}} (FLC), whose product
represses flowering: weeks of cold progressively silence it by
covering its chromatin with repressive marks, the silencing is
copied at each division, and it is erased in the embryo. The plant
thus \emph{integrates} the cold over time --- a few cold days do not
count --- and a mutant that lacks FLC needs no winter.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Why integration protects against a false spring]\label{thm:b2:plant-flowering:integration}
Suppose a repressor is silenced at a constant rate $\alpha$ per day
of cold, so that after $n$ cold days the fraction still active is
$f_n = e^{-\alpha n}$, and flowering is permitted when $f < f_c$.
The number of cold days required is $n_c = \ln(1/f_c)/\alpha$, and
--- because the silencing is stable --- the cold days need not be
consecutive: what counts is their total. A plant with $n_c = 40$
days is not fooled by a fortnight's thaw in January nor by a cold
snap in October; a plant that sensed only the current temperature
would flower at the first warm week. The same integration, with a
threshold, is how the plant reads the accumulated day length before
committing; both are examples of a system responding to the
\emph{integral} of a signal rather than to its instantaneous value.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If each cold day silences a fraction $\alpha$ of the remaining
active copies (or of the chromatin still unmarked), the active
fraction obeys $f_{n+1} = (1 - \alpha)f_n \approx e^{-\alpha}f_n$,
hence $f_n = e^{-\alpha n}$ with $n$ the count of cold days; the
inequality $e^{-\alpha n} < f_c$ gives $n > \ln(1/f_c)/\alpha$.
Days that are not cold neither add nor subtract, since the marks
are stable, so only the total matters.
\end{proof}

\section{Building the flower: the ABC model}

\begin{proposition}[The ABC model]\label{prop:b2:plant-flowering:abc}
\index{ABC model}\index{homeotic gene!floral}
A floral \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem} lays down four concentric whorls: sepals, petals,
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{stamens}, \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{carpels}. Their identities are set by three classes of
\emph{homeotic genes}, acting in overlapping rings: class A alone
in whorl 1 gives sepals; A and B together in whorl 2 give petals;
B and C together in whorl 3 give \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{stamens}; C alone in whorl 4 gives
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{carpels}. A and C repress each other, so that where one is lost the
other spreads over the whole \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower}. The rules predict every single
mutant: lose B and the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower} is sepal--sepal--carpel--carpel;
lose C and it is sepal--petal--petal--sepal, then another \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower}
inside, since C also stops the \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem}; lose A and it is
carpel--stamen--stamen--carpel. Most of these genes encode
\omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factors} of one family (MADS-box), and a fourth
class, E, is needed for all three functions; expressing B and C
together in a leaf turns it into a stamen-like organ. The garden's
double \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flowers} --- roses, peonies, camellias --- are class-C
mutants, their \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{stamens} turned into extra petals.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Coen and Meyerowitz (1991) compared the homeotic mutants of
\emph{Arabidopsis} and of the snapdragon: in both, three classes of
\omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation} each transformed two adjacent whorls, in the same pairs,
and the combinations of mutants behaved as the model predicts (the
triple mutant makes only leaves). Cloning showed the genes to be
\omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factors} expressed in the predicted rings; putting a
B gene under a promoter active in all four whorls gave
petal--petal--stamen--stamen.
\end{proof}

\section{Dormancy and germination}

\begin{definition}[Seed dormancy]\label{def:b2:plant-flowering:dormancy}
\index{dormancy!seed}\index{abscisic acid}\index{gibberellin}\index{stratification}
A viable \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} that does not germinate in conditions that would
support growth is \emph{dormant}. \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{Dormancy} is imposed during \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed}
maturation by \emph{abscisic acid} (ABA), which also drives the
accumulation of reserves and the drying of the embryo, and it is
maintained by the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed coat} (impermeable to water or oxygen,
mechanically restraining) or by the embryo's own state. It is
broken by the signals that mark the end of the unfavourable season:
weeks of cold and damp (\emph{stratification}, the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed}'s
vernalisation), light --- for small \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} that must be near the
surface, sensed by phytochrome --- or darkness, alternating
temperatures, the leaching of inhibitors by rain, the scarification
of the coat by soil or a gut, smoke and heat after fire. The
decision is a balance between ABA and \emph{gibberellin}: the
breaking signals lower ABA and raise gibberellin, which mobilises
the reserves (\cref{ch:b2:angiosperm-reproduction}) and lets the
radicle grow. A population of \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} is not uniform: a fraction
germinates at the first opportunity, the rest wait a year or more,
so that no single bad season kills the whole cohort --- the
\emph{\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} bank}, a hedge against an unpredictable world.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Lettuce \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} (Borthwick, 1952) germinate in the dark after a flash
of red light and not after a flash of far-red; given red then
far-red then red, they germinate --- the last flash decides. The
same reversible pigment that times flowering starts germination.
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{Seeds} of many trees sown fresh in warm soil do nothing; the same
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} after two months in a moist refrigerator germinate at once,
and ABA-deficient mutants germinate on the plant before the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} is
even dry.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Germination as a bet]\label{thm:b2:plant-flowering:bet}
\index{bet hedging}
Let a fraction $g$ of a \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} cohort germinate each year and the
rest survive in the soil with probability $s$ to the next year. In
a good year a germinating \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} leaves $R_g$ \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds}; in a bad year,
none; years are good with probability $p$. A strategy that
germinates everything at once ($g = 1$) has, over a run of years, a
long-term growth rate $\ln$-averaged as $p\ln R_g + (1 - p)\ln 0 =
-\infty$: one bad year extinguishes it. A strategy with $g < 1$
grows in good years by a factor $gR_g + (1 - g)s$ and in bad years
by $(1 - g)s > 0$, so its long-term rate,
\[
r = p\ln\bigl(gR_g + (1 - g)s\bigr) + (1 - p)\ln\bigl((1 - g)s\bigr),
\]
is finite and, for $p$ not too close to 1, is maximised at an
intermediate $g$. With $R_g = 20$, $s = 0.8$ and $p = 0.7$, the
optimum is near $g = 0.7$: germinate most, keep a reserve.
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{Dormancy} is not a failure to germinate but an evolved partial
refusal.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Population size after $T$ years is the product of the yearly
factors, so its growth rate is the average of their logarithms
(the geometric mean rate), which is what selection maximises over
long runs. With $g = 1$ the bad-year factor is zero and the log
is $-\infty$. For $g < 1$ both factors are positive; differentiating
$r$ with respect to $g$ and setting the derivative to zero gives the
optimum, which for the figures quoted lies near $g = 0.7$
(evaluating $r$ at $g = 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1$ gives $1.28$, $1.42$,
$1.40$, $-\infty$).
\end{proof}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:1}
Distinguish vegetative, inflorescence and floral \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristems}, and
say which are determinate.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:2}
Define short-day, long-day and day-neutral plants with an example
of each, and say what a \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{short-day plant} actually measures.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:3}
Give the organ identity of each whorl in the wild type and in the
three single mutants of the \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:abc}{ABC model}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:4}
Name four signals that break \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{dormancy} and, for each, the season
or place it reports.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:5}
A \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{short-day plant} has a critical night of \qty{10}{h}. Say whether
it flowers under: \qty{16}{h} light/\qty{8}{h} dark; \qty{12}{h}/\qty{12}{h};
\qty{12}{h} light, \qty{6}{h} dark, one minute of light, \qty{6}{h} dark;
\qty{8}{h} light, \qty{4}{h} dark, \qty{4}{h} light, \qty{8}{h} dark.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:6}
A leaf of an induced \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{short-day plant} is grafted onto a \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{long-day
plant} kept in long days, and the long-day plant flowers. What does
this show about \omterm{def:b2:plant-flowering:florigen}{florigen}? What if the graft is made with a leaf
that has been induced but has its phloem interrupted at the
petiole?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:7}
Cold silences the repressor at $\alpha = 0.05$ per day and
flowering needs $f < 0.15$. How many cold days are required? A
winter has three cold spells of 15, 12 and 20 days separated by
thaws: is the plant vernalised? What if $\alpha$ were $0.02$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:8}
Explain why a vernalised plant's memory is lost in its \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds}, in
terms of chromatin, and why this is useful.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:9}
Lettuce \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} are given, in the dark, the sequences: R; R then FR;
R, FR, R; R, FR, R, FR. Predict germination in each case and
explain in terms of the two forms of phytochrome.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:plant-flowering:10}
In the bet-hedging model with $R_g = 20$, $s = 0.8$, compute the
long-term growth rate for $g = 0.4$, $0.6$, $0.8$ and $1$ when
$p = 0.7$, and again when $p = 0.95$. How does the optimum shift,
and what does that predict for deserts versus meadows?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Grower's Calendar}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a chrysanthemum grower schedules
flowering with lights, a wheat breeder counts cold, a florist's
double flower is explained, and a seed lot's dormancy is modelled,
ending on the night length that triggers the crop, the cold days
the wheat needs, and the best germination fraction}]
\label{pb:b2:plant-flowering:1}
Chrysanthemum is a \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:photoperiod}{short-day plant} with a critical night length of
\qty{9.5}{h}; induction requires \num{12} consecutive inductive
nights, and flowers open \num{8} weeks after induction. In November
the greenhouse is dark from 17:00 to 07:30. Winter wheat
has $\alpha = 0.06$ per cold day and flowers when $f < 0.10$. \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{Seed}
lot: $R_g = 15$, $s = 0.7$, good years with probability $p$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The greenhouse.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item In June the natural night is \qty{8}{h}. Will the crop
        flower? What must the grower do to sell \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flowers} in August?
  \item The grower covers the crop with black cloth from
        19:00 to 07:00. What night length does the
        plant see, and when must covering start for an opening on
        \qty{15}{August}?
  \item In November the natural night is \qty{14}{h} and the grower
        wants to \emph{delay} flowering to Christmas. Propose a
        lighting schedule and explain why a brief flash suffices.
  \item The flash is given at 21:00 rather than at
        00:00, and induction is not prevented. Explain with
        the critical night length.
  \item One night in the twelve, the cloth is left off. Predict, and
        say what a cocklebur would have done.
  \item The flash is given in green light and fails; in red light it
        works; red followed by far-red fails. Explain.
  \item A single leaf of an induced plant is grafted onto a plant
        under long days. Predict, and say what it identifies.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The wheat.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many cold days does the wheat need?
  \item Sown in October, it gets 10 cold days in November, a mild
        December, 25 in January and 12 in February. When is it
        vernalised?
  \item Sown in March, it gets 6 cold days. Does it flower that
        summer? What is the agricultural consequence?
  \item A mutant lacks the repressor. Predict its behaviour and say
        why breeders call such wheats ``spring wheats''.
  \item Explain why the memory of cold survives the divisions of the
        \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem} through spring but not the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} that makes the
        next generation.
  \item Why must the cold be counted in the \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:meristem}{meristem} and not in the
        leaves, unlike day length?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The florist.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A double rose has petals where the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{stamens} should be and a
        few extra petals in the centre. Which class of gene has
        failed? Give the whorl formula.
  \item Such roses are sterile as \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} parents but sometimes set
        \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed}. Explain both facts from the \omterm{prop:b2:plant-flowering:abc}{ABC model}.
  \item A snapdragon has \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{stamens} in place of petals and \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{carpels} in
        place of sepals. Which class has failed?
  \item Predict the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower} of a plant in which class C is expressed
        in whorl 1 as well as its normal whorls.
  \item Why did the same \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations} produce the same transformations
        in a rose, a snapdragon and \emph{Arabidopsis}, plants
        separated by a hundred million years?
  \item In whorl 2 of a wild-type \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower} both A and B are active.
        Predict the organ if B alone were active there.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} lot.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the long-term growth rate $r$ for $g = 0.5$, $0.7$
        and $0.9$ when $p = 0.8$.
  \item Which of the three is best? What happens at $g = 1$?
  \item Recompute for $p = 0.5$ (a desert). Which $g$ is best now?
  \item A grower sows the whole lot in one field in a good year and
        harvests $R_g$ per \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed}. Explain why his strategy differs
        from the plant's and what he must do to keep the variety
        through a bad year.
  \item The \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} require light to germinate. Explain the adaptive
        sense of this for a small \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed}, and predict what ploughing
        does to the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} bank.
  \item State the result: the critical night length and the covering
        schedule, the wheat's cold requirement, and the best $g$ for
        $p = 0.8$ and $p = 0.5$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
