\chapter{Digestion and Absorption}\label{ch:b1:digestion-absorption}

A cow eats ten kilograms of grass a day and lives on acetic acid; a
horse eats the same grass and lives, in part, on glucose; a rabbit eats
its own night droppings and would sicken without them. None of them
can digest \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{cellulose}, and all of them do, by keeping bacteria that can.
A human, who cannot, digests a meal of bread, meat and butter to
sugars, \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} and \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids} within a few hours and absorbs
nine litres of water a day through a wall one \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} thick. This chapter
describes the \omterm{prop:b1:digestion-absorption:tract}{digestive tract} of a mammal, the \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzymes} and secretions
that take food apart, the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} that take the pieces in, the signals
that time it all, and the partnerships with microbes by which
herbivores live on what no mammalian \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme} can touch.

\section{Heterotrophic nutrition}

\begin{definition}[Nutrition, digestion, absorption]\label{def:b1:digestion-absorption:nutrition}
\index{digestion}\index{absorption}
A heterotroph must take from its food energy and carbon, the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino
acids} and \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids} it cannot make (\cref{ch:b1:biosyntheses-integration}),
vitamins, minerals and water. Food arrives as macromolecules --- \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch},
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}, \omterm{def:b1:lipids:triglyceride}{fat} --- that cannot cross a membrane. \emph{Digestion} is
their \omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:families}{hydrolysis}, by \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzymes}, into monomers small enough to be taken
up: glucose, \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids}, \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids} and glycerol, \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides}.
\emph{Absorption} is the transport of those monomers across the
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} of the gut into the blood or \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:compartments}{lymph}. In animals digestion is
\emph{extracellular}, in the lumen of a tube open at both ends, which
lets food be processed in stages as it moves, and lets the tube's
regions specialise.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The digestive tract of a mammal]\label{prop:b1:digestion-absorption:tract}
\index{digestive tract}
From mouth to anus the tube of a mammal is divided into regions, each
with its own \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium}, secretions and muscle: the \emph{mouth}, where
teeth grind and saliva moistens and begins to digest \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch}; the
\emph{oesophagus}, a conduit; the \emph{stomach}, a bag of acid where
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} are unfolded and cut, and food is held and released as a
paste (chyme); the \emph{small intestine} --- duodenum, jejunum, ileum,
six metres in a human --- where the secretions of the \emph{liver}
(bile) and the \emph{pancreas} (\omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzymes} and bicarbonate) complete
\omterm{def:b1:digestion-absorption:nutrition}{digestion} and where nearly all absorption takes place; the \emph{large
intestine}, where water is recovered and a dense microbial community
ferments what is left; and the rectum. Muscle in the wall
(\cref{ch:b1:body-plans-tissues}) mixes the contents and propels them
by \emph{peristalsis}; sphincters hold them at each stage.
\end{proposition}

\section{Taking food apart}

\begin{proposition}[The digestive enzymes]\label{prop:b1:digestion-absorption:enzymes}
\index{digestive enzyme}
\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\footnotesize\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}\begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm}p{2.9cm}p{4.0cm}l}
\hline
\omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme} & source, site & substrate $\to$ product & optimum \\
\hline
amylase & saliva; pancreas, small intestine & \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} $\to$ maltose, dextrins & pH 7 \\
pepsin & stomach (as pepsinogen) & \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} $\to$ large peptides & pH 2 \\
trypsin, chymotrypsin & pancreas (as zymogens) & \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} $\to$ small peptides & pH 8 \\
carboxypeptidases & pancreas & peptides $\to$ \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} & pH 8 \\
lipase (with colipase) & pancreas & \omterm{def:b1:lipids:triglyceride}{triglycerides} $\to$ \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids} $+$ monoglycerides & pH 8 \\
nucleases & pancreas & nucleic acids $\to$ \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} & pH 8 \\
maltase, sucrase, lactase & brush border of \omterm{def:b1:digestion-absorption:surface}{enterocytes} & \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:glycosidic}{disaccharides} $\to$ \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{monosaccharides} & pH 7 \\
peptidases & brush border & di- and tripeptides $\to$ \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} & pH 7 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme} is a hydrolase (\cref{ch:b1:enzymes}) specific for one
class of bond; each works at the pH of its region; and the proteases
are made and stored as inactive zymogens, activated only in the lumen.
Bile, made by the liver and stored in the gall bladder, contains no
\omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme}: its \emph{bile salts} emulsify \omterm{def:b1:lipids:triglyceride}{fat} into droplets a micrometre
across, on whose surface lipase can work, and carry the products in
micelles to the absorbing \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} (\cref{ch:b1:lipids}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[A meal's chemistry]\label{ex:b1:digestion-absorption:meal}
A slice of bread with butter and cheese: the \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} is attacked by
salivary amylase in the mouth, stopped by the acid of the stomach,
resumed by pancreatic amylase in the duodenum and finished by the
brush-border maltase on the \omterm{def:b1:digestion-absorption:surface}{enterocytes} themselves; the cheese \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}
is unfolded by the acid, cut by pepsin, cut further by trypsin and
chymotrypsin, and finished to \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} and dipeptides at the brush
border; the butter is melted, emulsified by bile, split by lipase into
\omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids} and monoglycerides, and ferried in micelles to the
membrane. Three hours from mouth to blood.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Control of secretion]\label{prop:b1:digestion-absorption:control}
\index{gastrin}\index{secretin}\index{cholecystokinin}
Secretion is timed to the food. The sight and smell of a meal, through
the vagus nerve, start the flow of saliva and gastric juice before the
first bite. Food in the stomach stretches its wall and its peptides
release the hormone \emph{gastrin}, which stimulates acid secretion;
acid entering the duodenum releases \emph{secretin}, which makes the
pancreas pour bicarbonate to neutralise it; \omterm{def:b1:lipids:triglyceride}{fat} and peptides in the
duodenum release \emph{cholecystokinin}, which makes the gall bladder
contract and the pancreas release its \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzymes}, and slows the stomach's
emptying so that the intestine is not overwhelmed. Each secretion is
called by the substrate it acts on.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Beaumont (1833) observed the stomach of a man left with an open
wound, and saw gastric juice appear only when food touched the wall,
and digest meat in a glass as it did in the stomach. Pavlov (1890s)
fitted dogs with pouches of the stomach and fistulae of the pancreatic
duct: the sight of food made the stomach secrete (cut the vagus and it
did not); acid placed in the duodenum made the pancreas secrete even
after every nerve was cut, and Bayliss and Starling (1902) showed that
an extract of duodenal lining injected into the blood did the same
--- the first hormone, secretin.
\end{proof}

\section{Taking the pieces in}

\begin{definition}[The absorptive surface]\label{def:b1:digestion-absorption:surface}
\index{villus}\index{enterocyte}
The small intestine folds its lining into circular folds, then into
\emph{villi} a millimetre high, each covered by a single layer of
\emph{enterocytes} whose apical membranes bear microvilli --- the
\emph{brush border} --- so that a tube of \qty{0.6}{m^2} offers some
\qty{200}{m^2} of membrane (\cref{ch:b1:body-plans-tissues}). Inside
each villus run a capillary network, which carries away sugars and
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} to the liver by the portal vein, and a central
\emph{lacteal}, a lymphatic vessel that carries away \omterm{def:b1:lipids:triglyceride}{fat}. The
enterocytes live four days and are replaced from the crypts between
the villi.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Routes of absorption]\label{prop:b1:digestion-absorption:routes}
\index{chylomicron}
\emph{Sugars}: glucose and galactose enter the \omterm{def:b1:digestion-absorption:surface}{enterocyte} by the
sodium--glucose \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} (\cref{ch:b1:membranes-transport}), fructose
by a carrier, and all leave by a carrier on the basal side into the
blood. \emph{\omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{Amino acids}} enter by several sodium-coupled \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporters},
and small peptides by a proton-coupled one, to be cut inside the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}.
\emph{\omterm{def:b1:lipids:triglyceride}{Fats}}: \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids} and monoglycerides leave their micelles and
diffuse through the apical membrane; inside the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} they are
re-esterified into \omterm{def:b1:lipids:triglyceride}{triglycerides}, coated with \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} into
\emph{chylomicrons} a micrometre across, and exocytosed into the
lacteal, whose \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:compartments}{lymph} reaches the blood only at the neck --- \omterm{def:b1:lipids:triglyceride}{fat} bypasses
the liver. \emph{Water} follows the solutes osmotically:
\qty{9}{L} a day enter the gut (two drunk, seven secreted as saliva,
gastric juice, bile, pancreatic and intestinal juice), and all but a
tenth of a litre are reabsorbed, most in the small intestine and the
rest in the colon. \emph{Vitamins}, \emph{iron} and \emph{calcium}
each have their carriers; vitamin B$_{12}$ needs a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} from the
stomach to be absorbed at all.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Reading an absorption experiment]\label{met:b1:digestion-absorption:everted}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Turn a segment of intestine inside out (an \emph{everted sac}),
        fill it with saline, and bathe it in a solution of the
        substance: what appears inside has crossed the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium}
        from the former lumen.
  \item Measure the transfer against concentration: saturating and
        stopped by the cold or by a poison of \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} synthesis --- active
        or carrier-mediated; linear and insensitive --- diffusion.
  \item Remove sodium from the bath: if glucose or amino-acid transfer
        collapses, it was sodium-coupled. Add a competing sugar: if
        transfer falls, the two share a carrier.
  \item Compare segments: most sugars and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} cross in the
        jejunum, bile salts and vitamin B$_{12}$ only in the ileum,
        water and salt everywhere.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[The water budget]\label{ex:b1:digestion-absorption:water}
Of the nine litres that enter a human gut each day, the small
intestine absorbs eight, the colon a further one, and \qty{0.1}{L}
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} in the faeces. The small intestine's sodium-coupled uptake of
glucose drags water with it --- which is why a solution of glucose and
salt, taken by mouth, rehydrates a child with cholera when the
toxin has turned the crypts into secretors: the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} is untouched
and absorbs water faster than the toxin loses it
(\cref{ch:b1:membranes-transport}).
\end{example}

\section{Living on grass}

\begin{proposition}[Symbiotic digestion of cellulose]\label{prop:b1:digestion-absorption:cellulose}
\index{rumen}\index{ruminant}\index{hindgut fermentation}
No mammal makes a cellulase. Herbivores digest \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{cellulose} by housing
bacteria, protists and fungi that do, in a \omterm{def:b1:respiration-fermentation:fermentation}{fermentation} chamber where
the microbes break the $\beta(1{\to}4)$ bonds (\cref{ch:b1:carbohydrates})
and ferment the sugars, without oxygen, to \emph{volatile \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids}}
--- acetate, propionate, butyrate --- plus carbon dioxide and methane.
The host absorbs the acids and lives on them. \emph{Ruminants} (cattle,
sheep, deer) put the chamber \emph{before} the true stomach: the
\emph{rumen}, a vat of a hundred litres in a cow, where food is
fermented for a day or two, regurgitated and chewed again, and from
which the microbes themselves pass on to the abomasum and intestine to
be digested as the animal's main source of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}. \emph{Hindgut
fermenters} (horses, rabbits, elephants) put the chamber \emph{after}
the small intestine, in an enlarged caecum and colon: they digest
\omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} themselves first, ferment the fibre afterward, and
lose the microbial \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} in their faeces --- unless, like the rabbit,
they eat their soft night droppings and pass them through a second
time (\emph{caecotrophy}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Two ways to eat a meadow]\label{ex:b1:digestion-absorption:cowhorse}
A cow ferments two thirds of the fibre and gets sixty percent of her
energy as acetate and propionate --- from which her liver must make
every gram of glucose she needs, since almost none reaches her
intestine; her rumen also turns the nitrogen of urea, recycled from her
blood into her saliva, into microbial \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}, so she can live on hay
poorer in \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} than any other mammal could. A horse ferments less
than half the fibre but digests the grass's \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} itself,
absorbs glucose, and passes food through in a day and a half instead of
three: it gets less from each kilogram and eats more kilograms. The
cow wins on poor pasture, the horse when it must run.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The length of a gut]\label{rem:b1:digestion-absorption:length}
A carnivore's intestine is four times its body length, a herbivore's
ten to twenty: the poorer the food, the longer the tube and the larger
the chambers. A tadpole eating algae has a long coiled gut that
shortens abruptly at metamorphosis when the frog turns to insects;
the same rule, written in one animal's lifetime.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Cow and the Horse}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- ten kilograms of grass through a
rumen and through a caecum: fibre fermented, acids absorbed, microbial
protein gained or lost, methane vented, ending on the fraction of a
cow's energy that comes from volatile fatty acids}]
\label{pb:b1:digestion-absorption:1}
A cow and a horse each eat \qty{10}{kg} of grass dry matter a day
holding, by mass, \qty{50}{\%} \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{cellulose} and hemicellulose (fibre),
\qty{20}{\%} \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} and sugars, \qty{15}{\%} \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} and \qty{15}{\%}
of indigestible lignin and ash. Energy: \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{carbohydrate} and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}
\qty{17.5}{kJ/g}. \omterm{def:b1:respiration-fermentation:fermentation}{Fermentation} of \qty{1}{g} of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{carbohydrate} yields
volatile \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids} holding \qty{75}{\%} of its energy, methane
holding \qty{8}{\%}, and heat and microbial growth the rest; it also
yields \qty{0.15}{g} of microbial \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}. The rumen ferments
\qty{70}{\%} of the fibre and all the \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} and sugars; the horse's
hindgut ferments \qty{45}{\%} of the fibre, while its small intestine
digests \qty{90}{\%} of the \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} and \qty{70}{\%} of the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}
before the fibre reaches the hindgut. Both animals need \qty{110}{MJ}
of metabolisable energy a day.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The cow's rumen.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the mass of fibre, of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} and sugars, and of
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} eaten per day.
  \item Compute the mass of \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{carbohydrate} fermented in the rumen.
  \item Compute the energy of that \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:monosaccharide}{carbohydrate} and the energy
        recovered as volatile \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids}.
  \item Compute the energy lost as methane, and the mass of methane
        (\qty{55}{kJ/g}) and its volume (\qty{1.4}{L/g}).
  \item Compute the microbial \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} produced.
  \item Explain why the cow's own \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} supply is largely the
        microbes, and where they are digested.
  \item Compute the fraction of the fibre that \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} the rumen
        unfermented, and its fate.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The cow's balance.}
The cow also digests \qty{60}{\%} of the dietary \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} that escapes
the rumen (\qty{40}{\%} of the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} eaten escapes) and all the
microbial \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}, at \qty{17.5}{kJ/g}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the energy the cow obtains from \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} (dietary
        escape plus microbial).
  \item Compute the cow's total metabolisable energy: volatile \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty
        acids} plus \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}.
  \item Compute the fraction of that energy supplied as volatile
        \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids}.
  \item Does the cow meet her need of \qty{110}{MJ}? What does she do
        if not?
  \item Almost no glucose is absorbed by the cow. Name the acid from
        which her liver makes glucose, and the process.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The horse's hindgut.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the energy the horse obtains from \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} digested in
        its small intestine (absorbed as glucose, \qty{17.5}{kJ/g}).
  \item Compute the energy from dietary \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} digested in the small
        intestine.
  \item Compute the fibre fermented in the hindgut and the energy
        recovered as volatile \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids}.
  \item Compute the microbial \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} produced in the hindgut. What
        becomes of it?
  \item Compute the horse's total metabolisable energy and the
        fraction supplied as volatile \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids}.
  \item Does the horse meet its \qty{110}{MJ}? How much grass would it
        need to eat, and how does it manage it?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Comparing designs.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the energy each animal extracts per kilogram of grass
        dry matter.
  \item The cow's methane is \qty{8}{\%} of the fermented energy.
        Compute its share of the cow's gross energy intake, and the
        mass of methane a herd of a hundred cows vents in a year.
  \item The cow's food stays \qty{70}{h} in the tract, the horse's
        \qty{36}{h}. Compute the mass of dry matter each holds in its
        gut at any moment, at constant intake.
  \item A rabbit ferments fibre in its caecum and then eats its
        caecal droppings. What fraction of the microbial \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} of
        question 16 could a horse recover if it did the same? Why does
        no horse do it?
  \item Explain why the cow can live on a diet of straw with
        \qty{4}{\%} \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} plus urea, and the horse cannot.
  \item A sprinting horse burns glucose from its own \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{glycogen}; a cow
        cannot sprint. Relate this to the two designs.
  \item State the result: the fraction of the cow's metabolisable
        energy that comes from volatile \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{fatty acids}, the horse's
        fraction, and the energy each extracts per kilogram of grass.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
