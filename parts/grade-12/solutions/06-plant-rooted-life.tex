\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g12:plant-rooted-life} --- Plant Life: Thriving While Rooted}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:1}
The root system, whose \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:surfaces}{root hairs} present a vast surface to the soil,
and the shoot system, whose leaves present a vast surface to light and
air.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:2}
A pore in the leaf's skin bounded by two guard \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. Carbon dioxide
enters, water vapour (and oxygen) leaves. Open in the light, closed at
night and in drought.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:3}
Raw sap: water and mineral ions, in the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{xylem}, from roots to leaves,
pulled by transpiration. Elaborated sap: water and sugars, in the
\omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{phloem}, from the leaves to the organs that use or store sugar, in
either direction, pushed by the pressure of the sugar-loaded \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:4}
Structural: cuticle and bark, thorns (also stinging hairs, silica,
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{cell walls}). Chemical: tannins, alkaloids (also toxins, resins,
latex).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:5}
A directed growth response to a stimulus (light, gravity, water).
Auxin, made in the growing tip of the shoot.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:6}
$\qty{40}{cm^2} = \qty{4000}{mm^2}$: $4000 \times 200 = \num{8e5}$
\omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata} per leaf; $\num{1.6e11}$ on the tree.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:7}
Around midday to early afternoon. In the heat of the afternoon the
guard \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} close the \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata} partly to limit water loss, and
transpiration follows the opening, not the light.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:8}
The leaves stay green and supplied with water (the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{xylem}, inside the
wood, is intact). The roots, cut off from the leaves' sugar, exhaust
their reserves over months and die, and the tree with them. Just above
the ring the bark swells with the sugar that can no longer pass.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:9}
Intact: the response exists. Tip covered: the tip is where light is
sensed. Base covered: the base need not be lit to bend --- it responds
to a signal, not to light. Tip removed: the tip is necessary; the
signal comes from it. Together they separate sensing (tip) from
responding (base) and show that something travels between them.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:10}
From the sun: the evaporation of water at the leaf surface, driven by
solar heat and dry air, pulls the continuous column of water up the
\omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{xylem}. The plant supplies the tubes and the open \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata}, not the
energy.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:11}
Wind flower: small, dull, scentless, no nectar, huge amounts of light
pollen, feathery stigmas --- the wind is blind and free, so the plant
invests in quantity. Insect flower: large, coloured, scented,
nectar-bearing, sticky pollen in modest amounts --- the carrier must be
attracted and paid, and delivers the pollen precisely.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:12}
It gains water, keeping its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} turgid and alive; it loses the
entry of carbon dioxide, hence \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis}, and the cooling of
transpiration. Closed \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata} mean no sugar made: the plant lives on
its reserves, and a long drought starves it before it dries it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:13}
In the leaf, a defence: a toxin against herbivores, built into the
tissue. In the nectar, a recruitment: a low dose that makes the
pollinator remember and return, paid alongside the sugar. One
substance, two needs, dosed by place.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:14}
Flowers with slightly longer tubes are pollinated only by moths that
reach the bottom, so their pollen goes to flowers of their own kind;
moths with slightly longer tongues get nectar others cannot; each
small advantage is selected, and tube and tongue lengthen together
over many generations. If the moth disappears the flower sets no
seed; if the flower disappears the moth loses its only food.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:plant-rooted-life:15}
A plant senses light with the tip of its shoots and gravity with its
roots; it responds by growing towards the one and along the other; it
defends itself with bark and thorns, and makes toxins and digestion
blockers within hours of an attack; and it recruits insects to carry
its pollen, birds to carry its seeds and wasps to kill its
caterpillars, paying in nectar, fruit and scent. It acts without
moving.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g12:plant-rooted-life:1}
\textbf{1.} $\num{250000} \times \qty{25}{cm^2} = \num{6.25e6}\,
\unit{cm^2} = \qty{625}{m^2}$.

\textbf{2.} $\qty{625}{m^2} = \num{6.25e8}\,\unit{mm^2}$; $\times 250
\approx \num{1.6e11}$ \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata}.

\textbf{3.} $625/150 \approx 4$ square metres of leaf per square metre
of ground.

\textbf{4.} About \qty{1000}{m^2} of \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:surfaces}{root hairs} against \qty{625}{m^2}
of leaves: comparable. Both are exchange surfaces sized to the same
flows --- the water the leaves lose must be absorbed by the roots.

\textbf{5.} Light is absorbed within a fraction of a millimetre of
tissue, and carbon dioxide diffuses only over short distances: a
thick organ would leave most of its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} in the dark and starved.
Thin, stacked leaves put every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} near light and air.

\textbf{6.} $625 \times 0.6 = \qty{375}{L}$.

\textbf{7.} $625 \times 8 = \qty{5}{kg}$ of carbon dioxide, giving
about $5/1.47 \approx \qty{3.4}{kg}$ of sugar (or simply: the sugar
made from \qty{5}{kg} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is about \qty{3.4}{kg});
water used $3.4/1.6 \approx \qty{2.1}{kg}$: about 0.6\% of the
\qty{375}{L} transpired.

\textbf{8.} Carbon dioxide enters through the same open \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata} that
let water out; closing them stops \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis}. The water is the
price of the sugar.

\textbf{9.} The sun, evaporating water at the leaves. Lifting
\qty{375}{kg} by \qty{20}{m} is about \qty{75}{kJ} of work --- little
in itself, but a biological pump would need tissues, ATP and
maintenance the tree does not have to build.

\textbf{10.} It has partly closed its \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata} against the dry heat;
carbon dioxide entry and sugar production fall by a similar fraction
--- some \qty{2}{kg} of sugar lost over the afternoon.

\textbf{11.} About \qty{3.4}{kg} made; \qty{2}{kg} (60\%) to roots,
trunk and shoots, \qty{0.5}{kg} (15\%) to the acorns.

\textbf{12.} The \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{phloem}, from the leaves (source) to the roots, trunk,
shoots and acorns (sinks).

\textbf{13.} Stored as starch in the trunk and roots; next spring it
is turned back into sugar to build the new leaves before they can
feed themselves.

\textbf{14.} Sugar still reaches the shoots and acorns above the ring;
the roots and lower trunk receive none: their \qty{2}{kg} share falls
to whatever was stored, and they starve over the season.

\textbf{15.} The remaining leaves keep making sugar, the stored starch
of the trunk and roots covers the shortfall, and the tree can put out
new leaves from its many growing points; the acorns are a sink the
tree can still fill if the summer's sugar suffices.

\textbf{16.} Small, greenish flowers without petals, scent or nectar,
hanging in catkins that shed clouds of light pollen; large feathery
stigmas to catch it.

\textbf{17.} Acorns dropped beneath the parent grow, if at all, in its
shade and compete with it; a jay carries them hundreds of metres and
buries them at the right depth. Losing most of them is the price of
placing a few well.

\textbf{18.} $3000 \times 0.05 = 150$ forgotten acorns; $150/50 = 3$
saplings.

\textbf{19.} Three saplings a year over a few decades of good crops
give perhaps a hundred saplings in the tree's life, of which one must
survive to adulthood. The numbers are large because almost every seed
and sapling is lost.

\textbf{20.} \qty{625}{m^2} of leaf and some $\num{1.6e11}$ \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata};
about \qty{375}{L} lifted in a day for \qty{3.4}{kg} of sugar; the
wind for its pollen and the jay for its acorns.
\end{solution}
