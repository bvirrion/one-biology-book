\chapter{Innate Immunity and Inflammation}\label{ch:b3:innate-immunity}

A splinter under the skin carries in ten thousand bacteria that will
double every half hour. Within minutes the vessels around it dilate and
leak; within an hour the first \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} have squeezed out of the
blood and are crawling toward the intruders along a chemical trail;
within a day the site is red, hot, swollen and painful --- the four
signs of \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammation} that Celsus listed two thousand years ago --- and
the bacteria are dead, eaten alive by cells that recognised them as
foreign without ever having met them before. This is \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{innate immunity}:
the defence every animal has, ready before infection, encoded in the
genome rather than learned. It is fast, it is what kills most invaders,
and it is what tells the slower, learned immunity of the next chapter
that there is something to learn. Its excesses --- septic shock, chronic
\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammation}, the fevers of autoinflammatory disease --- are as
instructive as its successes.

\section{Layers and cells}

\begin{definition}[Innate immunity]\label{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}
\index{innate immunity}\index{neutrophil}\index{macrophage}\index{dendritic cell}\index{natural killer cell}\index{barrier}
\emph{Innate immunity} is the set of defences present before any
exposure, encoded by germline genes that do not rearrange, recognising
conserved features of microbes rather than specific individuals, acting
within minutes to hours, and (with qualifications) without memory. Its
first layer is the \emph{barriers}: the skin's keratin, the mucus and
cilia of the airways, the acid of the stomach, the flushing of urine
and tears, the enzyme lysozyme that cuts \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{peptidoglycan}, the
\emph{antimicrobial peptides} (defensins, cathelicidins) that puncture
microbial membranes, and the commensal \omterm{def:b3:microbiomes:symbiosis}{microbiome} that occupies the
niches (\cref{ch:b3:microbiomes}). Its cells, all from the myeloid
lineage except the last: \emph{neutrophils}, \qty{60}{\%} of the blood's
leukocytes, $10^{11}$ made a day, living a day, the first to arrive and
the main killers of bacteria; \emph{macrophages}, long-lived residents
of every tissue (Kupffer cells in the liver, microglia in the brain,
alveolar macrophages in the lung) that eat, clean up and raise the
alarm; \emph{dendritic cells}, which sample tissues and carry what they
find to the lymph nodes; mast cells, basophils and eosinophils, armed
against parasites and responsible for allergy; and \emph{natural killer}
(NK) cells, lymphocytes that kill infected or transformed cells on
sight.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Metchnikoff (1882), in Messina, pushed a rose thorn into a transparent
starfish larva and watched, the next morning, motile cells crowding
round it; he had seen the same cells engulf food particles and dye
grains, and proposed that they engulfed microbes too --- \emph{phagocytes},
the agents of a host defence he then traced in every animal he could
find, from amoebae to man. He shared the Nobel prize of 1908 with
Ehrlich, whose antibodies represented the opposite, humoral view; both
were right, and a century later the two halves of immunity were shown
to be one system, the innate half instructing the adaptive.
\end{proof}

\section{Recognising the enemy}

\begin{definition}[Pattern recognition]\label{def:b3:innate-immunity:prr}
\index{pattern recognition receptor}\index{pathogen-associated molecular pattern}\index{Toll-like receptor}\index{damage-associated molecular pattern}\index{inflammasome}
Innate cells recognise \emph{pathogen-associated molecular patterns}
(PAMPs): molecules common to whole classes of microbes, essential to
them, and absent from the host --- \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{lipopolysaccharide} of \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negative}
walls, \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{peptidoglycan} and lipoteichoic acid of \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-positives},
flagellin, fungal $\beta$-glucans, double-stranded RNA, RNA without a
5$'$ cap, DNA with unmethylated \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG}, DNA in the cytosol. The receptors
are \emph{pattern recognition receptors} (PRRs), a few dozen in all,
each encoded by one gene: the \emph{Toll-like receptors} (TLRs) on the
surface and in \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{endosomes} (TLR4 for \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{lipopolysaccharide}, TLR5 for
flagellin, TLR3 for double-stranded RNA, TLR9 for \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG} DNA); the
cytosolic NOD proteins for \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{peptidoglycan} fragments, RIG-I for viral
RNA, cGAS--STING for cytosolic DNA; C-type lectins for fungal sugars.
The same receptors, or others of the same families, recognise
\emph{damage-associated molecular patterns} (DAMPs) released by injured
cells --- ATP, uric acid, the nuclear protein HMGB1, mitochondrial DNA
--- so that sterile injury also inflames. Binding activates the
transcription factors NF-$\kappa$B and the interferon regulatory
factors, which switch on \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{cytokines}, \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{chemokines}, adhesion molecules and
antimicrobial genes within an hour; a subset of the cytosolic sensors
assemble the \emph{inflammasome}, a platform that activates caspase-1
to cut interleukin-1$\beta$ into its active form and to trigger the
inflammatory death called \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:other}{pyroptosis} (\cref{ch:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis}).
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Janeway proposed in 1989 that the adaptive immune system does not
respond to antigen alone but needs a signal from innate receptors that
have recognised microbial patterns --- the explanation of why vaccines
need adjuvants, ``the immunologist's dirty little secret''. Hoffmann
(1996) found that \emph{Drosophila} lacking the receptor Toll, known
for its role in embryonic patterning, could not mount an antifungal
response and died covered in mould; Beutler (1998) mapped the
lipopolysaccharide-resistance of a mouse strain, which survived doses
of endotoxin lethal to others and failed to clear \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negative}
infections, to a mutation in the mammalian homologue TLR4. The receptor
for endotoxin, sought for fifty years, was a Toll.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Complement]\label{def:b3:innate-immunity:complement}
\index{complement}\index{C3 convertase}\index{opsonisation}\index{membrane attack complex}\index{anaphylatoxin}
\emph{Complement} is a system of some thirty plasma proteins,
\qty{10}{\%} of the serum globulins, that marks and destroys microbes
by a cascade of proteolytic activations. Three pathways converge on the
cleavage of the central protein C3: the \emph{classical} pathway,
started by C1q binding antibodies on a surface (or \omterm{prop:b3:innate-immunity:systemic}{C-reactive protein},
or apoptotic cells); the \emph{lectin} pathway, started by
mannose-binding lectin recognising microbial sugars; and the
\emph{alternative} pathway, in which C3 hydrolyses spontaneously at a
low rate and the resulting C3b attaches to any surface it meets. Each
pathway builds a \emph{C3 convertase}, an enzyme that cleaves more C3;
the C3b it deposits covalently on the surface builds more convertase in
turn --- an amplification loop --- so that a bacterium is coated with
millions of C3b within minutes. C3b is an \emph{opsonin}: phagocytes
carry receptors for it and eat what it coats. The small fragments C3a
and C5a are \emph{anaphylatoxins} that dilate vessels, attract
\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} and activate mast cells. And C5b assembles C6--C9 into the
\emph{membrane attack complex}, a pore of \qty{10}{nm} that lyses
\omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negative} bacteria and enveloped viruses. Host cells are spared
because they carry regulators --- factor H, CD55, CD59 --- that
dismantle the convertase and block the pore on their own surfaces;
microbes, lacking them, are consumed by the loop.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The complement loop as a kinetic discriminator]\label{thm:b3:innate-immunity:complement}
\index{complement!amplification loop}
Let $B(t)$ be the number of C3b molecules deposited on a surface. C3b
is deposited at a small constant rate $k_{0}$ from the tick-over of
C3, and each deposited C3b builds convertase that deposits more at
rate $a$ per C3b per second, while regulators (host) and decay remove
convertase activity at rate $d$ per C3b per second:
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_{0} + (a - d)\,B .
\]
On a surface where $a > d$ --- a microbe, without regulators ---
$B(t) = \bigl[k_{0}/(a-d)\bigr]\bigl(e^{(a-d)t} - 1\bigr)$ grows
exponentially with time constant $1/(a - d)$; on a host surface where
$d > a$, $B$ approaches the small steady value $k_{0}/(d - a)$. The
same molecules, at the same concentrations, deposit a million C3b on a
bacterium in a few minutes and a few dozen on the cell beside it: the
distinction between self and non-self is made not by recognition but by
the sign of $a - d$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The equation is linear with constant coefficients. For $a \ne d$ the
fixed point is $B^{*} = -k_{0}/(a-d)$; writing $B = B^{*} + u$,
$\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = (a-d)u$, so $u = u_{0}e^{(a-d)t}$ and with
$B(0) = 0$, $B(t) = \bigl[k_{0}/(a-d)\bigr](e^{(a-d)t} - 1)$. For $a > d$
this grows without bound (until C3 or surface runs out); for $a < d$
the exponential decays and $B \to k_{0}/(d-a) > 0$. With $k_{0} = 1$
per second, $a - d = \qty{0.1}{s^{-1}}$ on a bacterium, $B(\qty{2}{min})
= 10(e^{12} - 1) \approx 1.6\times 10^{6}$; with $d - a =
\qty{0.1}{s^{-1}}$ on a host cell, $B^{*} = 10$.
\end{proof}

\section{Inflammation}

\begin{definition}[Acute inflammation]\label{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}
\index{inflammation}\index{cytokine}\index{chemokine}\index{extravasation}\index{selectin}\index{integrin}
\emph{Inflammation} is the response of vascularised tissue to injury or
infection, orchestrated by \emph{cytokines} --- the small signalling
proteins of immune cells, here chiefly TNF, interleukin-1 and
interleukin-6 from activated \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{macrophages} --- and by \emph{chemokines},
the cytokines that direct migration. Its steps explain Celsus's signs.
Histamine from mast cells, nitric oxide and prostaglandins dilate the
arterioles: \emph{redness} and \emph{heat}. The endothelium of the
venules contracts and leaks plasma, whose proteins --- \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:complement}{complement},
antibodies, clotting factors --- flood the tissue: \emph{swelling}.
Bradykinin and prostaglandin E$_{2}$ sensitise nerve endings:
\emph{pain}. And the leukocytes arrive by \emph{extravasation}: TNF and
interleukin-1 make the endothelium display \emph{selectins}, on which
passing \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} catch and roll; chemokines (interleukin-8) on the
endothelial surface activate the \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils}' \emph{integrins}, which
bind tightly to endothelial adhesion molecules and stop the cell; and
the \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophil} squeezes between the endothelial cells and follows the
chemokine gradient into the tissue --- the whole sequence in a few
minutes, at up to a million cells an hour into an infected site.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Fever, the acute phase and resolution]\label{prop:b3:innate-immunity:systemic}
\index{fever}\index{acute-phase response}\index{C-reactive protein}\index{resolution of inflammation}
Interleukin-1, TNF and interleukin-6 also act at a distance. In the
hypothalamus they induce prostaglandin E$_{2}$, which raises the set
point of body temperature: \emph{fever}, which slows many bacteria,
speeds the immune cells' own reactions, and costs about \qty{12}{\%}
more metabolism per degree. In the liver they induce the
\emph{acute-phase proteins} --- \omterm{prop:b3:innate-immunity:systemic}{C-reactive protein}, which binds
bacterial phosphocholine and activates \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:complement}{complement} and rises a
thousandfold within two days, the clinician's measure of \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammation};
fibrinogen; mannose-binding lectin; and hepcidin, which locks iron away
from the microbes that need it. In the marrow they release \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils}
and speed their production. \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{Inflammation} is meant to end: as the
stimulus is cleared, the lipid mediators switch from prostaglandins to
lipoxins and resolvins, \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} die by \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} and are eaten by
\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{macrophages}, which then switch to a repair programme, secreting growth
factors and interleukin-10 --- \emph{resolution}. When the stimulus
persists (a tubercle bacillus that cannot be killed, a crystal, a
self-antigen) the response becomes chronic: \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{macrophages} wall the site
in a granuloma, fibroblasts lay down scar, and the tissue is destroyed
by its own defence.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Sepsis]\label{ex:b3:innate-immunity:sepsis}
\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{Inflammation} confined to a splinter is a cure; the same reactions
throughout the body are a catastrophe. When bacteria or their
\omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{lipopolysaccharide} reach the blood in quantity, \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{macrophages} everywhere
release TNF and interleukin-1 at once: every vessel dilates and leaks,
blood pressure collapses, clotting is activated in ten thousand
capillaries and consumes the clotting factors, and the organs, starved
of perfusion, fail --- \emph{septic shock}, which kills a quarter to a
half of those it strikes, some eleven million people a year. A few
micrograms of \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{lipopolysaccharide} injected into a volunteer produce
fever, rigors and a fall in blood pressure within two hours; a mouse
lacking TLR4 shrugs off a dose a hundred times lethal. The disease is
the host's response, and the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics} that kill the bacteria can
make it worse for a few hours by releasing more endotoxin.
\end{example}

\section{Killing}

\begin{definition}[Phagocytosis and the respiratory burst]\label{def:b3:innate-immunity:killing}
\index{phagocytosis}\index{respiratory burst}\index{NADPH oxidase}\index{neutrophil extracellular trap}
A phagocyte binds its prey through receptors for opsonins --- C3b, and
the constant regions of antibodies --- or directly through pattern
receptors, wraps it in membrane, and closes the \emph{phagosome}, which
fuses with granules and \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosomes}. Killing is by several means at once.
The \emph{respiratory burst}: the enzyme \emph{NADPH oxidase},
assembled on the phagosome membrane, pumps electrons onto oxygen to make
superoxide, which becomes hydrogen peroxide, which myeloperoxidase
turns, with chloride, into hypochlorite --- bleach, at millimolar
concentration inside a compartment a micrometre across. Nitric oxide
from inducible NO synthase, which with superoxide gives peroxynitrite.
Acid, to pH~5. Lysozyme, proteases, lactoferrin that starves the prey
of iron, and defensins that puncture it. A \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophil} that cannot eat
what it finds --- a fungal hypha, a clump --- can expel its own
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} as a sticky net of DNA and granule proteins, a \emph{\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophil}
extracellular trap}, and die doing it. Children who inherit a defective
NADPH oxidase (chronic granulomatous disease) suffer recurrent
abscesses and granulomas from bacteria and fungi that ordinary
phagocytes kill without difficulty: the burst is not optional.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Natural killer cells and interferon]\label{def:b3:innate-immunity:nk-ifn}
\index{natural killer cell!missing self}\index{interferon!type I}\index{antiviral state}
Viruses hide inside cells, where \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:complement}{complement} and phagocytes cannot
reach them. \emph{\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{Natural killer cells}} patrol for cells that have
stopped displaying MHC class~I --- the ``missing self'' of a cell whose
viral or \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumour} occupant has switched off antigen presentation to
escape T~cells (\cref{ch:b3:adaptive-immunity}) --- and for stress
proteins that infected and transformed cells put on their surface;
inhibitory receptors for MHC~I and activating receptors for the stress
ligands are summed, and a cell that tips the balance is killed within
minutes by perforin and granzymes injected into it, which trigger its
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis}. \emph{Type~I interferons} ($\alpha$ and $\beta$) are made by
any cell whose RIG-I or cGAS has detected viral nucleic acid; secreted,
they bind receptors on neighbouring cells and, through the JAK--STAT
pathway, induce hundreds of genes that put those cells into an
\emph{antiviral state}: a kinase that shuts down translation when it
meets double-stranded RNA, a nuclease that degrades RNA, proteins that
block viral entry and assembly, and more MHC~I to show T~cells what is
inside. The cell that made the interferon may die; its neighbours are
forewarned. Interferon was discovered by Isaacs and Lindenmann (1957)
as the factor in the medium of virus-infected cells that made fresh
cells resistant, and it is the reason a cell infected by one \omterm{def:b3:virology:virus}{virus}
resists a second.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Measuring inflammation and phagocyte function]\label{met:b3:innate-immunity:tests}
\index{C-reactive protein!assay}\index{nitroblue tetrazolium test}
At the bedside: (1) the \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:innate-immunity:systemic}{C-reactive protein}} in serum, by
immunoassay --- below \qty{5}{mg/L} at rest, tens in a viral infection,
hundreds in bacterial sepsis, falling within days of effective
treatment; (2) the \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophil} count, raised in bacterial infection,
with immature forms released early from the marrow; (3) the
erythrocyte sedimentation rate, a slow proxy for fibrinogen. For the
phagocytes: (4) the \emph{nitroblue tetrazolium} or dihydrorhodamine
test, in which \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} stimulated in vitro reduce a dye if their
\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:killing}{NADPH oxidase} works --- the diagnosis of chronic granulomatous disease
in a morning; (5) a chemotaxis assay, counting cells that migrate
through a filter toward a \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{chemokine}.
\end{method}

\section{From innate to adaptive, and across life}

\begin{proposition}[The dendritic cell's decision]\label{prop:b3:innate-immunity:bridge}
\index{dendritic cell!maturation}\index{costimulation}\index{adjuvant}
Adaptive immunity does not start itself. A \emph{\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{dendritic cell}} in a
tissue eats what it finds; if its pattern receptors are triggered ---
by microbe or by damage --- it matures: it stops eating, migrates
through the lymphatics to the draining lymph node, displays the
peptides of what it ate on MHC molecules, and puts up
\emph{costimulatory} molecules (B7) and \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{cytokines} that tell a T~cell
recognising the peptide to respond, and how (\cref{ch:b3:adaptive-immunity}).
A T~cell that sees its peptide on a \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{dendritic cell} without
costimulation is switched off. Innate recognition thus decides whether
an antigen is worth a response, and the \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{cytokines} the \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{dendritic cell}
makes --- decided by which pattern receptors fired --- decide whether
the response is aimed at viruses, bacteria or worms. An
\emph{adjuvant} is a pattern-receptor ligand added to a vaccine to
supply this signal; the alum and the lipid nanoparticles of modern
vaccines work by triggering it.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Innate immunity everywhere, and its memory]\label{rem:b3:innate-immunity:everywhere}
Every multicellular organism has \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{innate immunity}, and plants, fungi
and invertebrates have nothing else. Plants recognise flagellin and
chitin with receptor kinases and mount a two-tier response
(\cref{ch:b3:plant-molecular-physiology}); insects have Toll and
antimicrobial peptides; even bacteria have \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{restriction enzymes} and
\omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:crispr}{CRISPR} (\cref{ch:b3:genetic-engineering}). The dogma that \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{innate
immunity} has no memory has softened: a monocyte that has met
$\beta$-glucan or the BCG vaccine is epigenetically reprogrammed
(\cref{ch:b3:chromatin-epigenetics}) to respond more strongly to
unrelated microbes for months --- \emph{trained immunity}, which
explains why BCG lowers infant mortality from infections other than
tuberculosis. And the system's excess is a disease of our time:
sterile \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammation} driven by uric acid crystals (gout), cholesterol
crystals in the arterial wall (atherosclerosis), \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:amyloid}{amyloid} in the brain,
and the low-grade \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammation} of obesity and old age.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: A Splinter}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- ten thousand bacteria under the skin
raced against the neutrophils sent to meet them, coated with complement
by the arithmetic of the loop, fought with fever at its metabolic
price, and, in the bloodstream, turned into the physiology of shock,
ending on the bacteria left after six hours, the C3b on each one and
the cost of a day's fever}]
\label{pb:b3:innate-immunity:1}
Data: $10^{4}$ bacteria inoculated, doubling every \qty{40}{min};
\omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} arrive from \qty{1}{h} at $2\times 10^{4}$ per hour, each
killing $20$ bacteria then dying. \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:complement}{Complement}: $k_{0} = 1$ per second
per bacterium, $a - d = \qty{0.08}{s^{-1}}$ on the bacteria, $d - a =
\qty{0.1}{s^{-1}}$ on host cells; a bacterium's surface holds at most
$2\times 10^{6}$ C3b. Fever: $+\qty{2}{\celsius}$ costs \qty{24}{\%} of
an \qty{8}{MJ/day} metabolism and lengthens the bacterial doubling time
to \qty{60}{min}. Sepsis: $10^{9}$ bacteria in \qty{5}{L} of blood,
$3\times 10^{6}$ \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{lipopolysaccharide} molecules per bacterium; TLR4
signalling saturates at $10^{-9}$ mol/L of \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{lipopolysaccharide}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The race.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many bacteria at \qty{1}{h}, when the first \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils}
        arrive, if none have been killed?
  \item Between \qty{1}{h} and \qty{2}{h}, how many \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} arrive
        and how many bacteria can they kill? Compare with the bacterial
        growth in that hour from the count of question 1.
  \item Continue hour by hour (growth, then killing at the end of each
        hour) to \qty{6}{h}. When does the population peak, and how many
        remain at \qty{6}{h}?
  \item Repeat with a \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophil} arrival delayed to \qty{3}{h}. What
        happens by \qty{6}{h}? Comment on the value of speed.
  \item How many dead \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} accumulate by the time the bacteria
        are gone in question 3? What is pus, and why does it need to be
        cleared?
  \item A person with a tenth of the normal \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophil} count
        (neutropenia after chemotherapy): recompute question 3 and
        explain why such patients are given \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics} at the first
        fever.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The coat.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item From the theorem, how many C3b are deposited on one bacterium
        after \qty{30}{s}, \qty{60}{s} and \qty{120}{s}?
  \item When does the surface saturate?
  \item On a host cell beside it, what is the steady-state number of
        C3b?
  \item The bacterium acquires a capsule that halves $a$, so that $a -
        d = 0.03$. Recompute the C3b at \qty{120}{s}. What does the
        capsule buy?
  \item How many C3b does the whole inoculum of $10^{4}$ bacteria
        carry at saturation, and what fraction is that of the
        $2\times 10^{16}$ C3 molecules in a litre of plasma?
  \item A patient lacks C3. Which pathways are affected, and what
        infections follow? A patient lacks C9: why is the phenotype
        milder, and confined to one genus?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Fever.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How much extra energy does two days of fever at
        $+\qty{2}{\celsius}$ cost, in megajoules and in grams of fat
        (\qty{38}{kJ/g})?
  \item Redo question 1 at fever temperature: how many bacteria at
        \qty{1}{h}? By what factor has fever reduced the population the
        \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{neutrophils} meet?
  \item In the model of question 3, with the longer doubling time,
        when are the bacteria gone?
  \item \omterm{prop:b3:innate-immunity:systemic}{C-reactive protein} rises from \qty{1}{mg/L} to \qty{200}{mg/L} in
        two days. With a plasma volume of \qty{3}{L} and a molecular
        mass of \qty{115}{kDa}, how many molecules has the liver made,
        and how many per second?
  \item Antipyretic drugs block prostaglandin synthesis. What do they
        do to the set point, the comfort of the patient and, by the
        numbers above, to the infection?
  \item Why is a fever of \qty{41}{\celsius} dangerous when
        \qty{39}{\celsius} is not, and what stops the set point rising
        indefinitely?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Shock.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Lipopolysaccharide} molecules in the blood of the septic
        patient, and their concentration in mol/L.
  \item Compare with the saturating concentration for TLR4. Are the
        body's \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{macrophages} all activated?
  \item Each of the body's $10^{11}$ \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:innate}{macrophages} releases \num{1000} TNF
        molecules a second for an hour. How many moles of TNF, and what
        concentration in \qty{15}{L} of extracellular fluid? (TNF acts
        at \qty{1e-11}{mol/L}.)
  \item Explain the fall in blood pressure and the failure of the
        kidneys from the local mechanisms of \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammation}.
  \item The \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotic} given at admission kills the bacteria within an
        hour. Why may the patient worsen before improving, and what
        limits the usefulness of anti-TNF antibodies in sepsis?
  \item A mouse strain lacks TLR4. Predict its response to injected
        \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{lipopolysaccharide} and to a live \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negative} infection, and
        explain the apparent paradox.
  \item Summarise: the bacteria remaining at \qty{6}{h} (question~3),
        the C3b on one bacterium at \qty{120}{s} (question~7), and the
        cost of two days' fever (question~13).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
