\chapter{Gene Expression: Transcription and Translation}\label{ch:b1:gene-expression}

A \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} of thirty thousand \omterm{thm:b1:nucleic-acids:helix}{base pairs} is read in a quarter of an hour
into a working copy, cut down to two thousand letters, shipped out of
the nucleus, and translated by twenty-five \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes} at once into a
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} of five hundred \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} --- one every four seconds, for as
long as the copy lasts. The \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} spends more energy on this than on
anything else it does. This chapter follows the information from \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}
to \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}: the copying of a \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} into \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA}, the editing of that \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} in
eukaryotes, the code that maps triplets of bases onto \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids}, the
\omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosome} that reads it, and what happens to a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} once it is made.

\section{From gene to protein}

\begin{proposition}[The flow of information]\label{prop:b1:gene-expression:dogma}
\index{gene expression}\index{transcription}\index{translation}
Genetic information flows from \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} to \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} to \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}.
\emph{Transcription} copies one strand of a \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} into an \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} of the
same sequence as the other strand (with U for T); \emph{translation}
reads the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{messenger RNA} three bases at a time and assembles the
corresponding \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} into a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{polypeptide}. Every step uses the
pairing of bases: \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} with \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} in transcription, messenger with
\omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:trna}{transfer RNA} in translation. The sequence of a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} is thus a
transcript of the sequence of its \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}, and a change of one base can
change one \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid} --- the link between mutation and phenotype.
Information does not flow back from \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} to nucleic acid; the one
reverse step, \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} copied into \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} by retroviruses, does not touch
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}.
\end{proposition}

\section{Transcription}

\begin{definition}[RNA polymerase, promoter, transcription]\label{def:b1:gene-expression:transcription}
\index{RNA polymerase}\index{promoter}
\emph{\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} polymerase} synthesises \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} on a \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} template, $5' \to 3'$,
from the four ribonucleoside triphosphates, without a primer. It
starts at a \emph{promoter}, a sequence just upstream of the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}
that it recognises and binds; in bacteria a subunit called
$\sigma$ (\emph{sigma}) does the recognising, at two conserved
stretches ten and thirty-five pairs before the start. The \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme} opens
about fifteen pairs of the helix into a \emph{transcription bubble},
copies the \emph{template strand} (read $3' \to 5'$) into an \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA}
identical in sequence to the other, \emph{coding} strand, and moves
along at about fifty \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} a second, re-closing the helix behind
it; the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} peels off as it is made. It stops at a
\emph{terminator}: in bacteria a self-complementary sequence whose \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA}
folds into a hairpin that pulls the transcript free. Many polymerases
can follow one another along a \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}, so a transcript can be started
every second.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Eukaryotic transcription and RNA processing]\label{prop:b1:gene-expression:processing}
\index{splicing}\index{messenger RNA}\index{cap}\index{poly-A tail}
Eukaryotes have three polymerases: I for the large \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{ribosomal RNAs}, II
for \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{messenger RNAs}, III for transfer and small \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNAs}. Polymerase II does
not recognise its \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:transcription}{promoter} alone: a set of \emph{general transcription
factors} assembles on it (at a TATA sequence about thirty pairs
upstream in many \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes}) and recruits the \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme}, and regulatory
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} bound near or far modulate the rate
(\cref{ch:b1:expression-control}). The primary transcript is
\emph{processed} in the nucleus: a modified guanine \emph{cap} is added
to the $5'$ end, a tail of some two hundred adenines (\emph{poly-A})
to the $3'$ end, and the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{introns} are removed by \emph{splicing} --- a
complex of small \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNAs} and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins}, the \emph{spliceosome}, recognises
the GU at each \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{intron}'s start and the AG at its end, cuts, and joins
the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{exons}. Only then is the mature messenger exported to the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol}.
Many \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes} are spliced in more than one way (\emph{alternative
splicing}), so that one \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} yields several \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins}; the twenty
thousand human \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes} make perhaps a hundred thousand.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Sharp and Roberts (1977) hybridised a viral \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{messenger RNA} to the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA}
of its \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} and looked at the hybrids in the \omterm{prop:b1:cell-unit-of-life:microscopes}{electron microscope}: the
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} paired with the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} in several stretches, and between them the
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{DNA} looped out unpaired --- the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{introns}, present in the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} and
absent from the message. Bacterial \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{genes}, treated the same way, gave
no loops. The size of a \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}'s primary transcript, measured in the
nucleus, matches the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}; the size of the cytoplasmic messenger
matches the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{exons} alone.
\end{proof}

\section{The genetic code}

\begin{definition}[The genetic code]\label{def:b1:gene-expression:code}
\index{genetic code}\index{codon}\index{reading frame}
The \emph{genetic code} maps triplets of messenger bases,
\emph{codons}, onto \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids}. Of the 64 codons, 61 specify the
twenty \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} and three (UAA, UAG, UGA) are \emph{stop} signals;
AUG specifies methionine and is also the \emph{start} codon, so every
new \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{polypeptide} begins with methionine. The code is
\emph{degenerate} --- most \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} have several codons, differing
mostly at the third position --- \emph{non-overlapping}, read without
punctuation in a \emph{reading frame} fixed by the start codon, and
almost \emph{universal}, the same in bacteria, plants and animals
with minor variants in \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:mitochondrion}{mitochondria}: a human \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} put into a
bacterium is read correctly.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[How the code was read]\label{prop:b1:gene-expression:nirenberg}
The code is a non-overlapping triplet code, and each \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon}'s meaning
can be determined by chemistry.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Crick and Brenner (1961) made mutations in a phage \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} that added or
removed one base: one such change destroyed the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}'s function, as
did two, but three insertions (or three deletions) close together
restored it --- the message is read in threes, from a fixed starting
point, and an insertion shifts the frame of everything downstream.
Nirenberg and Matthaei (1961) added a synthetic \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA} of uracils only to
a cell-free extract of \emph{E. coli} with the twenty \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids}: it
made a chain of phenylalanine only, so UUU means Phe; other synthetic
messengers, and then binding of single trinucleotides to \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes}
with their \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:trna}{transfer RNAs}, assigned all sixty-four by 1966.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Reading a message]\label{ex:b1:gene-expression:reading}
The messenger $5'$-\ldots{}GC\underline{AUG}GCUUUCGGAUAA\ldots{}-$3'$
is read from the AUG: AUG GCU UUC GGA UAA --- Met-Ala-Phe-Gly-stop: a
peptide of four residues. Delete the first G after AUG and the frame
shifts: AUG CUU UCG GAU AA\ldots{} --- Met-Leu-Ser-Asp\ldots, a
different \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} of a different length. Change UUC to UUU and nothing
changes: both are Phe, a \emph{silent} mutation.
\end{example}

\section{Translation}

\begin{definition}[Transfer RNA and its synthetases]\label{def:b1:gene-expression:trna}
\index{transfer RNA}\index{anticodon}\index{aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase}
A \emph{transfer \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:chain}{RNA}} (\cref{ch:b1:nucleic-acids}) is the adaptor
between \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid}: its \emph{anticodon}, three bases in a
loop, pairs antiparallel with a \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} of the message, and its $3'$
end carries the corresponding \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid}. The pairing at the third
\omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} position is loose (\emph{wobble}), so about forty tRNAs suffice
for sixty-one \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codons}. Each tRNA is loaded by its own
\emph{aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase}, an \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzyme} that recognises both the
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid} and the tRNA and joins them in two steps at the cost of
one \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} (split to AMP: two high-energy bonds), proofreading the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino
acid} as it does so. These twenty \omterm{def:b1:enzymes:enzyme}{enzymes} are where the code is
actually implemented: the \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosome} does not check which \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid} a
tRNA carries, only that its anticodon matches.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The ribosome]\label{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}
\index{ribosome}
The \emph{ribosome} is a particle of two subunits, each of \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{ribosomal
RNA} and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} (in bacteria: a small 30S subunit and a large 50S
subunit, together 70S, \qty{2.5}{MDa}; eukaryotic ribosomes are
larger, 80S). The small subunit binds the messenger and decodes it;
the large subunit holds the tRNAs and forms the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{peptide bond} --- the
catalyst is the \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{ribosomal RNA} itself. Three tRNA sites span both
subunits: A (aminoacyl, where the next charged tRNA enters), P
(peptidyl, holding the growing chain), E (exit).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The cycle of elongation]\label{prop:b1:gene-expression:elongation}
Translation starts when the small subunit finds the start \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} ---
in bacteria by pairing a \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{ribosomal RNA} sequence with a site just
upstream of the AUG, in eukaryotes by binding the cap and scanning to
the first AUG --- and the initiator tRNA (methionine) settles in the P
site; the large subunit then joins. Each round of \emph{elongation}
adds one residue: a charged tRNA whose \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:trna}{anticodon} matches the A-site
\omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} is delivered by an elongation factor and checked (one GTP); the
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:rnas}{ribosomal RNA} of the large subunit transfers the growing chain from
the P-site tRNA onto the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid} of the A-site tRNA, forming the
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{peptide bond}; the \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosome} moves one \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} along (a second GTP),
shifting the tRNAs to P and E, and the empty one \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}. At a stop
\omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} a \emph{release factor} enters the A site and the chain is
hydrolysed free. Bacterial \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes} add fifteen to twenty residues a
second, eukaryotic ones two to five; several \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes} read one
message at once, forming a \emph{polysome}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Reckoning a protein's synthesis]\label{met:b1:gene-expression:reckon}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Length: a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} of $n$ residues needs a coding sequence of
        $3n + 3$ \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} (the stop \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} included), inside a
        messenger longer by its untranslated ends and, in the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene},
        by its \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{introns}.
  \item Time: divide $n$ by the elongation rate (\qtyrange{15}{20}{}
        per second in bacteria, \qtyrange{2}{5}{} in eukaryotes); the
        message is being read by one \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosome} every \qtyrange{80}{100}{}
        \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides}, so the output per message is one chain every
        (spacing / rate) seconds.
  \item Energy: four high-energy phosphate bonds per residue (two to
        charge the tRNA, two GTP on the \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosome}), plus the
        transcription of the message at two per \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide}, shared
        among all the chains that message yields.
  \item Fidelity: about one wrong \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid} in $10^4$ \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codons}; a
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} of \qty{500}{} residues is wrong somewhere in one copy
        out of twenty --- tolerable because \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} are replaceable
        and errors are not inherited.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{After translation}

\begin{proposition}[What happens to a new polypeptide]\label{prop:b1:gene-expression:after}
\index{signal peptide}\index{post-translational modification}
The chain folds as it emerges, helped by \omterm{prop:b1:proteins:denaturation}{chaperones}
(\cref{ch:b1:proteins}). Its destination is written in its sequence:
a \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:gene-expression:after}{signal peptide}} at the N-terminus binds a \emph{signal
recognition particle} that halts translation, docks the \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosome} on
the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:endomembrane}{endoplasmic reticulum}, and threads the chain into its lumen
(\cref{ch:b1:eukaryotic-cell}); other sequences direct \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} into
\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:mitochondrion}{mitochondria}, \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plastid}{chloroplasts}, the nucleus or \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:peroxisome}{peroxisomes} after
synthesis; a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} with no signal stays in the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol}. Many
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} are \emph{modified}: the initial methionine removed, sugars
attached in the ER and Golgi, phosphates added and removed by kinases
and phosphatases, \omterm{def:b1:lipids:fattyacid}{lipids} attached, pieces cut out (insulin is made as
one chain and cut into two; zymogens are cut to activate them). And
every \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} is eventually \emph{degraded}: tagged with the small
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} \emph{ubiquitin} and unfolded and digested by the
\emph{proteasome}, after a life of minutes (regulatory \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins}) to
months (haemoglobin) --- so that the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} present are those the
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} is currently making.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The numbers of a cell]\label{ex:b1:gene-expression:numbers}
A growing \emph{E. coli} holds some \num{20000} \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes}, each
adding twenty residues a second: $\num{4e5}$ residues a second, a
million \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} of \qty{300}{} residues in an hour --- its own
content, which is what doubling every hour requires. Half its
energy goes to \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} synthesis. A liver \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} holds ten million
\omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes} and makes \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} for weeks of use rather than for
division; a plasma \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, secreting antibody, devotes nearly all of
them to one \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} and pours out two thousand molecules a second.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: From a Gene to a Protein}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a thirty-kilobase gene followed
into a five-hundred-residue protein: transcription timed, splicing
weighed, ribosomes counted, energy and errors reckoned, ending on the
cost of one protein in ATP}]
\label{pb:b1:gene-expression:1}
A human \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} spans \qty{30}{kb} with eight \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{exons} totalling
\qty{2000}{} \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} of mature messenger, of which \qty{1503}{}
code (500 residues plus the stop). Polymerase II transcribes at
\qty{30}{} \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} per second; \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes} elongate at \qty{4}{}
residues per second and space themselves one per \qty{80}{}
\omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides}; the messenger's half-life is \qty{2}{h}. Costs: two
high-energy bonds per \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide} transcribed, four per residue
translated; take one high-energy bond as one \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP}. Error rates:
$10^{-5}$ per \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide} transcribed, $10^{-4}$ per \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:code}{codon} translated.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Transcription and splicing.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How long does one polymerase take to transcribe the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene}?
  \item What fraction of the primary transcript is removed by
        splicing?
  \item How many \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides} are transcribed for every \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide} of
        mature messenger?
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} spent transcribing one primary transcript.
  \item If a polymerase starts every \qty{10}{s}, how many
        polymerases are on the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} at once, and how many messengers
        does the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} produce per hour?
  \item Compute the number of \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{introns} and their mean length.
  \item The spliceosome removes each \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{intron} in about a minute, in
        parallel. Does splicing or transcription set the time from
        \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} to messenger?
  \item Compute the physical length of the \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} and of the mature
        messenger (\qty{0.34}{nm} per \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide}).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Translation.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How long does one \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosome} take to translate the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}?
  \item How many \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes} read one messenger at once?
  \item How many \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} molecules does one messenger yield per
        hour?
  \item Over its \qty{2}{h} half-life, how many does it yield in all
        (a message with half-life $t_{1/2}$ yields, on average,
        $t_{1/2}/\ln 2$ hours of full production)?
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} spent translating one \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}.
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} spent on transcription per \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}, sharing
        the transcript's cost among the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} of question 11.
  \item Compute the total cost of one \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} and the fraction due to
        translation.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Errors.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the probability that a given messenger carries at
        least one transcription error in its \qty{1503}{} coding
        \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotides}.
  \item Compute the probability that a given \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} molecule carries
        at least one translation error.
  \item About a quarter of \omterm{def:b1:nucleic-acids:nucleotide}{nucleotide} changes are silent and a third
        of amino-acid substitutions are harmless. What fraction of
        the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} molecules made are defective?
  \item A defective messenger yields defective \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} for two
        hours; a defective \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} is one molecule. Explain why the
        \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} can afford a translation error rate ten times the
        transcription rate, and both far above the replication rate.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s budget.}
The \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} makes \num{3000} copies of this \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} per hour, and in
all \num{3e9} residues of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} per day.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many messengers of this \omterm{def:b1:genomes:gene}{gene} must be present at once
        (each yielding the number of question 10)?
  \item How many \omterm{def:b1:gene-expression:ribosome}{ribosomes} are occupied by this \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}?
  \item Compute the daily \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} spends on all its \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}
        synthesis (four per residue) and, at \qty{50}{kJ/mol}, the
        power in watts for a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} of \qty{2000}{\micro m^3}.
  \item Compare with the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s total power if it consumes
        \qty{1e9}{} \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} per second.
  \item A drug blocks the spliceosome. Predict its effect on this
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} and on a bacterial \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}.
  \item State the result: the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} cost of one molecule of the
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}, split between translation and transcription, and the
        time from the start of transcription to the first finished
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
