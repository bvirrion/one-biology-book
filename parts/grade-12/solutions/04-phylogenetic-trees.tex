\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g12:phylogenetic-trees} --- Reading Kinship: Phylogenetic Trees}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:1}
Tips: the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} compared. Nodes: their hypothetical most recent
common ancestors. Root: the common ancestor of all of them.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:2}
The lizard's closest relatives are mouse and human together (they
share the node just below the lizard's branch). The frog's closest
relative is the whole group of the other three, equally.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:3}
A state that appeared in an ancestor and was inherited by its
descendants; sharing it means sharing that ancestor. The ancestral
state was possessed by the ancestor of the whole group, so every
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} could have kept it: sharing it shows nothing about closer
kinship.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:4}
An ancestor and all its descendants. Birds are a \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}. "Reptiles" in
the everyday sense is not: it leaves out the birds, which descend from
within it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:5}
The chimpanzee; the oldest node is the one joining the macaque to the
apes, 25 million years ago.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:6}
Rotate at each node: root splits into frog (bottom) and the rest;
then lizard, then mouse and human at the top. Mouse and human still
share the most recent node, the lizard joins them next, the frog
last.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:7}
The pigeon has jaws, limbs and amnion: it enters the amniote \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}
beside the lizard and the mouse. Feathers, present in one \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species},
define no group here; they would define the birds if more birds were
added.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:8}
Hair, milk and the jaw place the dolphin among the mammals; putting it
with the shark would require all the mammalian characters to have
arisen twice. The fins and streamlined body arose separately in the
two lineages: a convergence.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:9}
$2.2/0.27 \approx 8$ million years.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:10}
Both are present tips; neither descends from the other. Correct:
humans and chimpanzees are sister \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, descended from a common
ancestor, now extinct, that lived about six million years ago and was
neither a human nor a chimpanzee.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:11}
The lamprey is known to lie outside the group of jawed \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates},
so its states are ancestral for them. With the mouse as outgroup,
hair, amnion, limbs and jaws would all be read as ancestral and their
absence as derived: the tree would be built upside down.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:12}
The ancestral \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} carried several \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of each \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}; when
it split twice in quick succession, different \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} were sorted
into the three descendants at different \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, so one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}'s history
groups \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} 1 with 2 and another's groups 2 with 3. Each \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} tree
is right for that \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}; the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} tree is the majority verdict of
many \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:13}
At 1\% per year, viral samples taken months apart already differ
measurably: the virus tree resolves who infected whom within an
epidemic, but over millions of years the sequence would have been
overwritten many times. At 0.3\% per million years, a mammalian \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}
changes too slowly to see anything within an epidemic but keeps a
readable record over 200 million years.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:14}
A fossil is a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} of its own, with its own derived states, not
necessarily the ancestral \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}; it sits on a side branch near
the node where feathers appeared. It shows that the node --- the
common ancestor of birds and their nearest reptilian relatives --- is
at least 150 million years old and that feathers preceded the loss of
teeth and tail.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:phylogenetic-trees:15}
A tree has no top: rotating any node moves humans to the bottom
without changing a kinship. Every present tip has evolved for the same
time since the root, so none is more advanced. And humans are one tip
among millions, on one branch of the apes; the tree's shape records
descent, not rank.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g12:phylogenetic-trees:1}
\textbf{1.} Bony skeleton (five \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}): the bony \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates}; the
shark is outside.

\textbf{2.} Four limbs: tetrapods (salamander, crocodile, pigeon,
cat). Amniotic egg: amniotes (crocodile, pigeon, cat). Nested:
bony \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates} $\supset$ tetrapods $\supset$ amniotes.

\textbf{3.} No: a state in a single \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} groups nothing. They
would be useful with more \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} sharing them (other birds, other
mammals, other reptiles).

\textbf{4.} Root: shark versus the rest (bony skeleton); then salmon
versus tetrapods (four limbs); then salamander versus amniotes
(amniotic egg); among the amniotes, an unresolved three-way split, with
feathers on the pigeon's branch and hair and the skull opening on the
cat's.

\textbf{5.} The table places crocodile, pigeon and cat together but
cannot order them: the crocodile's closest relative is undecided.

\textbf{6.} Crocodile--pigeon, 8\%.

\textbf{7.} The cat (15\% from each); then the salamander (19--20\%);
then the salmon (24--25\%); then the shark (30--31\%).

\textbf{8.} They agree on every nesting; the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} resolves the
amniotes: crocodile and pigeon are sisters, the cat joins them next.

\textbf{9.} The shark's lineage separated before all the others
diverged from one another; each of them has been apart from the shark
for the same time and shows the same distance.

\textbf{10.} The crocodile is closer kin to the pigeon than to the
cat --- and, by the same logic, than to a lizard; "reptiles" without
the birds is not a \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}.

\textbf{11.} 19\% over $2 \times 340$ million years of branch: about
0.028\% per million years per lineage, i.e. 0.056\% per million years
of separation.

\textbf{12.} Crocodile--pigeon $8/0.056 \approx 140$ million years;
amniote node $15/0.056 \approx 270$ million years.

\textbf{13.} Salmon $24.5/0.056 \approx 440$; shark $30.5/0.056
\approx 540$ million years.

\textbf{14.} Fossils: 250 and 310; the clock gives 140 and 270. The
order is right and the older date close, but the crocodile--bird date
is far too young: a single \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}'s rate varies between lineages, and
one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} gives a rough clock at best.

\textbf{15.} At large distances many positions have changed more than
once, so differences no longer add up: the clock saturates. Old nodes
are underestimated by this \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}; a slower \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, or many \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, are
needed.

\textbf{16.} (cat, (crocodile, pigeon)) is the smallest \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}
containing the cat: the amniotes; then the tetrapods; then the bony
\omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates}; then all six with the shark.

\textbf{17.} "Fish" is not a \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}: the node joining shark and salmon
is the root, whose descendants include everything. Amniotes is a
\omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}: crocodile, pigeon, cat and their common ancestor.

\textbf{18.} The salamander is a present \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, not an intermediate;
it is the sister group of the amniotes, sharing with them the tetrapod
ancestor and lacking the amniotic egg that arose later in their line.

\textbf{19.} Beside the branch between the tetrapod node and the
amniote node: a tetrapod that is not an amniote. Its date confirms
that four limbs existed by 360 million years ago and the amniotic egg
appeared later.

\textbf{20.} (shark, (salmon, (salamander, (cat, (crocodile,
pigeon))))); the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} settled that crocodile and pigeon are sisters;
their split dates, by this \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, to about 140 million years (fossils
say 250).
\end{solution}
