\chapter{Global Change and the Biosphere}\label{ch:b2:global-change}

Between 1851 and 1858 Henry Thoreau walked the \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:wood}{woods} and meadows
around Concord, Massachusetts, and wrote down the day each of some
five hundred plants first flowered. A century and a half later,
botanists went back to the same places with his notebooks. The
highbush blueberry that Thoreau saw open on May 11 now opens on
April 12; the whole flora flowers, on average, ten days earlier
than in his time, in step with a spring that is \qty{2.4}{\celsius}
warmer; and a quarter of his species are gone from Concord --- not
at random, but those whose flowering could not track the
temperature. The previous chapters gave the machinery: the cycles
of \cref{ch:b2:biogeochemical-cycles}, the selection and drift of
\cref{ch:b2:population-genetics}, the competition and speciation
of \cref{ch:b2:speciation}, the \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soils} of \cref{ch:b2:living-soil}.
This chapter puts a number on what is happening to living things
as one species changes the chemistry of the air and the sea: how
much warming per tonne of carbon, how many days of spring per
degree, how many metres uphill, how much acid in the sea, how many
species per hectare lost --- and what the numbers say about the
century ahead.

\section{The physical change}

\begin{proposition}[Warming is proportional to cumulative emissions]\label{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}
\index{global warming}\index{cumulative emissions}\index{carbon budget}\index{greenhouse effect}
Carbon dioxide absorbs the infrared the ground emits and returns a
part of it; more \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} means a warmer surface, by about
\qty{3}{\celsius} for each doubling once the oceans have caught up
(the \emph{climate sensitivity}). Because the temperature response
to \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} is logarithmic in concentration while the \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne
fraction} of emissions falls as the concentration rises, the two
curvatures nearly cancel, and the global mean warming is, to a
good approximation, \emph{linear in the cumulative amount of
carbon emitted}, whatever the timing:
\[
\Delta T \approx \lambda\,C_{\text{cum}}, \qquad \lambda \approx
\qty{1.65}{\celsius} \text{ per } \qty{1000}{GtC}
\]
(\qty{0.45}{\celsius} per \qty{1000}{Gt} of \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}). Some
\qty{700}{GtC} emitted since 1850 have warmed the planet by
\qty{1.2}{\celsius} --- and the relation gives every target a
budget: \qty{1.5}{\celsius} allows about \qty{900}{GtC} in all,
\qty{200}{GtC} more than has been emitted, twenty years at the
present rate. The warming is unequal: twice the average over land
and three times in the Arctic; nights more than days; and it comes
with the rest --- more intense rain and longer droughts, a sea
level rising \qty{4}{mm} a year, ice that is a fifth smaller in
summer, and an ocean that has absorbed \qty{90}{\%} of the heat and
a quarter of the carbon.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The proportionality was found in climate models of every
complexity and confirmed by the observed record: plotting the
warming since 1850 against \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{cumulative emissions} gives a straight
line through the decades. The \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{greenhouse effect} itself is measured
from orbit: satellites see the infrared the Earth emits with the
absorption bands of \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}, methane and water vapour cut out of
it, and the cut has deepened between 1970 and 2020 by exactly the
amount the rise in these gases predicts. The isotopes and the
falling oxygen of \cref{ch:b2:biogeochemical-cycles} tie the extra
\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} to fossil fuel.
\end{proof}

\section{The calendar: phenology}

\begin{theorem}[Degree-days and the advance of spring]\label{thm:b2:global-change:phenology}
\index{phenology}\index{degree-days}\index{thermal time}\index{phenological mismatch}
Many events in the life of plants and ectotherms --- budburst,
flowering, egg hatching, emergence --- occur when the accumulated
\emph{\omterm{thm:b2:global-change:phenology}{thermal time}} since a start date, the sum of daily
temperatures above a base $T_b$ (\emph{degree-days}), reaches a
fixed total $K$. If spring temperature rises linearly, $T(t) = T_b
+ b\,(t - t_0)$ at $b$ degrees per day from the day $t_0$ it crosses
the base, the event occurs at
\[
t^{*} = t_0 + \sqrt{2K/b},
\]
and a uniform warming of $\Delta T$ brings the crossing day forward
by $\Delta T/b$ and the event with it: with $b \approx
\qty{0.1}{\celsius}$ per day, \emph{about ten days earlier per
degree}. Species differ in $T_b$, $K$ and in whether they also use
day length, which warming does not change --- so the members of a
food chain advance at different rates and their timing comes
apart: a \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:global-change:phenology}{phenological mismatch}} between the caterpillar and
the bird that must feed its chicks on it.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Degree-days from $t_0$: $\int_{t_0}^{t}(T - T_b)\,\mathrm{d}t' =
b\,(t - t_0)^{2}/2$, equal to $K$ when $t - t_0 = \sqrt{2K/b}$.
Warming by $\Delta T$ replaces $T$ by $T + \Delta T$: the base is
crossed $\Delta T/b$ days earlier and the accumulation, identical
from that day on, completes $\Delta T/b$ days earlier. (If the
warming also steepens $b$, the interval $\sqrt{2K/b}$ shortens
too.) A species cued by day length keeps its date; one cued by
degree-days moves; the gap between them grows by $\Delta T/b$.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Thoreau's Concord flora, and the Marsham family's record of
twenty-seven signs of spring in Norfolk kept from 1736 to 1947,
both show flowering and leafing advancing by \qtyrange{3}{6}{days}
per degree of spring warming. The Kyoto cherry blossom, dated in
court diaries since the ninth century, held around mid-April for a
thousand years and has moved to early April since 1900, the
earliest dates on record falling in the 2020s. In the Netherlands
the oak's caterpillars now peak two weeks earlier than in 1985; the
great tit, cued by the same warmth, has kept pace, but the pied
flycatcher, which times its migration from Africa by day length,
arrives as before and finds the peak gone --- its populations have
fallen by \qty{90}{\%} where the mismatch is largest (Both and
colleagues, 2006).
\end{proof}

\section{The map: range shifts}

\begin{proposition}[Isotherms move, and species follow]\label{prop:b2:global-change:range}
\index{range shift}\index{climate velocity}\index{lapse rate}
Temperature falls by about \qty{6.5}{\celsius} per kilometre of
altitude and by about \qty{0.65}{\celsius} per hundred kilometres of
latitude in the temperate zone; a warming of \qty{1}{\celsius}
therefore moves every isotherm about \qty{150}{m} uphill and
\qty{150}{km} poleward. Species whose limits are set by temperature
move with their isotherm if they can: the average of hundreds of
studied species has shifted \qty{11}{m} uphill and \qty{17}{km}
poleward per decade since 1970, at the speed of the isotherms, and
the fastest --- mobile, generalist, short-lived --- outrun them,
while trees, whose \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} travel metres a year, and species already
at the summit or the pole, cannot follow: a mountain's top has
nowhere to go, and each degree of warming removes the highest
\qty{150}{m} of every range. Marine species, with no barriers and
sharper thermal limits, move fastest, tens of kilometres a decade;
the cod, the mackerel and the fisheries with them have moved north
across whole seas. The upper edge of a range advances, the lower
edge retreats as it warms and as new competitors arrive from
below, and the community at any point is reassembled from species
that have never met.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Parmesan (1996) resurveyed the Edith's checkerspot butterfly
across its western North American range: populations at the
southern edge and low altitudes were extinct, those at the northern
edge and high altitudes thriving, and the whole range had moved
\qty{92}{km} north and \qty{124}{m} up --- the first species shown
to have tracked the isotherms. Lenoir and colleagues (2008)
compared forest plant surveys of 1905--1985 with 1986--2005 across
the French mountains: the optimum altitude of 171 species had
risen by \qty{29}{m} a decade. Chen and colleagues (2011), across
two thousand species, found the shifts two to three times faster
than earlier estimates, and proportional to the local warming.
\end{proof}

\section{The sea: acidification and heat}

\begin{theorem}[Acidification]\label{thm:b2:global-change:acid}
\index{ocean acidification}\index{carbonate system}\index{saturation state}\index{coral bleaching}
Carbon dioxide dissolving in seawater makes carbonic acid, which
dissociates: $\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} + \ensuremath{\mathrm{H_2O}} \rightleftharpoons \ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}} +
\ensuremath{\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}}$, with $[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}][\ensuremath{\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}}]/[\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}] = K_1$.
Bicarbonate is the ocean's largest carbon pool and is held nearly
constant by the alkalinity, so
\[
[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}] \propto p_{\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}}, \qquad
\Delta\mathrm{pH} \approx -\log_{10}\frac{p_{\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}}}{p_0}
\]
--- more precisely $-0.9\log_{10}$ of the ratio, because bicarbonate
rises a little. From 280 to \qty{420}{ppm} the surface pH has fallen
by $0.15$, from $8.2$ to $8.05$: a \qty{40}{\%} increase in hydrogen
ions; at \qty{800}{ppm} it would fall by $0.4$. The extra hydrogen
ions consume carbonate, $\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}} + \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}} \to \ensuremath{\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}}$, and
the \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:global-change:acid}{saturation state}} $\Omega$ of calcium carbonate ---
the product of calcium and carbonate concentrations over the
solubility product --- falls in proportion: shells and skeletons
of aragonite (corals, pteropods) form readily at $\Omega > 3$, with
difficulty near 1, and dissolve below it; the tropical surface,
at $\Omega \approx 4$ before industry and 3 now, reaches 2 at
\qty{560}{ppm}, and the cold, \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}-rich polar surface waters
reach 1 first.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
From the equilibrium, $[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}] = K_1[\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}]/[\ensuremath{\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}}]$ and
$[\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}]$ is proportional to $p_{\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}}$ by Henry's law;
with $[\ensuremath{\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}}]$ constant, $[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}]$ is proportional to
$p_{\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}}$ and $\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}]$ changes by
$-\log_{10}$ of the ratio. The second equilibrium, $[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}][\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}}]/[\ensuremath{\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}}]
= K_2$, gives $[\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}}] \propto 1/[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}] \propto
1/p_{\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}}$, so $\Omega$ falls as $p_{\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}}$ rises, and
the \qty{40}{\%} rise in $[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}]$ is a \qty{30}{\%} fall in
carbonate. (The full chemistry, with the change in bicarbonate and
the ion activities, gives the factor $0.9$ and the observed
numbers; the Year 3 volume treats it.)
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The ocean station north of Hawaii has measured surface pH monthly
since 1988: it falls by $0.0018$ a year, in step with the
\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} at Mauna Loa above it, as the chemistry requires.
Pteropods --- the sea's snails, food for salmon and whales ---
collected off the Pacific coast show their aragonite shells
pitted and dissolving where upwelling brings water with $\Omega <
1$ to the surface. \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:acid}{Coral bleaching} is heat, not acid: when the
water stays \qty{1}{\celsius} above the summer maximum for a few
weeks, the coral expels its symbiotic algae and starves; the Great
Barrier Reef bleached in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022 and 2024,
and half its coral cover is gone since 1995.
\end{proof}

\section{The ledger: extinction and its estimate}

\begin{theorem}[The species--area relation and the cost of habitat loss]\label{thm:b2:global-change:sar}
\index{species--area relation}\index{extinction rate}\index{habitat loss}\index{extinction debt}
The number of species found in an area $A$ grows as a power of the
area, $S = cA^{z}$, with $z \approx 0.25$ for islands and habitat
fragments (doubling the area adds a fifth more species; a
hundredfold more area, three times as many). If a habitat is
reduced to a fraction $1 - h$ of its extent, the number of species
it can eventually hold falls to $(1 - h)^{z}$ of the original: a
loss of half the habitat costs \qty{16}{\%} of its species,
\qty{90}{\%} costs \qty{44}{\%}, and \qty{99}{\%} costs
\qty{68}{\%}. The loss is not immediate --- the survivors in the
fragment persist for a while, an \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:global-change:sar}{extinction debt}} paid over
decades as small populations succumb to the drift and inbreeding of
\cref{ch:b2:population-genetics} --- and it is the basis of the
estimate that the current \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:sar}{extinction rate} is a hundred to a
thousand times the background rate of the fossil record: about
one species in a million per year, against the several thousand a
year, out of some ten million, now being lost.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$S'/S = (A'/A)^{z} = (1 - h)^{z}$; with $z = 0.25$, $0.5^{0.25} =
0.84$, $0.1^{0.25} = 0.56$, $0.01^{0.25} = 0.32$. The relation
itself is empirical; its exponent reflects the balance, on islands,
between the immigration of new species (falling as the island
fills) and the extinction of resident ones (rising as their
populations shrink with area), the equilibrium of MacArthur and
Wilson's theory.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The islands of the Krakatau group, sterilised by the eruption of
1883, were recolonised by plants, birds and insects in a
succession that reached, within fifty years, the species numbers
predicted from their areas; the mangrove islets Simberloff and
Wilson fumigated in Florida in 1966 returned to their original
species counts, with different species, within a year. The Atlantic
forest of Brazil, reduced to \qty{10}{\%} of its extent, has lost
or nearly lost the fraction of its bird species the relation
predicts; and in every group assessed by the World Conservation
Union, a quarter to a third of species are threatened --- birds
\qty{13}{\%}, mammals \qty{27}{\%}, amphibians \qty{41}{\%}, corals
\qty{44}{\%} --- with \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:sar}{habitat loss} the first cause, and hunting,
\omterm{prop:b2:global-change:invasion}{invasive species}, pollution and, increasingly, climate the others.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Invasions, services, and what can be done]\label{prop:b2:global-change:invasion}
\index{invasive species}\index{ecosystem services}\index{mitigation}\index{net zero}
Ships, planes and trade have moved thousands of species past the
barriers that kept them apart; most fail to establish, but the
few that do --- the rabbit in Australia, the zebra mussel in the
Great Lakes, the brown tree snake that emptied Guam of its birds,
the chytrid fungus that has driven ninety amphibian species to
extinction --- spread as a front advancing at constant speed, $c =
2\sqrt{rD}$ for a population growing at rate $r$ and dispersing
with diffusion coefficient $D$ (Skellam's muskrat, released near
Prague in 1905, expanded across Europe at \qty{11}{km} a year,
its range radius a straight line in time). Against all this stands
what the biosphere does for its one troublesome species:
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:pollination}{pollination} of three quarters of crops, water purification, flood
control, the fixing of nitrogen and the making of \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil}, fisheries,
the carbon held in forests and \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soils} --- \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:global-change:invasion}{ecosystem services}}
valued, where a price can be put on them, at more than the world's
economic product. What can be done follows from the numbers.
Warming stops when net emissions do, and not before: the \omterm{thm:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:box}{box
models} of \cref{ch:b2:biogeochemical-cycles} allow no other
reading of \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:global-change:invasion}{net zero}}. Land can help within limits --- a
gigatonne of carbon a year from reforestation and \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soils} for a few
decades, against ten from fossil fuels --- and species can be
helped to move, or kept in reserves large and connected enough
that the \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:sar}{species--area relation} and the drift of small populations
do not finish them. The tools are those of this book: the
\omterm{def:b2:population-genetics:frequencies}{population genetics} of the reserve, the competition of the
invader, the phenology of the crop, the budget of the \omterm{def:b2:living-soil:soil}{soil}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Three futures]\label{ex:b2:global-change:scenarios}
Emissions held at \qty{10}{GtC} a year to 2100 add \qty{800}{GtC}
to the \qty{700}{} already emitted: $1.65\times 1.5 =
\qty{2.5}{\celsius}$ by the century's end and still rising. Emissions
falling to zero by 2070 add about \qty{250}{GtC}: \qty{1.6}{\celsius},
then level. Doubling emissions first, as the century's early
decades did, gives \qty{4}{\celsius} or more. The ranges of the
temperate zone move \qty{400}{km} in the first case and
\qty{60}{km} beyond today's in the second; spring comes twenty-five
days earlier, or six; surface pH reaches $7.8$, or stays near
$8.0$; and the extinction estimates, which depend on habitat and
warming together, range from a tenth to a third of species. The
difference between the futures is the cumulative area under one
curve, which is the sum of decisions not yet made.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:1}
State the relation between warming and \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{cumulative emissions}, and
compute the warming for \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{cumulative emissions} of 700, 1000 and
\qty{2000}{GtC}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:2}
Define degree-days and \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:phenology}{phenological mismatch}, with the flycatcher
as an example.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:3}
How far do isotherms move uphill and poleward per degree of
warming, and why can a mountain-top species not follow?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:4}
Explain in three sentences why adding \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} to the sea lowers
its pH and its carbonate, and which organisms are hurt.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:5}
Compute the \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{carbon budget} remaining for \qty{1.5}{\celsius} and for
\qty{2}{\celsius} with $\lambda = \qty{1.65}{\celsius}$ per
\qty{1000}{GtC} and \qty{700}{GtC} already emitted; convert both to
years at \qty{10}{GtC/yr}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:6}
Spring warms at $b = \qty{0.12}{\celsius}$ per day from a base of
\qty{5}{\celsius}; a moth needs $K = 150$ degree-days. Compute the
days from base-crossing to emergence, and the advance for a
warming of \qty{1.5}{\celsius}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:7}
A plant's range spans \qtyrange{800}{1400}{m} on a mountain
\qty{1800}{m} high whose area halves every \qty{300}{m} of altitude.
After \qty{3}{\celsius} of warming, where is the band and how much
area has it lost? After \qty{5}{\celsius}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:8}
Compute the surface pH and the rise in hydrogen-ion concentration
at 560, 800 and \qty{1000}{ppm}, from $\mathrm{pH} = 8.2 -
0.9\log_{10}(p/280)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:9}
With $z = 0.25$, what fraction of species is eventually lost when
\qty{70}{\%}, \qty{95}{\%} and \qty{99.9}{\%} of a habitat is
destroyed? Why is the immediate loss smaller?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:10}
An invader grows at $r = 0.7$ per year and disperses with $D =
\qty{20}{km^2/yr}$. Compute its front speed and the time to cross a
country \qty{1000}{km} wide. What halves the speed: halving $r$ or
halving $D$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:11}
The background \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:sar}{extinction rate} is 1 per million species-years and
there are 8 million species. Compute the expected extinctions a
year and per century at background, and at 1000 times background.
Compare with the ten thousand documented extinctions of the last
century and comment on the discrepancy.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:global-change:12}
``Warming stops when net emissions stop.'' Justify from the \omterm{thm:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:box}{box
model} of the atmosphere and the proportionality of warming to
\omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{cumulative emissions}, and say what is missing from the argument.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Biosphere in 2100}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- two emission paths carried
to 2100: the warming of each, the spring it makes, the distance
its isotherms travel, the acid it puts in the sea and the species
it costs a forest, ending on the two warmings, the two pH values
and the fraction of species lost}]
\label{pb:b2:global-change:1}
Data. $\lambda = \qty{1.65}{\celsius}$ per \qty{1000}{GtC};
\qty{700}{GtC} emitted by 2020 (warming \qty{1.2}{\celsius}). Path
A: \qty{10}{GtC/yr} constant to 2100. Path B: falling linearly to
zero in 2070. Spring warms at $b = \qty{0.1}{\celsius}$ per day
above a base of \qty{5}{\celsius}; a tree needs $K = 200$
degree-days to leaf, and a migratory bird arrives on a fixed date,
20 days after the tree's leafing in 2020. \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:range}{Lapse rate}
\qty{6.5}{\celsius/km}; latitudinal gradient \qty{0.65}{\celsius}
per \qty{100}{km}. Ocean: $\mathrm{pH} = 8.2 - 0.9\log_{10}(p/280)$;
\ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} in 2100: \qty{600}{ppm} on path A, \qty{420}{ppm} on path B.
A forest region of \qty{e5}{km^2} with 400 tree species, $z = 0.25$,
loses \qty{60}{\%} of its area to clearing by 2100 on path A and
\qty{20}{\%} on path B.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Warming.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{cumulative emissions} in 2100 on each path.
  \item Compute the warming in 2100 on each path.
  \item Compute the warming in 2050 on each.
  \item What is the warming rate per decade on path A? Compare with
        the observed \qty{0.2}{\celsius} per decade.
  \item On path B, does the warming stop in 2070? Why, according
        to the proportionality?
  \item What cumulative emission corresponds to \qty{2}{\celsius},
        and in what year does path A cross it?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Spring.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the days from base-crossing to leafing in 2020.
  \item Compute the advance of leafing, relative to 2020, in 2100
        on each path (warming relative to 2020).
  \item The bird's arrival date does not change. Compute the gap
        between leafing and arrival on each path in 2100.
  \item The caterpillars the bird needs peak 15 days after leafing
        and last 10 days. On which path does the bird still find
        them?
  \item Suppose instead the bird advances by \qty{3}{d} per
        degree. Recompute the gap on path A.
  \item State in one sentence why the mismatch, not the warming
        itself, is what threatens the bird.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Ranges.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the altitudinal and latitudinal displacement of
        isotherms on each path, relative to 2020.
  \item A tree species disperses \qty{100}{m} a year. Can it track
        the isotherms poleward on path A? On path B?
  \item A species occupies \qtyrange{1500}{1800}{m} on a
        \qty{2000}{m} mountain. On path A, where does its band sit
        in 2100, and what happens?
  \item The mountain's area halves every \qty{300}{m}. Compute the
        area factor lost by that species on path B.
  \item A marine fish tracks isotherms at \qty{30}{km} a decade.
        Is that enough on path A?
  \item Explain why the lower edge of a range retreats even where
        the species could tolerate the heat.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Sea and forest.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the surface pH in 2100 on each path, and the rise
        in hydrogen-ion concentration since 1750.
  \item The \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:acid}{saturation state} $\Omega$ of the tropical surface was
        4 in 1750 and falls as $[\ensuremath{\mathrm{H^{+}}}]^{-1}$. Compute it in 2100
        on each path and say what it means for coral.
  \item Compute the number of tree species the forest region can
        eventually hold on each path, and the number lost.
  \item Warming adds losses: suppose each degree above 2020 removes
        a further \qty{5}{\%} of the surviving species. Recompute
        the species remaining on each path.
  \item Give the fraction of the 400 species lost on each path.
  \item Which of the two causes, clearing or warming, dominates on
        each path?
  \item State the result: the warming, the pH and the fraction of
        tree species lost in 2100 on each path.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
