\chapter{Genomics and Sequencing}\label{ch:b3:genomics}

The first complete genome of a free-living organism, the \qty{1.8}{Mb}
of a bacterium, was published in 1995 after a year's work by a team of
forty. The human genome, \num{3.2} billion base pairs, took an
international consortium thirteen years and about three billion
dollars and was declared finished in 2003. Today a bench-top machine
\omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} a human genome overnight for a few hundred dollars, a hospital
sequences the tumour of a patient to choose a drug, and a museum
extracts and \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} the genome of a forty-thousand-year-old bone. The
technology that made this possible, the mathematics that turns
millions of short \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} into a genome, and what the finished genomes
have taught about the size, content and history of our DNA are the
subject of this chapter. The chapter after it takes up the algorithms
that compare the sequences once they are in hand.

\section{Reading DNA}

\begin{definition}[Sequencing by chain termination]\label{def:b3:genomics:sanger}
\index{Sanger sequencing}\index{dideoxynucleotide}\index{chain termination}
\emph{Sanger sequencing} copies a single-stranded template from a primer
with DNA polymerase in the presence of the four normal nucleotides and
a small proportion of \emph{dideoxynucleotides}, which lack the 3$'$
hydroxyl and so terminate the chain wherever they are incorporated.
Each dideoxynucleotide carries a different fluorescent dye. The
product is a mixture of fragments, one for every position of the
template, each ending in a base of known identity; separated by size
in a capillary of gel, they pass a detector in order of length, and
the sequence of colours is the sequence of the template. A run \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}
\qtyrange{700}{900}{bp} with an error rate below $10^{-3}$; it remains
the method for verifying a construct or a single gene.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Sanger, Nicklen and Coulson published the method in 1977 and used it
that year to \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} the \qty{5386}{bp} of the phage $\phi$X174 --- the
first complete DNA genome --- and in 1981 the \qty{16569}{bp} of the
human mitochondrion. Fleischmann and colleagues (1995) \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} the
\qty{1.83}{Mb} of \emph{Haemophilus influenzae} by breaking the whole
genome into random fragments, sequencing \num{24000} of them and
assembling the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} by computer --- the \emph{whole-genome shotgun}
strategy that every later project scaled up.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Massively parallel sequencing]\label{def:b3:genomics:ngs}
\index{sequencing by synthesis}\index{read}\index{long-read sequencing}\index{nanopore}
Second-generation instruments read millions to billions of fragments
at once. In \emph{sequencing by synthesis} the fragments, with
adapters ligated to their ends, are bound to a glass flow cell and
amplified in place into clusters of identical molecules; the clusters
are then extended one base per cycle with fluorescent, reversibly
blocked nucleotides, imaged, unblocked, and extended again, so that
each cycle adds one base to every cluster's \emph{read}. Reads are
\qtyrange{100}{300}{bp}, usually from both ends of a fragment
(\emph{paired ends}), with an error rate of about $10^{-3}$ per base,
and one run yields up to $10^{12}$ bases. Third-generation
\emph{long-read} instruments read single molecules without
amplification: by watching one polymerase incorporate fluorescent
nucleotides in real time, or by threading the DNA through a protein
\emph{nanopore} and recording the ionic current, which each sequence of
bases modulates in its own way. Reads of \qtyrange{10}{100}{kb} and
more span the repeats that short reads cannot, at a higher raw error
rate that consensus reduces.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Coverage and gaps in a shotgun project]\label{thm:b3:genomics:lander-waterman}
\index{coverage}\index{Lander--Waterman}\index{contig}
Let $N$ \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} of length $L$ be taken at random positions along a
genome of length $G$, and let $c = NL/G$ be the \emph{coverage}, the
average number of \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} covering a base. Then a given base is covered
by a number of \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} that is Poisson with mean $c$: the fraction of
the genome left unsequenced is
\[
P(\text{uncovered}) = e^{-c},
\]
and if two \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} are recognised as overlapping only when they share at
least $T$ bases, so that $\theta = T/L$, the expected number of
\emph{contigs} (islands of overlapping \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}) is
\[
\text{contigs} = N\,e^{-c(1-\theta)},
\]
and their mean length is $L\,\bigl(e^{c(1-\theta)} - 1\bigr)/c + L\theta$,
approximately.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{Read} start points fall at random with density $N/G$ per base. A base
is covered by the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} that start in the $L$ bases before it; the
number of starts in a window of length $L$ is Poisson with mean $LN/G
= c$, and the probability of none is $e^{-c}$. A \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} is the rightmost
of its contig if no other \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} starts within the $L - T = L(1-\theta)$
bases after its own start (a \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} starting later would overlap it by
less than $T$ and not be joined); that probability is $e^{-c(1-\theta)}$,
and since each contig has exactly one rightmost \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read}, the expected
number of contigs is $N e^{-c(1-\theta)}$. The mean contig length
follows from $G$ divided by the number of contigs, corrected for the
uncovered fraction.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[How much is enough]\label{ex:b3:genomics:coverage}
At $c = 5$ the unsequenced fraction is $e^{-5} = 0.7\,\%$ --- for a
\qty{3.2}{Gb} genome, twenty million bases in some tens of thousands of
gaps. At $c = 10$ it is $4.5\times 10^{-5}$, \qty{150}{kb} in total.
Human genomes are routinely sequenced at $c = 30$ not because of
coverage gaps ($e^{-30} \approx 10^{-13}$) but because each base must
be \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} several times on each of the two chromosomes to call a
heterozygous variant with confidence against an error rate of $10^{-3}$
per \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read}. Bacterial genomes are sequenced at $c = 50$--$100$ for the
same reason and because it is cheap. The formula also shows what
coverage cannot fix: a repeat longer than a \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} is a place where the
overlap graph branches, and no amount of short \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} resolves it. That
is what long \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} are for.
\end{example}

\section{From reads to a genome}

\begin{definition}[Assembly and annotation]\label{def:b3:genomics:assembly}
\index{assembly}\index{scaffold}\index{N50}\index{annotation}\index{reference genome}
\emph{Assembly} reconstructs a genome from its \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} by overlap: in an
\emph{overlap graph} each \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} is a node joined to the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} it
overlaps; in a \emph{de Bruijn graph}, used for billions of short
\omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}, every $k$-mer (subsequence of length $k$) is a node and the
genome is a path through them. Both are broken by repeats longer than
the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read}, which give branching paths. Contigs are ordered and oriented
into \emph{scaffolds} by paired-end \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} and long-range information
(long \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}, optical maps, chromosome-contact maps), and the
scaffolds are placed on chromosomes. Assembly quality is summarised by
the \emph{N50}: the contig length such that half of the assembled
bases lie in contigs at least that long. \emph{Annotation} then finds
the genes: in bacteria, open reading frames longer than chance; in
eukaryotes, by combining sequence signals (splice sites, promoters,
codon bias), homology to known proteins, and transcripts sequenced
from RNA. The result, for a species, is a \emph{reference genome} to
which every later \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} from that species is aligned rather than
assembled anew.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[From a sample to variants]\label{met:b3:genomics:pipeline}
\index{variant calling}
For a resequencing study of an individual against a reference: (1)
extract DNA, fragment it to \qtyrange{300}{500}{bp}, ligate adapters
(the \emph{library}); (2) sequence to the coverage required (30$\times$
for a human genome, 100$\times$ for an exome, which captures the
\qty{1.5}{\%} of the genome that codes for protein); (3) align each
\omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} to the reference, tolerating mismatches; (4) at each position
count the bases in the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}: a position where about half the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}
disagree with the reference is a heterozygous \emph{variant}, where
nearly all disagree a homozygous one, where a few disagree an error;
(5) filter by depth, base quality and strand balance; (6) annotate each
variant with its effect on any gene (synonymous, missense, nonsense,
splice, frameshift) and its frequency in population databases; (7) for
a diagnosis, keep the rare variants predicted damaging in genes
consistent with the phenotype, and confirm by \omterm{def:b3:genomics:sanger}{Sanger sequencing}.
\end{method}

\section{What genomes look like}

\begin{proposition}[Genome size and gene number]\label{prop:b3:genomics:sizes}
\index{C-value paradox}\index{genome size}
\omterm{prop:b3:genomics:sizes}{Genome sizes} span a factor of $10^{5}$ among eukaryotes ---
\qty{12}{Mb} for yeast, \qty{100}{Mb} for the worm, \qty{140}{Mb} for
the fly, \qty{3.2}{Gb} for a human, \qty{16}{Gb} for an onion,
\qty{130}{Gb} for a lungfish, \qty{150}{Gb} for the lily \emph{Paris
japonica} --- while gene numbers span barely a factor of $10$:
\num{6000} in yeast, \num{20000} in the worm, \num{14000} in the fly,
about \num{20000} protein-coding genes in a human, \num{40000} in rice.
This is the \emph{C-value paradox}: \omterm{prop:b3:genomics:sizes}{genome size} does not measure
complexity, and neither does gene number. What varies is the non-coding
content --- introns, transposable elements, satellite repeats --- and
the number of proteins a genome can produce is multiplied far beyond
its gene count by alternative splicing (\cref{ch:b3:rna-regulation})
and by regulation, which is where the complexity of an organism is
mostly written. Bacterial genomes, by contrast, are compact and their
size does track gene number: about one gene per kilobase, \qty{88}{\%}
coding in \emph{E.~coli}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The human genome by content]\label{ex:b3:genomics:content}
Of the \qty{3.2}{Gb}: protein-coding exons, \qty{1.5}{\%}; introns
and untranslated regions of genes, about \qty{35}{\%}; transposable
elements and their fossils, about \qty{45}{\%} --- LINE-1 retrotransposons
\qty{17}{\%}, Alu elements \qty{10}{\%} (over a million copies of a
\qty{300}{bp} sequence), endogenous retroviruses \qty{8}{\%}; segmental
duplications \qty{5}{\%}; simple repeats and satellites, including the
centromeres, about \qty{5}{\%}; the rest unique non-coding sequence,
where the regulatory elements live. Some \numrange{100}{200} genes
still encode active LINE-1 machinery, and new insertions occur about
once in every twenty births. About \qty{8}{\%} of the genome is under
detectable purifying selection --- far more than the exons --- and
most of that is regulatory.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Comparative genomics]\label{def:b3:genomics:comparative}
\index{ortholog}\index{paralog}\index{synteny}\index{conserved non-coding element}\index{whole-genome duplication}
Genes in two species descended from one gene in their common ancestor
are \emph{orthologs}; genes within a species descended from a
duplication are \emph{paralogs}. Blocks of chromosome in which gene
order is conserved between species are \emph{syntenic}; synteny lets a
gene be located in one genome from its position in another and reveals
the rearrangements that separate two karyotypes (about \num{1000}
between human and mouse). Sequences conserved across distant species
that code for no protein --- \emph{conserved non-coding elements},
some ultraconserved to the base over \qty{200}{bp} between human and
fish --- are mostly enhancers of developmental genes. \emph{Whole-genome
duplications} have marked the history of lineages: two rounds at the
origin of vertebrates (the four Hox clusters of mammals against the one
of invertebrates), one in the ancestor of salmonids, one in the yeast
lineage, several in flowering plants; the duplicate genes are mostly
lost over tens of millions of years, and the survivors diverge in
function.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Human and chimpanzee]\label{ex:b3:genomics:chimp}
Aligned single-copy sequence differs between human and chimpanzee by
\qty{1.2}{\%} of bases --- some \num{35} million substitutions --- and
by insertions and deletions that together amount to another
\qty{3}{\%} of each genome. Two humans differ at about one base in a
thousand, some \numrange{4}{5} million sites, plus a few thousand
structural variants; two chimpanzees, whose population has been larger
for longer, at rather more. A child carries about \num{70} new
mutations absent from both parents, four fifths of them from the
father, and the number rises by about two per year of paternal age ---
the arithmetic of \cref{ch:b3:dna-repair} applied to the many divisions
of spermatogenesis.
\end{example}

\section{Genomes and traits}

\begin{definition}[Genome-wide association]\label{def:b3:genomics:gwas}
\index{genome-wide association study}\index{single-nucleotide polymorphism}\index{polygenic score}
A \emph{single-nucleotide polymorphism} (SNP) is a position at which
two bases each occur in at least \qty{1}{\%} of a population; about
ten million are common in humans. A \emph{genome-wide association
study} (GWAS) genotypes hundreds of thousands of SNPs in thousands of
people with a disease and thousands without, and asks at each SNP
whether one allele is more frequent in the cases. Because a million
tests are made, a result counts only below a significance threshold of
about $5\times 10^{-8}$ ($0.05$ divided by the million effectively
independent tests). Associated SNPs mark a region, not a causal
variant, since neighbouring alleles travel together (linkage
disequilibrium) over tens of kilobases. For most common diseases the
associated alleles each shift risk by a few percent and lie mostly in
regulatory sequence; their combined effect, summed over thousands of
SNPs as a \emph{polygenic score}, explains a fraction of the
heritability and predicts risk about as well as family history.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[What genomics has and has not delivered]\label{rem:b3:genomics:delivered}
Sequencing has been decisive where a single gene has a large effect:
several thousand Mendelian diseases have their gene, and sequencing the
exome of a child with an undiagnosed disorder yields a diagnosis in
about a third of cases. Cancer genomes reveal which drivers a tumour
carries and which drugs may work (\cref{ch:b3:cancer-biology});
pathogen genomes trace outbreaks strain by strain
(\cref{ch:b3:bacteriology}); ancient genomes have rewritten human
prehistory, showing that people outside Africa carry about \qty{2}{\%}
Neanderthal DNA. For common diseases --- diabetes, heart disease,
schizophrenia --- genomics has delivered thousands of small effects and
few mechanisms, and the promise of prediction from a genome for a
healthy person remains modest. The genome is a parts list; the
biology of how the parts interact is not \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} off it.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:1}
Explain why a \omterm{def:b3:genomics:sanger}{dideoxynucleotide} terminates a growing DNA chain, and why
a Sanger reaction must contain both normal and dideoxy forms of each
nucleotide.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:2}
A run produces $4\times 10^{8}$ paired \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} of $2\times \qty{150}{bp}$.
What coverage does that give of a \qty{3.2}{Gb} human genome? Of a
\qty{5}{Mb} bacterial genome?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:3}
Define contig, \omterm{def:b3:genomics:assembly}{scaffold} and \omterm{def:b3:genomics:assembly}{N50}. An \omterm{def:b3:genomics:assembly}{assembly} of \qty{100}{Mb} has ten
contigs of \qty{8}{Mb} and \num{2000} of \qty{10}{kb}. What is its \omterm{def:b3:genomics:assembly}{N50}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:4}
State the C-value paradox with two examples, and say what mostly
accounts for the excess DNA of large genomes.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:5}
Using \cref{thm:b3:genomics:lander-waterman}, find the coverage needed
to leave at most one base in a million unsequenced, and the expected
number of contigs for a \qty{5}{Mb} genome \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} at $c = 8$ with
\qty{150}{bp} \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} and a minimum overlap of \qty{30}{bp}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:6}
A heterozygous variant is covered by $30$ \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}. Assuming each \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read}
shows either allele with probability $1/2$, what is the probability
that fewer than $8$ \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} show the variant allele (so that it might be
mistaken for errors)? Use a normal approximation with mean $15$ and
standard deviation $\sqrt{7.5}$. Why is $30\times$ the standard?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:7}
A genome has a repeat of \qty{6}{kb} present in \num{500} copies.
Explain why an \omterm{def:b3:genomics:assembly}{assembly} from \qty{150}{bp} \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} collapses it, what the
resulting graph looks like, and what \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} length would resolve it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:8}
Two humans differ at one base in a thousand. How many differences in
their exomes (\qty{48}{Mb} of coding sequence)? If two thirds of coding
differences are synonymous or benign and the rest alter a protein, how
many protein-altering variants does one person carry relative to
another?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:9}
A GWAS tests $10^{6}$ SNPs at threshold $p < 5\times 10^{-8}$. How
many false positives are expected if no SNP is truly associated? A SNP
allele raises the risk of a disease with frequency \qty{2}{\%} to
\qty{2.3}{\%}. Explain why such an effect is undetectable in a study of
a thousand people and useless for an individual, yet may still point
to a mechanism.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:10}
Bacterial genomes are about \qty{88}{\%} coding; the human genome
\qty{1.5}{\%}. Give three hypotheses --- population genetic, structural
and regulatory --- for the difference, and for each a genomic
observation that supports or undermines it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:11}
Derive the Lander--Waterman contig count for \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} of two different
lengths mixed: $N_{1}$ short \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} of length $L_{1}$ and $N_{2}$ long
\omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} of length $L_{2}$, overlaps counted with the same minimum $T$.
(Treat each \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} as the rightmost of its contig if no \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} of either
kind starts within its own length minus $T$.) Show that a few long
\omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} reduce the contig count more than the same number of bases in
short \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:genomics:12}
The four Hox clusters of mammals are argued to descend from one
cluster by two rounds of \omterm{def:b3:genomics:comparative}{whole-genome duplication} at the base of the
vertebrates. State what pattern of \omterm{def:b3:genomics:comparative}{paralogs} across the genome, and
what pattern in the genomes of lampreys and of the invertebrate
chordates, that hypothesis predicts, and how one would distinguish it
from two independent duplications of the cluster alone.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: Two Genomes on One Machine}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a bacterium and a human sequenced on
the same run: the reads shared out, the coverage and the gaps computed,
the contigs counted, the heterozygous variants called against the
error rate, the content of the human genome weighed, and an association
study sized, ending on the contig count at low coverage, the coverage
that makes a variant call safe and the number of new mutations in a
child}]
\label{pb:b3:genomics:1}
Data: one run yields $6\times 10^{8}$ \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} of \qty{150}{bp}. A
bacterial genome is \qty{4.6}{Mb}; the human genome \qty{3.2}{Gb}
(haploid). Minimum overlap $T = \qty{30}{bp}$. Error rate $10^{-3}$
per base per \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read}. Human coding sequence \qty{48}{Mb}; the genome is
\qty{45}{\%} transposon-derived, with $1.1$ million Alu copies of
\qty{300}{bp}. Two humans differ at one site in \num{1000}. Mutation
rate $1.2\times 10^{-8}$ per base per generation.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The bacterium.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The bacterium is given \qty{0.2}{\%} of the run. How many
        \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}, and what coverage?
  \item What fraction of its genome is unsequenced? How many bases is
        that?
  \item Compute $\theta$ and the expected number of contigs.
  \item An earlier pilot used only \num{30000} \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}. Coverage,
        uncovered fraction, and contig number?
  \item The genome contains \num{7} copies of a \qty{5}{kb} rRNA
        operon. Explain what happens to them in the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:assembly}{assembly} and how
        many contig ends this alone produces.
  \item The bacterium has about one gene per kilobase. How many genes,
        and, if \qty{88}{\%} of the genome is coding, what is the mean
        gene length?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The human.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The rest of the run goes to one human genome. Coverage of the
        diploid genome per haploid copy (that is, total bases divided
        by \qty{3.2}{Gb})?
  \item At a heterozygous site each of the two alleles is covered by
        about half the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}. With the coverage of question 7, what is
        the expected number of \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} showing each allele?
  \item An error shows a wrong base in a \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read} with probability
        $10^{-3}$. At a homozygous site covered by $c$ \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads}, what is
        the expected number of \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} showing any particular wrong base
        ($10^{-3}/3$ each)? Why does a rule ``call a variant if at
        least $3$ \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} and at least \qty{20}{\%} of \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} show it''
        reject errors at this coverage?
  \item How many heterozygous sites does one human carry (half of the
        differences between two random genomes lie in each, roughly:
        take one site in \num{1500})? How many in coding sequence?
  \item The \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} are aligned to a reference. Explain why \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} from
        Alu elements often align to the wrong copy and what
        consequence this has for calling variants inside them.
  \item A child is sequenced with both parents. How many new mutations
        do you expect? How many \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} in the child, at this coverage,
        must show a variant absent from \emph{both} parents for it to
        be believed, and why is the parents' coverage as important as
        the child's?
  \item A clinical laboratory captures the exome (\qty{48}{Mb}) and
        sequences it at $100\times$. How many \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} does that take, and
        why is it cheaper per patient than a genome at $28\times$
        although its coverage is higher?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The content of a genome.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item What fraction of the human genome is Alu, and what fraction of
        the \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} of question 7 come from Alu elements?
  \item The reference is \qty{3.2}{Gb} but a diploid cell holds
        \qty{6.4}{Gb}; and a \qty{16}{Gb} onion has about \num{60000}
        genes. What is the coding fraction of the onion genome, if its
        genes average \qty{1.5}{kb} of coding sequence?
  \item Human and chimpanzee differ at \qty{1.2}{\%} of aligned bases.
        How many substitutions is that in \qty{2.9}{Gb} of alignable
        sequence? Split evenly between the two lineages over $6.5$
        million years, what mutation rate per base per year does this
        imply, and per generation of $25$ years? Compare with the
        direct measurement given in the data.
  \item Two vertebrate genome duplications should give up to four
        copies of each ancestral gene. Humans have about \num{20000}
        genes and the invertebrate chordate \emph{Ciona} about
        \num{16000}. What fraction of the duplicates has been lost, on
        the hypothesis that the ancestor had \num{16000}?
  \item Explain why gene number is a poor measure of organismal
        complexity, with the alternative splicing count of
        \cref{ch:b3:rna-regulation} as one argument.
  \item \qty{8}{\%} of the genome is under purifying selection but only
        \qty{1.5}{\%} codes for protein. What is the rest likely to
        be, and how would you test one candidate element?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- An association study.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item $10^{6}$ SNPs are tested. State the Bonferroni threshold for a
        family-wise error of $0.05$, and the expected number of false
        positives at $p < 10^{-5}$.
  \item A disease has prevalence \qty{1}{\%}. A risk allele of frequency
        $0.3$ raises the odds by \qty{5}{\%}. Compute the disease risk
        of a carrier of two copies, of one copy and of none, taking the
        non-carrier risk as \qty{0.9}{\%}. (Multiply the odds.)
  \item Two hundred such alleles are found. Explain what a \omterm{def:b3:genomics:gwas}{polygenic
        score} is, and why the score of a person in the top \qty{1}{\%}
        may correspond to a several-fold risk although each allele
        does almost nothing.
  \item Explain why a SNP that reaches significance is usually not the
        causal variant, and what experiment would identify the causal
        one in the region.
  \item An ancient genome from a \num{40000}-year-old bone is sequenced
        at $c = 0.5$. What fraction of it is \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{read}? Why can this still
        answer questions about population history that a modern genome
        alone cannot?
  \item Summarise: the number of contigs in the pilot \omterm{def:b3:genomics:assembly}{assembly}
        (question~4), the coverage per haploid genome that gives about
        $15$ \omterm{def:b3:genomics:ngs}{reads} per allele (question~7--8), and the number of new
        mutations in a child (question~12).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
