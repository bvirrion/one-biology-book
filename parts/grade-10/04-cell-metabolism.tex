\chapter{Cell Metabolism}\label{ch:g10:cell-metabolism}

Stretch a balloon over the neck of a flask of warm sugar water with a
spoonful of baker's yeast in it. Within an hour the liquid clouds and
fizzes, the balloon stands up, and the flask smells faintly of bread and
beer. The yeast \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} are eating the sugar and turning it into
something else, and the gas they give off is the same carbon dioxide you
breathe out. A leaf in the sun does the reverse: it takes carbon dioxide
in and gives oxygen off. Both are chemistry, run inside \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, at body
temperature, with no flame and no acid --- and this chapter is about
that chemistry.

\section{Metabolism: the chemistry of a cell}

\begin{definition}[Metabolism]\label{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}
The \emph{metabolism}\index{metabolism} of a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} is the set of all the
chemical reactions that take place inside it: those that break
molecules down to release energy, and those that build the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s own
molecules from simpler ones. Every reaction of metabolism is driven by
an \emph{enzyme}\index{enzyme}, a \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} that speeds up one specific
reaction without being consumed by it.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Enzymes make cell chemistry possible]\label{prop:g10:cell-metabolism:enzymes}
Glucose left in air at \qty{37}{\celsius} does not burn; in a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} it
is fully oxidised within minutes. The difference is the \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzymes}: each
one binds a particular molecule (its \emph{substrate}), transforms it
into a product, releases it and starts again, thousands of times per
second. A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{metabolism} is therefore fixed by the set of \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzymes} it
possesses --- and that set is fixed by its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[One enzyme at work]\label{ex:g10:cell-metabolism:catalase}
Pour hydrogen peroxide on a piece of raw liver or potato: it froths
violently as oxygen is released. The \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme} catalase, present in almost
every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, splits the toxic peroxide into water and oxygen at a rate
of millions of molecules per second per \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme} molecule. Boil the liver
first and nothing happens: the \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme}, a \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}, has been destroyed
by heat. Bubble-free peroxide on a stone shows the reaction does not
run by itself.
\end{example}

\section{Two ways of feeding a cell}

\begin{definition}[Autotrophy and heterotrophy]\label{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}
A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} is \emph{autotrophic}\index{autotrophy} if it builds its own
organic molecules from \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:mineral-organic}{mineral matter} alone --- carbon dioxide, water,
\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:mineral-organic}{mineral salts} --- using an external source of energy. Plant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} with
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{chloroplasts}, algae and some bacteria are autotrophs, and their energy
source is light: this is \emph{photosynthesis}\index{photosynthesis}.
A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} is \emph{heterotrophic}\index{heterotrophy} if it must take in
organic molecules made by other organisms, both as building material
and as a source of energy: animal \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, fungi, most bacteria, and the
non-green \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} of plants (roots, for instance) are heterotrophs.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Photosynthesis]\label{prop:g10:cell-metabolism:photosynthesis}
In the light, a chloroplast-containing \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} absorbs carbon dioxide and
water, releases oxygen, and builds glucose, which it then converts into
starch, cellulose, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids} and, with mineral nitrogen, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acids}. In
summary:
\[
6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\ \text{light, chlorophyll}\ }
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2}.
\]
The energy of light is stored in the chemical bonds of the glucose.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
A suspension of the green alga \emph{Chlorella} in a sealed vessel
fitted with an oxygen probe shows the dissolved oxygen rising steadily
when the lamp is on and falling when it is off; the rise stops if the
carbon dioxide of the water is removed. Leaves kept in the dark for two
days no longer stain blue-black with iodine, and a leaf half covered
with foil stains only on its exposed half: the starch is made where
light falls. Radioactive carbon dioxide supplied to a plant ends up in
its sugars within minutes.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Euglena, both at once]\label{ex:g10:cell-metabolism:euglena}
The single-celled \emph{Euglena} has \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{chloroplasts} and swims with a
flagellum. In the light, in mineral water, it grows: autotroph. In the
dark it survives only if organic molecules are added to the water,
which it absorbs: heterotroph. Kept long in the dark it loses its
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{chloroplasts}; brought back to light with a few days' patience it makes
them again. Its \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{metabolism} depends on its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}, which allow both
options, and on its environment, which selects one.
\end{example}

\section{Getting energy from organic molecules}

\begin{proposition}[Cellular respiration]\label{prop:g10:cell-metabolism:respiration}
Every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, autotroph or heterotroph, obtains usable energy by breaking
down organic molecules. When oxygen is available the breakdown is
complete --- this is \emph{cellular respiration}\index{cellular
respiration}, in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria}:
\[
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow
6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O} + \text{energy},
\]
about \qty{2870}{kJ} per mole of glucose (\qty{180}{g}), of which
roughly 40\% is captured in a small molecule,
\emph{ATP}\index{ATP}, that every energy-requiring process of the
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} --- movement, transport, synthesis --- draws on; the rest is
released as heat.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Yeast in a sealed vessel with an oxygen probe consumes no oxygen while
starved; add glucose and the dissolved oxygen falls at a steady rate
while carbon dioxide appears, until the oxygen is exhausted. Germinating
seeds in a vacuum flask warm the flask by several degrees within a day
--- the heat of respiration --- while boiled seeds do not. Muscle \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
seen in the electron microscope are crowded with \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria}, and a
poison of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} stops the muscle.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Fermentation]\label{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}
Without oxygen, some \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} break glucose down only partially, in the
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cytoplasm}, into smaller organic molecules that still hold most of the
energy: this is \emph{fermentation}\index{fermentation}. Yeast
performs alcoholic fermentation,
\[
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \longrightarrow 2\,\mathrm{C_2H_5OH} +
2\,\mathrm{CO_2} + \text{energy},
\]
and muscle \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} short of oxygen, as well as the bacteria of yoghurt,
perform lactic fermentation, turning glucose into lactic acid. A
fermentation yields about fifteen times less ATP per glucose than
respiration.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Yeast in air and out of it]\label{ex:g10:cell-metabolism:pasteur}
Yeast bubbled with air uses glucose sparingly and grows fast: it
respires. The same yeast in a sealed vat uses glucose greedily, grows
little and makes ethanol: it ferments. Pasteur, who described this in
1861, called \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation} "life without air"; the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} switch their
\omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{metabolism} to whichever pathway their environment allows, and, since
\omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation} gives so little energy per glucose, they must burn
through far more sugar to live.
\end{example}

\section{What decides a cell's metabolism}

\begin{proposition}[Genes and environment]\label{prop:g10:cell-metabolism:decides}
A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{metabolism} depends on two things: on its
\emph{equipment} --- the \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzymes} and \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{organelles} its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} allow it to
build, so that a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} without chlorophyll can never photosynthesise
--- and on its \emph{environment} --- light, oxygen, available
nutrients --- which decides which of the possible pathways actually
runs. A yeast is a respirer in air and a fermenter without it; a
\emph{Euglena} is an autotroph in the light and a heterotroph in the
dark; a root \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} of a green plant is a heterotroph all its life, fed
by the leaves.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Reading a metabolic trace]\label{met:g10:cell-metabolism:trace}
Oxygen-probe experiments give a concentration against time.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify what changed at each marked instant (light on,
        substrate added, oxygen exhausted).
  \item Read the slope of each straight segment: rise over run, in
        \unit{mg/L} per minute. A positive slope means net production
        of oxygen, a negative one net consumption.
  \item Remember that a photosynthesising \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} also respires: the slope
        in the light is production minus respiration, so the true rate
        of \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} is the light slope plus the size of the dark
        slope.
  \item Convert if asked: at these concentrations \qty{1}{mg/L} of
        dissolved oxygen is $1/32$ millimole per litre.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[The alga's true rate]\label{ex:g10:cell-metabolism:truerate}
In the \emph{Chlorella} figure the oxygen rises by \qty{2.5}{mg/L} in
5 minutes of light (slope $+0.5$) and falls by \qty{0.5}{mg/L} in 5
minutes of dark (slope $-0.1$). Respiration consumes \qty{0.1}{mg/L}
per minute in the light as well, so \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} produces
$0.5 + 0.1 = \qty{0.6}{mg/L}$ per minute: six times what respiration
uses. The alga is a net producer of oxygen and \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:mineral-organic}{organic matter}, which
is why it grows.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Winemaker's Vat}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a vat of grape juice left to the
yeast: the alcohol it will hold, the gas it will give off, the heat it
will make, and why a cellar can be a dangerous place}]
\label{pb:g10:cell-metabolism:1}
Grape juice (must) contains about \qty{200}{g} of sugar per litre,
which we take as glucose, and no dissolved oxygen once the vat is
sealed. Molar masses: glucose \qty{180}{g/mol}, ethanol
$\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}$ \qty{46}{g/mol}, carbon dioxide \qty{44}{g/mol},
oxygen \qty{32}{g/mol}. The density of ethanol is \qty{0.79}{g/mL}.
One mole of gas occupies about \qty{24}{L} at cellar temperature. The
vat holds \qty{1000}{L}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- What the yeast makes.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which pathway does the yeast use in the sealed vat, and why?
        Write its equation.
  \item How many moles of glucose does one litre of must contain?
  \item Compute the mass of ethanol produced per litre, then its
        volume, then the alcohol content of the wine in per cent by
        volume.
  \item Compute the mass of carbon dioxide produced per litre of must,
        and its volume as a gas.
  \item For the whole vat, what volume of carbon dioxide is released?
        Compare with the volume of a cellar of \qty{4}{m} by \qty{6}{m}
        by \qty{2.5}{m}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- If the yeast had air.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Write the equation the yeast would follow if the must were
        continuously aerated.
  \item Compute the mass of oxygen needed to respire the sugar of one
        litre of must, and the mass of carbon dioxide released.
  \item Compare the carbon dioxide of question 7 with that of
        question 4, and explain the difference in terms of how far the
        glucose is broken down.
  \item Respiration yields about 30 ATP per glucose and \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation}
        2. If the yeast needs the same ATP in both cases, in which case

  \item Explain why a winemaker nevertheless keeps air out.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The heat of the vat.}
\omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{Fermentation} releases about \qty{70}{kJ} of heat per mole of glucose;
heating \qty{1}{kg} of water by \qty{1}{\celsius} takes \qty{4.2}{kJ}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the heat released per litre of must, then for the
        vat.
  \item If none of it escaped, by how much would the vat's temperature
        rise? (Take the must as water.)
  \item Yeast \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzymes} are destroyed above about \qty{40}{\celsius}.
        Starting from \qty{20}{\celsius}, would an uncooled vat
        survive? What does this tell you about why large vats are
        cooled?
  \item Explain, using \cref{prop:g10:cell-metabolism:enzymes}, why
        heat stops \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation} even though the sugar is still there.
  \item Respiration would have released about \qty{2870}{kJ} per mole.
        Where does the energy that \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation} does \emph{not}
        release remain?
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Carbon dioxide is denser than air. Where in the cellar does it

  \item A worker's \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, deprived of oxygen, would switch to which

  \item The \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation} stops on its own when the alcohol reaches

  \item Bread dough uses the same yeast for an hour. What do the
