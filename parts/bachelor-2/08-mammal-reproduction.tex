\chapter{Sexual Reproduction of Mammals}\label{ch:b2:mammal-reproduction}

A human egg is a tenth of a millimetre across, the largest cell in
the body; a sperm cell is a nucleus with a propeller, one of three
hundred million released at once, of which a few dozen will reach
the egg and one will enter it. Within a week the fertilised egg has
become a hollow ball that burrows into the wall of the uterus and
builds, out of its own outer cells, an organ --- the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} ---
through which its mother will feed it for nine months. Then it is
born and fed on milk. Every mammal does this; no other animals do
all of it. This chapter follows the gametes to fertilisation, the
embryo to \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{implantation}, and the organism through gestation, birth
and lactation, and ends with how the two sexes are made.

\section{Gonads and gametes}

\begin{definition}[The testis and spermatogenesis]\label{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}
\index{spermatogenesis}\index{seminiferous tubule}\index{Sertoli cell}\index{Leydig cell}
The \emph{testis} is a mass of \emph{seminiferous tubules}, several
hundred metres in all, whose walls make sperm; between the tubules
lie \emph{Leydig cells}, which make testosterone. In the tubule wall,
\emph{spermatogonia} at the outer edge divide by mitosis, keeping a
stem population and sending cells inward; these grow into primary
\emph{spermatocytes}, which undergo \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} I (to two secondary
spermatocytes) and \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} II (to four \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} \emph{spermatids});
the spermatids shed most of their cytoplasm, condense their nucleus,
grow a flagellum and cap the nucleus with the \emph{acrosome}, a
vesicle of enzymes, to become \emph{spermatozoa} that are released
into the lumen. Large \emph{Sertoli cells} span the wall, nursing the
germ cells and forming, by tight junctions between them, a
\emph{blood--testis barrier} that keeps the immune system away from
cells that first appear at puberty. The whole sequence takes about
\num{64} days in a man and yields some $10^{8}$ sperm a day from
puberty to old age. Sperm leave the testis immotile and mature in
the \emph{epididymis} for two weeks.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The ovary and oogenesis]\label{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}
\index{oogenesis}\index{follicle}\index{ovulation}\index{corpus luteum}
Oogenesis is the mirror image of \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{spermatogenesis} in almost every
respect. The \emph{oogonia} multiply only in the fetus: by birth a
girl carries a stock of about a million primary \emph{oocytes},
each arrested in \omterm{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:stages}{prophase I} of \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} and wrapped in a single layer
of cells --- a \emph{primordial follicle} --- and no new ones are ever
made. From puberty, each month a cohort of follicles grows: the
oocyte enlarges, the surrounding \emph{granulosa} cells multiply
into layers, an outer \emph{theca} forms, a fluid-filled cavity
opens (the \emph{Graafian follicle}, two centimetres across), and
usually a single one reaches \emph{ovulation}: the oocyte, only now
completing \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} I and arresting again in metaphase II, is expelled
with its halo of granulosa cells into the oviduct. The emptied
follicle becomes the \emph{corpus luteum}, a temporary gland of
progesterone. \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{Meiosis} produces one egg and three minute polar bodies
--- all the cytoplasm goes to one cell --- and \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} II is
completed only if a sperm enters. Of the million oocytes, about
\num{400} are ovulated in a lifetime; the rest degenerate
(\emph{atresia}), and when the stock is exhausted, around fifty,
cycles stop: the menopause.
\end{definition}

\section{Fertilisation}

\begin{proposition}[The steps of fertilisation]\label{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:fertilisation}
\index{fertilisation!mammalian}\index{acrosome reaction}\index{zona pellucida}\index{cortical reaction}
Sperm deposited in the vagina --- some $3\times 10^{8}$ --- cross the
cervical mucus, are swept up the uterus by its contractions, and a
few hundred reach the ampulla of the oviduct within an hour, where
the egg waits for a day. On the way the sperm undergo
\emph{capacitation}: secretions of the female tract strip their
membranes of coating proteins and make them hyperactive. At the egg
a sperm pushes through the granulosa cells, binds a glycoprotein of
the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:fertilisation}{zona pellucida}}, and undergoes the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:fertilisation}{acrosome
reaction}}, releasing enzymes that digest a path through the zona;
its membrane fuses with the egg's, and the sperm nucleus enters. The
egg responds within seconds: calcium sweeps across it, and cortical
granules release enzymes that harden the zona and strip its sperm
receptors --- the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:fertilisation}{cortical reaction}}, the block to polyspermy.
The egg completes \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} II, sheds the second polar body, and the
two \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} \emph{pronuclei} replicate their DNA, come together and
enter the first mitosis: the zygote is a day old. Species
specificity lies in the zona proteins, which is why a mouse sperm
does not enter a human egg.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Edwards and Steptoe (1978) fertilised a human oocyte, recovered
from the ovary shortly before \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation}, with capacitated sperm in
a dish, cultured the embryo to eight cells and returned it to the
uterus, where it implanted and was born: in vitro fertilisation.
Every step of the sequence above had first been worked out in the
sea urchin and the mouse, where the fusion, the calcium wave and the
cortical granule release can be watched; in the mouse, removing one
zona glycoprotein (ZP2 or ZP3) by \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation} leaves eggs that sperm
cannot bind.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Why one egg needs millions of sperm]\label{thm:b2:mammal-reproduction:poisson}
\index{polyspermy}
If, of $N$ sperm deposited, each reaches the egg's surface
independently with a small probability $\pi$, the number arriving
is Poisson with mean $m = N\pi$ and the probability that at least one
arrives is
\[
P = 1 - e^{-m}.
\]
With $N = 3\times 10^{8}$ and $\pi \approx 10^{-8}$, $m \approx 3$
and $P \approx 0.95$; with $N = 2\times 10^{7}$ (the clinical
threshold of low sperm count), $m = 0.2$ and $P = 0.18$. The same
arithmetic shows why the block to polyspermy is indispensable: when
$P$ is high, several sperm arrive within seconds of one another,
and an egg entered by two would be triploid and die.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each sperm is an independent trial with success probability $\pi$;
the number of successes is binomial, which for large $N$ and small
$\pi$ is Poisson with mean $N\pi$. The probability of zero
successes is $e^{-m}$, so of at least one, $1 - e^{-m}$; the
probability of two or more is $1 - e^{-m}(1 + m)$, about $0.8$ for
$m = 3$.
\end{proof}

\section{Implantation and the placenta}

\begin{definition}[Cleavage, blastocyst, implantation]\label{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}
\index{blastocyst}\index{trophoblast}\index{inner cell mass}\index{implantation}
The zygote divides every twelve to twenty hours without growing
(\emph{cleavage}) as it is moved down the oviduct: two cells, four,
eight, a compact ball of sixteen (\emph{morula}), and by day five a
\emph{blastocyst} of about a hundred cells: an outer layer, the
\emph{trophoblast}, around a cavity, with a clump of cells at one
side, the \emph{inner cell mass}. The trophoblast will build the
\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} and membranes; the inner cell mass will become the embryo
--- and, taken out at this stage, gives embryonic stem cells. On day
six the blastocyst hatches from its zona, sticks to the lining of
the uterus, and the trophoblast invades it: cells fuse into a
multinucleate front that digests maternal tissue and opens maternal
capillaries, so that by day twelve the embryo is buried in the wall
and bathed in maternal blood. This is \emph{implantation}. It
succeeds only in a uterus prepared by progesterone, in a window of a
few days; about half of all human conceptions fail before or at this
step, usually from chromosomal errors of the egg.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The placenta]\label{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}
\index{placenta}\index{chorionic villus}\index{umbilical cord}
The \emph{placenta} is an organ made jointly by the embryo (the
chorion, from the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{trophoblast}) and the mother (the uterine lining).
Finger-like \emph{chorionic villi}, branched into a surface of some
\qty{12}{m^2} at term, hang in lakes of maternal blood delivered by
the uterine arteries at half a litre a minute; inside each villus
run fetal capillaries, joined to the fetus through the two arteries
and one vein of the \emph{umbilical cord}. The two bloods never mix:
they are separated by the villus wall, a few micrometres of
\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{trophoblast} and capillary endothelium, across which oxygen,
$\mathrm{CO_2}$, water, urea and fat-soluble molecules diffuse,
glucose and amino acids are carried by transporters, and maternal
antibodies (IgG) are ferried by receptor, giving the newborn its
first months of immunity. The placenta is also a gland: from the
first days the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{trophoblast} secretes \emph{human chorionic
gonadotropin} (hCG), which keeps the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum} making
progesterone, and from the third month the placenta itself makes the
progesterone and oestrogens that maintain the pregnancy
(\cref{ch:b2:reproductive-hormones}). It is, in effect, a lung, a
gut, a kidney and an endocrine gland grown for nine months and
discarded at birth.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Exchange across the placenta]\label{thm:b2:mammal-reproduction:fick}
\index{Fick's law!placenta}
A gas crossing a membrane of area $A$ and thickness $d$ moves at a
rate $J = K\,A\,\Delta P/d$, where $\Delta P$ is the difference in
its partial pressure and $K$ the tissue's permeability. For oxygen
in tissue $K \approx \qty{3e-8}{mL/(cm.min.mmHg)}$; with $A =
\qty{12}{m^2}$, $d = \qty{3.5}{\micro m}$ and $\Delta P =
\qty{20}{mmHg}$ (maternal \qty{40}{mmHg} in the intervillous space
against \qty{20}{mmHg} in the umbilical artery), the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} could
pass about \qty{200}{mL} of oxygen a minute, some eight times the
\qty{25}{mL/min} a term fetus consumes. Oxygen transfer is therefore
limited not by diffusion but by blood flow; the fetus works at a low
oxygen pressure and compensates with a haemoglobin of higher
affinity and a higher red-cell count.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fick's law states that flux is proportional to the gradient
$\Delta P/d$ and to the area; the constant $K$ absorbs the solubility
and diffusion coefficient of the gas in tissue. Numerically:
$3\times 10^{-8}\times 1.2\times 10^{5}\times 20/(3.5\times 10^{-4})
= \qty{206}{mL/min}$ with $A$ in \unit{cm^2} and $d$ in \unit{cm}.
The fetal demand is about \qty{7}{mL} per kilogram per minute for
\qty{3.5}{kg}.
\end{proof}

\section{Gestation, birth and lactation}

\begin{proposition}[Gestation and birth]\label{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:birth}
\index{gestation}\index{parturition}\index{oxytocin}
Gestation lasts \num{20} days in a mouse, \num{280} in a human, \num{660}
in an elephant --- roughly as the quarter power of body mass, with
primates slow for their size. It is maintained by progesterone,
which keeps the uterine muscle quiet and the cervix closed. Birth
(\emph{parturition}) is triggered by the fetus itself: its adrenal
cortisol, rising at term, shifts the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} from progesterone to
oestrogen, which fits the uterus with oxytocin receptors and gap
junctions and starts prostaglandin release. Then a \emph{positive
feedback} takes over: the head presses on the cervix, sensory
nerves signal the hypothalamus, \emph{oxytocin} is released from the
posterior pituitary, the uterus contracts, the head presses harder;
the loop escalates until delivery, after which the stimulus ends
and the loop stops --- one of the few positive feedbacks in
physiology, and one that must end in an event. The \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} is
delivered minutes later.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Ferguson (1941) showed in the rabbit that distending the cervix
provoked uterine contractions and that cutting the spinal cord or
removing the posterior pituitary abolished the response: a
neuroendocrine reflex from cervix to pituitary to uterus. Oxytocin,
isolated and synthesised by du Vigneaud in 1953, is now the standard
drug for inducing labour.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Lactation]\label{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:lactation}
\index{lactation}\index{prolactin}\index{milk}
The mammary gland, a modified sweat gland, is built during pregnancy
by oestrogen, progesterone and prolactin but held back from
secreting by progesterone; when the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} leaves, progesterone
falls and, under \emph{prolactin} from the anterior pituitary, the
alveoli begin to make milk. Two reflexes run the process: suckling
stimulates prolactin release (which maintains synthesis and
suppresses \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation}) and oxytocin release (which contracts the
cells around the alveoli and ejects the milk). Human milk carries
about \qty{7}{\%} lactose, \qty{4}{\%} fat, \qty{1}{\%} protein and
\qty{2.9}{kJ/mL}; the first days' \emph{colostrum} is rich in
antibodies (IgA) that coat the infant's gut. A mother makes about
\qty{750}{mL} a day, at a cost of some \qty{2.5}{MJ} --- a quarter of
her food --- for months; lactation is the most expensive part of
mammalian reproduction and the one that names the class.
\end{proposition}

\section{Making the two sexes}

\begin{proposition}[Sex determination and differentiation]\label{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:sex}
\index{SRY}\index{anti-Müllerian hormone}\index{sex differentiation}
Until the sixth week a human embryo of either sex has the same
equipment: an indifferent gonad, two pairs of ducts (\emph{Wolffian}
and \emph{Müllerian}) and neutral external genitalia. The gene
\emph{SRY} on the Y chromosome switches on in the gonad and turns it
into a \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis}; without it the gonad becomes an ovary. The fetal
\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis} then does two things: its \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{Sertoli cells} secrete
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:sex}{anti-Müllerian hormone}}, which makes the Müllerian ducts
regress, and its \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{Leydig cells} secrete \emph{testosterone}, which
keeps the Wolffian ducts (the future epididymis, vas deferens,
seminal vesicles) and, converted to dihydrotestosterone, moulds the
external genitalia into penis and scrotum. In the absence of both
signals the Wolffian ducts regress, the Müllerian ducts become the
oviducts, uterus and upper vagina, and the genitalia develop as
female. The female path is the one taken when nothing is said: the
male path must be actively imposed by the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis}, and every step at
which the imposition fails --- a mutant SRY, a missing hormone
receptor --- gives an XY individual with a female or intermediate
body.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Jost (1947) removed the gonads from rabbit fetuses in the uterus
before their ducts had differentiated: castrated fetuses of either
chromosomal sex developed a uterus and oviducts and female
genitalia. A crystal of testosterone implanted in a castrated fetus
kept the Wolffian ducts on that side but did not remove the
Müllerian ducts; a grafted fetal \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis} did both, on its own side:
the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{testis} must make a second, then unknown, factor to suppress the
female ducts, identified thirty years later as \omterm{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:sex}{anti-Müllerian
hormone}. Later the sex-reversing effect of a small piece of the Y
chromosome pinned the switch to \emph{SRY} (1990), and a mouse made
XX with the \emph{Sry} gene alone developed as a male.
\end{proof}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:1}
Compare \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:testis}{spermatogenesis} and \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{oogenesis}: when the stem cells divide,
how many gametes one \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} yields, how long a gamete takes to
make, where the arrests are, and how many gametes a lifetime
produces.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:2}
List the steps of fertilisation from the arrival of a sperm at the
zona to the first mitosis, and say which step blocks polyspermy.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:3}
Name the two cell populations of the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{blastocyst} and the fate of
each. On what day does \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{implantation} begin, and what hormone must
have prepared the uterus?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:4}
For each of the following, say whether it crosses the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} and
how: oxygen, glucose, maternal red cells, IgG antibodies, urea,
alcohol, most bacteria.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:5}
With $\pi = 10^{-8}$ per sperm, compute the probability of
fertilisation for sperm counts of $3\times 10^{8}$, $10^{8}$,
$4\times 10^{7}$ and $10^{7}$. At which count does it drop below one
half? What does this say about the shape of the dose--response of
fertility to sperm count?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:6}
A girl is born with $10^{6}$ oocytes, has \num{300000} at puberty
(age 13) and about \num{1000} at 50, having ovulated \num{400}.
Compute the mean rate of atresia (oocytes lost per day) before and
after puberty, and compare with the rate of \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:7}
Fetal haemoglobin has a half-saturation pressure $P_{50} =
\qty{19}{mmHg}$, adult haemoglobin \qty{27}{mmHg}; take the
saturation as $S = P^{n}/(P_{50}^{n} + P^{n})$ with $n = 2.7$.
Compute both saturations at \qty{30}{mmHg}, the pressure in the
umbilical vein, and explain the advantage.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:8}
hCG appears in maternal blood at \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{implantation} (day 7 after
fertilisation) at about \qty{1}{IU/L} and doubles every two days; a
pregnancy test detects \qty{25}{IU/L}. On what day after
fertilisation does the test turn positive? On what day after the
last menstrual period (\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation} on day 14)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:9}
Recompute the placental oxygen transfer of the theorem for a
\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} of \qty{6}{m^2} with a villus wall of \qty{10}{\micro m}, as
in some pathologies, and compare with the fetal demand. What happens
to the fetus?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:10}
Predict the duct system and external genitalia of: (a) an XY fetus
whose cells lack the testosterone receptor; (b) an XY fetus lacking
\omterm{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:sex}{anti-Müllerian hormone}; (c) an XX fetus exposed to high testosterone
from its adrenals; (d) an XX fetus carrying \emph{SRY} on an X
chromosome. Explain each from Jost's results.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:11}
Identical twins arise when one embryo splits. If it splits before
day 3 the twins have separate \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placentas} and sacs; between days 4 and
8, one \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} and two sacs; between days 8 and 13, one \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} and
one sac; later, they are conjoined. In a series of identical twins,
\qty{30}{\%} have separate \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placentas}, \qty{68}{\%} one \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} and
two sacs, \qty{2}{\%} one sac. Deduce which stage of development
most often splits, and explain in terms of the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{blastocyst} which
structures are shared in each case.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:mammal-reproduction:12}
Marsupials give birth after a few weeks to a bean-sized young that
completes its growth on a teat in the pouch; placental mammals
gestate long and give birth to a large young. Compare the two
strategies in terms of maternal cost, risk, and the mother's ability
to abandon a litter in a bad season, and suggest why placentals
have displaced marsupials on every continent but Australia.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A Pregnancy in Numbers}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a human pregnancy followed from
the sperm to the milk, through the probabilities of fertilisation,
the timing of implantation and its hormone, the exchange capacity of
the placenta, and the energy cost of gestation and lactation, ending
on the fertilisation probability, the day the test turns positive,
the placenta's oxygen margin and the cost of a day of milk}]
\label{pb:b2:mammal-reproduction:1}
Data: $3\times 10^{8}$ sperm per ejaculate, each reaching the egg
with probability $10^{-8}$; sperm swim at \qty{50}{\micro m/s}; the
ampulla is \qty{15}{cm} from the cervix. hCG at \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{implantation} (day 7)
\qty{1}{IU/L}, doubling every \qty{2}{d}, test threshold
\qty{25}{IU/L}. \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{Placenta} at term: \qty{12}{m^2}, wall
\qty{3.5}{\micro m}, $K_{\mathrm{O_2}} = \qty{3e-8}{mL/(cm.min.mmHg)}$,
$\Delta P = \qty{20}{mmHg}$; fetus \qty{3.5}{kg} consuming
\qty{7}{mL} of oxygen per kilogram per minute; maternal placental
blood flow \qty{500}{mL/min} carrying \qty{0.16}{mL} of oxygen per
millilitre of blood. Glucose: fetus uses \qty{5}{mg} per kilogram per
minute; maternal blood \qty{0.9}{g/L}. Milk: \qty{750}{mL/d} at
\qty{2.9}{kJ/mL}, made at \qty{80}{\%} efficiency; infant gains
\qty{30}{g/d} of tissue at \qty{6}{kJ/g} deposited and spends
\qty{300}{kJ/kg/d} on maintenance at \qty{4}{kg}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Fertilisation.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the mean number of sperm reaching the egg and the
        probability of fertilisation.
  \item Compute the probability that two or more reach it, and say
        what would happen without the \omterm{prop:b2:mammal-reproduction:fertilisation}{cortical reaction}.
  \item How long would a sperm take to swim to the ampulla? Sperm
        are found there within \qty{30}{min}: what carries them?
  \item Only a few hundred sperm reach the ampulla. What fraction of
        the ejaculate is that, and where are the others lost?
  \item An egg is fertilisable for about a day, sperm survive about
        three days in the tract. Over how many days of the cycle can
        intercourse lead to conception?
  \item A man's count falls to $5\times 10^{7}$. Recompute the
        probability of fertilisation per cycle, and the expected
        number of cycles to conception (geometric law).
  \item Explain why the egg, and not the sperm, provides the
        mitochondria of the embryo.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{Implantation} and its hormone.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many cleavage divisions have occurred by day 5 if they
        take \qty{16}{h} each? How many cells?
  \item On what day after fertilisation does hCG reach the test
        threshold? After the last period (\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation} on day 14)?
  \item Why must hCG appear within days of \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{implantation}? What
        happens to the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{corpus luteum}, and to the pregnancy, if it
        does not?
  \item hCG peaks near \qty{1e5}{IU/L} around week 10 and then falls.
        How many doublings from \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{implantation} is that, and why can it
        fall afterward without loss of the pregnancy?
  \item Half of conceptions are lost before or at \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{implantation},
        mostly from aneuploid eggs. Explain why aneuploidy is more
        common in eggs than in sperm, using the timeline of
        \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{oogenesis}.
  \item Twins: an embryo that splits on day 6. State whether the
        twins share the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} and the amniotic sac.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the maximal diffusive oxygen transfer.
  \item Compute the fetal oxygen demand and the diffusion margin.
  \item Compute the oxygen delivered per minute by maternal blood
        flow to the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta}. What fraction must be extracted to
        meet the demand?
  \item Compute the fetal glucose demand in grams per day, and the
        volume of maternal blood that contains it. Compare with the
        daily maternal placental flow.
  \item The fetus's blood leaves the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} at \qty{30}{mmHg} of
        oxygen. Why does it not suffocate at a pressure that would
        leave an adult unconscious?
  \item Name two things the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta} does that no other organ of the
        embryo could do at the time, and one thing it cannot do.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Birth and milk.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Describe the positive feedback of labour and explain why it
        does not run away before term.
  \item Compute the energy in a day's milk and the energy the
        mother spends to make it.
  \item Compute the infant's daily energy budget (growth plus
        maintenance) and compare with the milk supplied.
  \item Compute the mother's total energy cost of nine months of
        lactation, and compare with the \qty{300}{MJ} cost of the
        pregnancy itself.
  \item Explain why suckling delays the return of \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:ovary}{ovulation}, and
        why this matters in populations without contraception.
  \item State the result: the probability of fertilisation, the day
        the test turns positive, the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta}'s oxygen margin, and
        the cost of a day's milk.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
