\begin{solution}{exo:b2:global-change:2}
Degree-days: the sum over days of the excess of the daily mean
temperature over a base, the \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:phenology}{thermal time} that governs the
development of plants and ectotherms. Mismatch: when two
interacting species time their seasons by different cues and
warming moves one and not the other --- the flycatcher, cued by
day length in Africa, arrives on its old date to find the
caterpillar peak, cued by temperature, already two weeks past.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:global-change:3}
About \qty{150}{m} uphill and \qty{150}{km} poleward per degree. A
species at the summit has no higher ground: its isotherm rises
above the mountain and its habitat disappears, while the area of
each band shrinks with altitude on the way up.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:global-change:4}
Dissolved \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} forms carbonic acid, which
releases hydrogen ions, and the pH falls in proportion to the
logarithm of the \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}} pressure. The extra
hydrogen ions convert carbonate to bicarbonate, so the carbonate
concentration and the \omterm{thm:b2:global-change:acid}{saturation state} of calcium carbonate fall.
Organisms that build aragonite --- corals, pteropods, many larvae
--- and calcite --- coccolithophores, foraminifera, molluscs ---
must spend more to build shells and, below saturation, lose them.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:global-change:7}
\qty{3}{\celsius}: isotherms rise $3000/6.5 = \qty{460}{m}$; the
band moves to \qtyrange{1260}{1860}{m}, but the mountain ends at
\qty{1800}{m}: the top \qty{60}{m} of the band are lost and the rest
sits where the area is $2^{-460/300} = 0.35$ of what it was --- about
two thirds of the area gone. \qty{5}{\celsius}: \qty{770}{m}; the
band would be \qtyrange{1570}{2170}{m}; only \qtyrange{1570}{1800}{m}
exists, a third of the band's width at $2^{-770/300} = 0.17$ of the
area --- under a tenth of the original area: the species is
effectively gone.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:global-change:10}
$c = 2\sqrt{0.7\times 20} = \qty{7.5}{km/yr}$; \qty{1000}{km} in
about 130 years. Halving either $r$ or $D$ divides $c$ by
$\sqrt 2$, to \qty{5.3}{km/yr}; neither halves it --- to halve the
speed one must quarter the product $rD$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:global-change:11}
Background: $8\times 10^{6}\times 10^{-6} = 8$ a year, 800 a
century; at a thousand times, 8000 a year, \num{800000} a century.
Ten thousand documented in a century is about ten times
background, not a thousand --- but documentation covers only the
few per cent of species that are described and monitored (birds,
mammals, a few plants), among which the rate is indeed a hundred
times background or more; for the undescribed majority, most
extinctions are never seen, and the species--area estimate is the
only handle. The truth lies between the counted and the
estimated, which is why the range quoted is so wide.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:global-change:12}
The temperature is proportional to \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{cumulative emissions}; when
net emissions are zero the cumulative total stops growing and so
does the warming. In the \omterm{thm:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:box}{box model}, with emissions zero, the
atmospheric excess begins to fall as the sinks work on, which
would cool --- but the ocean, still warming toward equilibrium
with the raised \ensuremath{\mathrm{CO_2}}, continues to warm the
surface, and the two effects nearly cancel: the temperature stays
where it is for centuries. Missing from the argument: the other
greenhouse gases (methane's short lifetime means its warming
falls quickly once emissions stop), the aerosols that now mask
some warming and vanish within weeks of the emissions that make
them, and the feedbacks --- permafrost carbon, forest dieback ---
that could add emissions of their own.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b2:global-change:1}
\textbf{1.} A: $700 + 80\times 10 = \qty{1500}{GtC}$. B: $700 +
\tfrac12\times 50\times 10 = \qty{950}{GtC}$.
\textbf{2.} A: $1.65\times 1.5 = \qty{2.5}{\celsius}$. B:
$1.65\times 0.95 = \qty{1.6}{\celsius}$.
\textbf{3.} 2050. A: \qty{1000}{GtC}, \qty{1.65}{\celsius}. B:
emissions $10\int_0^{30}(1 - t/50)\,\mathrm{d}t = 10(30 - 9) =
\qty{210}{GtC}$, total 910, \qty{1.5}{\celsius}.
\textbf{4.} $\lambda\times 100 = \qty{0.165}{\celsius}$ per decade,
slightly below the observed $0.2$ (which includes the other gases
and the loss of aerosol masking).
\textbf{5.} Yes: \omterm{prop:b2:global-change:tcre}{cumulative emissions} stop growing in 2070, and so
does the warming, which stays at \qty{1.6}{\celsius} --- the
proportionality means the temperature is set by the total, not by
the rate.
\textbf{6.} $2/1.65\times 1000 = \qty{1212}{GtC}$; path A reaches it
after $512/10 = 51$ years, in 2071.
\textbf{7.} $\sqrt{2\times 200/0.1} = 63$ days.
\textbf{8.} Warming relative to 2020: A $2.5 - 1.2 =
\qty{1.3}{\celsius}$, B \qty{0.4}{\celsius}. Advance $\Delta T/b$:
A 13 days, B 4 days.
\textbf{9.} Gap: A $20 + 13 = 33$ days; B 24 days.
\textbf{10.} Caterpillars available from day 15 to day 25 after
leafing. B: the bird arrives at day 24, within the window, just.
A: day 33, eight days after the last caterpillar.
\textbf{11.} The bird advances $3\times 1.3 = 4$ days: gap $33 - 4 =
29$ days --- still outside the window.
\textbf{12.} The bird can live at the new temperature; what it
cannot do is feed chicks when the food has gone, because its cue
and its prey's cue respond to warming differently.
\textbf{13.} A: $1.3/6.5\times 1000 = \qty{200}{m}$ up and
$1.3/0.65\times 100 = \qty{200}{km}$ poleward. B: \qty{57}{m} and
\qty{57}{km}.
\textbf{14.} In 80 years the tree moves \qty{8}{km}: it cannot
track \qty{200}{km} (A) and falls short of \qty{57}{km} (B) too ---
trees lag, and their ranges will be out of equilibrium with climate
for centuries on either path.
\textbf{15.} A: the band would be \qtyrange{1700}{2000}{m}: it
reaches the summit, its upper third is cut off, and it holds on
in a strip a third smaller and $2^{-200/300} = 0.63$ times as wide
in area --- less than half its former area, at the top of the
mountain with nowhere further to go.
\textbf{16.} B: \qty{57}{m} up, area factor $2^{-57/300} = 0.88$: a
\qty{12}{\%} loss.
\textbf{17.} Eight decades at \qty{30}{km}: \qty{240}{km}, more than
the \qty{200}{km} required on A: the fish keeps up (and moves out
of its old fishery).
\textbf{18.} At the lower edge the species meets competitors,
pathogens and predators moving up from below that it did not face
before, and its own physiology, adapted to the old temperature, is
outperformed by theirs: the retreat is competitive as much as
physiological.
\textbf{19.} A: $\mathrm{pH} = 8.2 - 0.9\log_{10}(600/280) = 7.90$;
hydrogen ions $10^{0.30} = 2.0$ times 1750's. B: $8.04$; $1.44$
times.
\textbf{20.} $\Omega = 4/(\text{ratio})$: A $2.0$, B $2.8$. At 2 the
corals still calcify but slowly, their skeletons weaker and their
reefs eroding faster than they grow; at $2.8$ they build, though
the heat of path B still bleaches them.
\textbf{21.} A: $400\times 0.4^{0.25} = 318$, 82 lost. B:
$400\times 0.8^{0.25} = 378$, 22 lost.
\textbf{22.} A: $318\times(1 - 0.05\times 1.3) = 298$. B:
$378\times(1 - 0.05\times 0.4) = 371$.
\textbf{23.} A: $1 - 298/400 = \qty{26}{\%}$. B: \qty{7}{\%}.
\textbf{24.} On both, clearing: 82 against 20 species on A, 22
against 7 on B --- the model's warming term is modest; in reality
the two interact, since a fragmented forest cannot shift its range.
\textbf{25.} Path A: \qty{2.5}{\celsius}, pH $7.90$, a quarter of the
tree species lost. Path B: \qty{1.6}{\celsius}, pH $8.04$, \qty{7}{\%}
lost.
\end{solution}
