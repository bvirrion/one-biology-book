\chapter{The Heart and the Cardiac Cycle}\label{ch:b2:heart}

Cut out a frog's \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} and drop it into salt solution, and it goes
on beating for hours --- no nerves, no brain, no \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}, just a
muscle that contracts on its own about once a second because a
small patch of its cells cannot stay still. A human \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} does this
some three billion times in a life, moving on the order of two
hundred million litres, without ever being switched off. This
chapter is about that pump: its chambers and valves, the cycle of
filling and emptying and the pressures that drive it, the electrical
system that times it and the trace it leaves on the skin, the law
that lets it match its output to its inflow beat by beat, and the
nerves and hormones that adjust its pace and force.

\section{The pump}

\begin{definition}[Chambers and valves]\label{def:b2:heart:anatomy}
\index{heart}\index{atrium}\index{ventricle}\index{heart valve}
The heart is two pumps side by side, each an \emph{atrium} that
receives \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} from the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} and a \emph{ventricle} that ejects it
into an \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{artery}. The right heart takes \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} from the venae cavae
and sends it to the lungs through the pulmonary \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{artery}; the left
heart takes it back from the pulmonary \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} and sends it round the
body through the aorta. Four valves make the flow one-way: the
\emph{atrioventricular valves} (tricuspid on the right, mitral on
the left), flaps tethered by cords to papillary muscles so that
they cannot be blown back into the atrium; and the
\emph{semilunar valves} (pulmonary and aortic), three pockets that
fill and seal when the arterial pressure exceeds the ventricle's.
The valves are passive: they open and close on pressure
differences alone. The ventricular wall is \emph{myocardium},
striated muscle of branched cells joined end to end by
\emph{intercalated discs} full of gap junctions, so that the whole
ventricle is electrically one cell and contracts as one; the left
ventricle's wall is three times thicker than the right's because it
pumps against five times the pressure. The heart muscle is fed by
its own \emph{coronary arteries}, which fill in diastole, and it
runs almost entirely on aerobic metabolism --- it cannot borrow.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The cardiac cycle]\label{prop:b2:heart:cycle}
\index{cardiac cycle}\index{systole}\index{diastole}\index{stroke volume}\index{ejection fraction}
One beat at rest lasts about \qty{0.8}{s}. \emph{Diastole}
(\qty{0.5}{s}): the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles} relax, their pressure falls below the
atria's, the atrioventricular valves open and \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} flows in,
mostly passively, then with a final push from atrial contraction;
the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} ends diastole holding about \qty{120}{mL} (the
\emph{end-diastolic volume}). \emph{Systole} (\qty{0.3}{s}): the
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles} contract; as soon as their pressure exceeds the atrial
pressure the atrioventricular valves close (the first \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} sound,
``lub''), and for a few hundredths of a second the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle}
squeezes a closed chamber --- \emph{isovolumetric contraction} ---
until its pressure passes the arterial pressure (\qty{80}{mmHg} on
the left), when the semilunar valves open and \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} is ejected. The
left \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} reaches \qty{120}{mmHg} and expels about
\qty{70}{mL} (the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume}}, an \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{ejection fraction}}
of \qty{60}{\%}); as it relaxes, its pressure falls below the
aorta's, the aortic valve snaps shut (the second sound, ``dub''),
and an isovolumetric relaxation brings the pressure down to the
atrial level, when filling resumes. The right \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} does the same
at a fifth of the pressure, ejecting the same volume.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[The work of the heart]\label{thm:b2:heart:work}
\index{cardiac work}\index{pressure--volume loop}
Plotted as pressure against volume, one beat of the left \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle}
traces a loop --- filling along the bottom, isovolumetric
contraction up the right side, ejection along the top from
\qty{120}{mL} to \qty{50}{mL}, isovolumetric relaxation down the
left --- and the area of the loop is the mechanical work of the
beat:
\[
W = \oint P\,\mathrm{d}V \approx \bar P_{\text{ejection}}\times \text{stroke volume} \approx \qty{100}{mmHg}\times\qty{70}{mL} = \qty{0.93}{J}.
\]
At 72 beats a minute the left \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} does about \qty{1.1}{W}, the
right \qty{0.2}{W}; the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}'s metabolism is some \qty{8}{W}, so its
mechanical efficiency is near \qty{15}{\%}, the rest being heat and
the cost of tension. Raising the pressure the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} pumps against
(hypertension, a narrowed aortic valve) raises the work per beat in
proportion, and the muscle thickens in response as any muscle does
--- until its coronary supply can no longer keep up with its bulk.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Work is force times distance, and for a pressure acting on a moving
wall, pressure times swept volume: $\mathrm{d}W = P\,\mathrm{d}V$.
Round a closed loop the net work is the enclosed area (filling at
low pressure costs little; ejection at high pressure returns much
more). $\qty{100}{mmHg} = \qty{1.33e4}{Pa}$ and $\qty{70}{mL} =
\qty{7e-5}{m^3}$: $1.33\times 10^{4}\times 7\times 10^{-5} =
\qty{0.93}{J}$; times $1.2$ beats a second, \qty{1.1}{W}.
\end{proof}

\section{The heart's own clock}

\begin{proposition}[Automaticity and conduction]\label{prop:b2:heart:conduction}
\index{sinoatrial node}\index{pacemaker}\index{atrioventricular node}\index{Purkinje fibres}
The beat originates in the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:heart:conduction}{sinoatrial node}}, a patch of
specialised muscle cells in the wall of the right \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{atrium} whose
membrane potential never rests: after each action potential it
drifts slowly upward (a \emph{pacemaker potential}, driven by a
sodium current that switches on at negative potentials and by
calcium channels) until it reaches threshold and fires again, about
a hundred times a minute if left alone. The impulse spreads through
the atrial muscle, cell to cell through the gap junctions, and the
atria contract; it reaches the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:heart:conduction}{atrioventricular node}}, the
only electrical bridge between atria and \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles}, which conducts
slowly and delays it by a tenth of a second --- time for the atria
to finish filling the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles}; then it races down the
\emph{bundle of His} and the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:heart:conduction}{Purkinje fibres}}, fast-conducting
cells that deliver it to the whole ventricular muscle within
\qty{30}{ms}, so that the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles} contract from the apex upward,
as a unit, and drive the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} toward the outflow valves. Every
part of this system can pace on its own, each more slowly than the
last (the node at 100, the AV node at 50, the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:conduction}{Purkinje fibres} at
30), so that if the node fails a lower centre takes over --- at a
slower rate.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
An isolated \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}, or a strip of sinoatrial tissue, beats in a dish
without nerves; a strip of \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} beats too, but more slowly.
Cooling or warming the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:conduction}{sinoatrial node} alone changes the rate of
the whole \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}; cutting the AV bundle dissociates the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles},
which then beat at their own slow rhythm while the atria continue
at the node's (\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} block). Recording from a single node cell
shows the slow diastolic depolarisation that no ordinary muscle
cell has; blocking the pacemaker current slows it.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The electrocardiogram]\label{prop:b2:heart:ecg}
\index{electrocardiogram}
The \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} is a large mass of cells depolarising and repolarising in
sequence, and the currents that flow in the body around it can be
recorded as voltages of a millivolt between electrodes on the
limbs: the \emph{electrocardiogram}. Its waves are the events of
the conduction system: the \emph{P wave} is atrial depolarisation;
the flat \emph{PR interval} (\qty{0.16}{s}) is the AV delay; the
\emph{QRS complex}, sharp and brief (\qty{0.08}{s}), is ventricular
depolarisation --- large because the ventricular mass is large, brief
because the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:conduction}{Purkinje fibres} are fast; the \emph{T wave} is
ventricular repolarisation. (Atrial repolarisation is buried in the
QRS.) The trace reports timing and conduction, not force: a blocked
AV node lengthens the PR interval or dissociates P from QRS, a dead
region of \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} distorts the QRS, an irregular \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{atrium} (atrial
fibrillation) replaces P waves with noise and makes the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles}
beat irregularly, and an interval between R waves gives the rate.
\end{proposition}

\section{Regulating the output}

\begin{theorem}[The Frank--Starling law]\label{thm:b2:heart:starling}
\index{Frank--Starling law}\index{preload}\index{cardiac output}
The force of a ventricular contraction rises with the volume that
filled it: within the working range, the more the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} is
stretched in diastole (the \emph{preload}), the more it ejects in
systole. The \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} therefore pumps out, beat by beat, whatever it
receives --- a rise in venous return of \qty{20}{\%} is met by a rise
in \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} of \qty{20}{\%} without any nerve or hormone --- and
the two \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles}, which must move the same volume, balance each
other automatically: if the right \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} delivers more \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} to the
lungs, the left \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} receives more and pumps more. The mechanism is
in the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomere}: at the short lengths of a poorly filled
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} the thick and thin filaments overlap too much and the
calcium sensitivity is low; stretching toward the optimal length
(\qty{2.2}{\micro m}) increases both the overlap available for
cross-bridges and the sensitivity, so that the same calcium pulse
produces more force. Beyond the optimum (an overstretched, failing
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}) the force falls again.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Frank (1895) recorded the pressure developed by a frog \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle}
filled to different volumes: the peak pressure rose with the
filling volume up to a maximum and then fell. Starling (1914), with
a dog heart--lung preparation in which venous return and arterial
resistance could be set independently, showed that raising the
return raised the output stroke by stroke, and that raising the
arterial resistance was met, after a few beats of accumulation, by
a larger end-diastolic volume and a restored output --- the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle}
answers a load by stretching and a stretch by contracting harder.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Nerves and hormones]\label{prop:b2:heart:control}
\index{sympathetic nervous system!heart}\index{vagus nerve}\index{adrenaline}\index{cardiac output}
The \omterm{thm:b2:heart:starling}{cardiac output} is \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} rate times \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume}, \qty{5}{L/min}
at rest and up to \qty{25}{L/min} in a trained athlete's exercise.
Both factors are set by the autonomic nerves. The \emph{vagus}
(parasympathetic), releasing acetylcholine onto the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:conduction}{sinoatrial node},
slows the pacemaker drift and holds the resting rate at 70 rather
than the node's own 100; cut the vagi and the rate rises. The
\emph{sympathetic} nerves, releasing noradrenaline, and the adrenal
medulla, releasing \emph{adrenaline} into the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}, speed the drift
(more beats), speed conduction, and raise the force of contraction
at any filling (a steeper Starling curve, the property called
\emph{contractility}), by raising the calcium delivered to the
\omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomeres} at each beat. In exercise the vagal brake is released
first, then the sympathetic accelerator pressed, and a rate of 180
with a \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} of \qty{140}{mL} gives \qty{25}{L/min}; the
signals are relayed through the receptors and second messengers of
\cref{ch:b2:cell-signalling}, and the whole is commanded by the
pressure-regulating centres of \cref{ch:b2:blood-pressure}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Reading a heart]\label{ex:b2:heart:reading}
Two sounds per beat: lub, the AV valves closing at the start of
systole; dub, the semilunar valves closing at its end. A murmur is
turbulent flow through a narrowed valve (a stenosis) or back
through a leaking one (an insufficiency): a murmur between lub and
dub is a leaking mitral valve or a narrowed aortic valve, after dub
a leaking aortic valve. A pulse of 70 with a \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} pressure of
$120/80$~mmHg means a \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} near \qty{70}{mL};
a rate of 40 in an athlete is a large \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} and a strong
vagal tone; a rate of 40 with a PR interval that lengthens until a
beat drops is a diseased AV node. The physiology of this chapter is
what a physician hears through a stethoscope in thirty seconds.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:1}
Trace a drop of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} from the vena cava to the aorta, naming each
chamber, valve and vessel in order.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:2}
Give the phases of the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{cardiac cycle} with the state of each valve
in each phase, and the event that causes each of the two \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}
sounds.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:3}
Name the parts of the conduction system in order, with the time the
impulse takes to reach each, and the ECG wave each produces.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:4}
State the \omterm{thm:b2:heart:starling}{Frank--Starling law} and say why it guarantees that the two
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles} pump the same volume.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:5}
A \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} has an end-diastolic volume of \qty{130}{mL} and an
end-systolic volume of \qty{50}{mL} at 70 beats a minute. Compute
the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume}, the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{ejection fraction} and the \omterm{thm:b2:heart:starling}{cardiac output}. A
failing \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} has \qty{180}{mL} and \qty{120}{mL}: recompute, and say
what has happened to the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{ejection fraction}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:6}
Compute the work of a beat for a \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} of \qty{70}{mL} at a
mean ejection pressure of \qty{100}{mmHg}, and the power at 72 beats
a minute. Recompute for a hypertensive at \qty{150}{mmHg}. How
much extra oxygen per minute does the second \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} need, if the
mechanical efficiency is \qty{15}{\%} and \qty{1}{mL} of oxygen
yields \qty{20}{J}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:7}
On an ECG the R waves are \qty{0.5}{s} apart in one recording and
\qty{1.5}{s} in another. Give the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} rates. In a third, P waves
come every \qty{0.6}{s} and QRS complexes every \qty{1.8}{s}, with no
fixed relation. What has happened?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:8}
The sinoatrial cell's potential drifts from \qty{-60}{mV} to a
threshold of \qty{-40}{mV} at \qty{25}{mV/s} before each action
potential of \qty{0.15}{s}. Compute the interval between beats and
the rate. Acetylcholine lowers the drift to \qty{18}{mV/s} and the
starting potential to \qty{-65}{mV}; noradrenaline raises the drift
to \qty{40}{mV/s}. Compute both rates.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:9}
Explain why the AV node's delay is necessary, what would happen to
the output without it, and why a slow AV node (a long PR interval)
is harmless up to a point and dangerous beyond it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:10}
An athlete at rest has a rate of 45 and an output of \qty{5}{L/min};
in exercise a rate of 180 and \qty{30}{L/min}. Compute the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke
volumes} and say what the Starling curve and the sympathetic nerves
each contribute. Why can the rate not usefully exceed about 200?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:11}
A narrowed aortic valve leaves an opening of \qty{1}{cm^2} instead
of \qty{3}{cm^2}. Using continuity, compute the velocity of the
ejected \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} at \qty{250}{mL/s} through each, and, from Bernoulli
($\Delta P = \tfrac12\rho v^2$), the pressure the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} must
generate above the aortic pressure in each case. What does the
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} do about it, and what is the eventual cost?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:heart:12}
``The \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} is a pump that regulates itself and is only adjusted by
the nervous system.'' Discuss, distinguishing what automaticity, the
\omterm{thm:b2:heart:starling}{Frank--Starling law} and the autonomic nerves each provide, and what
a transplanted \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} --- which has no nerves --- can and cannot do.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A Heart Under Load}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- one heart followed from rest to
exercise and into disease: its cycle timed, its work computed, its
electrocardiogram read, its Starling response and its autonomic
control quantified, and a narrowed valve's cost estimated, ending on
the output at rest and in exercise, the work per beat, and the
gradient across the stenosis}]
\label{pb:b2:heart:1}
Data: at rest, rate 70, end-diastolic volume \qty{130}{mL},
end-systolic \qty{60}{mL}, mean ejection pressure \qty{100}{mmHg}
($\qty{1}{mmHg} = \qty{133}{Pa}$), aortic pressure $120/80$~mmHg.
Systole lasts \qty{0.3}{s} at any rate. Pacemaker: drift from
\qty{-60}{mV} to \qty{-40}{mV}, action potential \qty{0.15}{s}. The
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} consumes \qty{20}{J} per millilitre of oxygen with a
mechanical efficiency of \qty{15}{\%}; coronary \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} carries
\qty{0.2}{mL} of oxygen per millilitre and the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} extracts
\qty{70}{\%} of it. \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{Blood} density \qty{1060}{kg/m^3}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The cycle at rest.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume}, \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{ejection fraction} and \omterm{thm:b2:heart:starling}{cardiac
        output}.
  \item Compute the duration of one cycle and of diastole.
  \item Compute the mean ejection rate during systole, in
        millilitres per second, and the mean velocity through an
        aortic valve of \qty{3}{cm^2}.
  \item The ventricular pressure rises at \qty{1500}{mmHg/s} during
        isovolumetric contraction, from 10 to \qty{80}{mmHg}. How
        long does that phase last?
  \item Compute the work of one beat and the mechanical power of the
        left \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle}.
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}'s total power and oxygen consumption
        (both \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles}: take the right as a fifth of the left).
  \item Compute the coronary \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} flow needed to supply that oxygen.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The pacemaker and the trace.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item What drift rate (\unit{mV/s}) gives a rate of 70?
  \item The vagus is cut: the rate becomes 100. What drift rate is
        that?
  \item Compute the drift rate for a rate of 180 in exercise.
  \item On the ECG at rest, give the R--R interval, and say what
        the P, QRS and T waves correspond to.
  \item The PR interval is \qty{0.16}{s} at rest. Its length is
        mostly the AV node's delay: explain what the delay achieves
        and why a rate of 180 requires the node to speed up too.
  \item A patient's ECG shows QRS complexes every \qty{1.5}{s} with P
        waves every \qty{0.8}{s}, unrelated. Diagnose, give the
        ventricular rate, and say which tissue is pacing the
        \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricles}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Exercise.}
In exercise the rate rises to 180 and the sympathetic nerves raise
contractility so that the end-systolic volume falls to
\qty{30}{mL}; venous return raises the end-diastolic volume to
\qty{150}{mL}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume}, \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{ejection fraction} and output.
  \item Compute the duration of diastole at 180 and explain why the
        rate cannot usefully go much higher.
  \item Compute the power of the left \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} at a mean ejection
        pressure of \qty{120}{mmHg}, and the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}'s oxygen
        consumption.
  \item Compute the coronary flow needed, and compare with rest.
        Why is the diastolic shortening a problem for it?
  \item Without the sympathetic nerves (a transplanted \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}) the
        rate rises only slowly, through adrenaline in the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}, and
        contractility less. Predict the output in the first minute
        of exercise, using the Starling law alone with the same
        filling.
  \item Attribute the rise of output from rest to exercise to its
        three causes (rate, filling, contractility), in litres per
        minute each.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- A narrowed valve.}
The aortic valve narrows to \qty{1}{cm^2}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the ejection velocity at rest through the narrowed
        valve.
  \item Using Bernoulli, $\Delta P = \tfrac12\rho v^{2}$, compute the
        pressure the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} must add to push \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} through it, in
        mmHg.
  \item Recompute both in exercise.
  \item What must the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle}'s peak pressure be in each case to
        keep the aortic pressure normal, and by what factor is its
        work per beat raised at rest?
  \item The \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle} wall thickens in response. Explain why this
        helps and why it eventually fails (think of the coronary
        supply and of diastolic filling).
  \item State the result: the output at rest and in exercise, the
        work per beat at rest, and the gradient across the narrowed
        valve at rest and in exercise.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
