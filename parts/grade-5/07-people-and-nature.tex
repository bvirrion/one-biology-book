\chapter{People and Nature}\label{ch:g5:people-and-nature}

From a hilltop, look down at the valley: fields in neat
squares, a managed forest, straightened riverbanks, roads,
roofs. Almost nothing in sight grew that way by itself. Humans
are the one \omterm{def:g5:biodiversity:species}{species} that reshapes whole landscapes to its
needs --- and this chapter looks honestly at that power: what
it gives us, what it costs, and how it can be used well.

\section{Landscapes made by people}

\begin{proposition}[Most landscapes are managed]\label{prop:g5:people-and-nature:managed}
Nearly every landscape around us is shaped by people:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{fields}\index{field} are managed ground where one
        chosen \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:plant}{plant} --- wheat, maize, potatoes --- is grown in
        place of the wild mixture;
  \item most \emph{forests}\index{forest (managed)} are
        planted, thinned and harvested like slow crops;
  \item \emph{meadows} exist because they are mowed or grazed
        --- abandoned, they grow back to scrub and then
        woodland;
  \item towns, roads and dams redraw the land itself.
\end{itemize}
``Untouched'' corners are the exception, and most are
protected on purpose.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Reading the valley]\label{ex:g5:people-and-nature:valley}
That hilltop view, item by item: the wheat field feeds the
bakery; the spruce rows are a timber crop older than their
forester; the flowery meadow is mowed each July --- skip two
summers and brambles take it; the river was straightened long
ago to protect the fields from floods. The valley is a
workshop --- one that has been running for centuries.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Neither villain nor garden gnome]\label{rem:g5:people-and-nature:honest}
It would be false to tell this story as pure destruction ---
farming feeds humanity, and hedged farmland can be rich in
\omterm{def:g5:biodiversity:species}{species} (\cref{ex:g5:biodiversity:orchard}) --- and just as
false to pretend reshaping costs nothing
(\cref{prop:g5:biodiversity:loss} listed the bills). The
honest question is never ``touch nature or not?'' but ``how
to manage what we touch so it keeps working --- for us and
for the rest of the web?''
\end{remark}

\section{Managing well and managing badly}

\begin{example}[Two ways to farm a hillside]\label{ex:g5:people-and-nature:twofarms}
Hillside A: one giant field, hedges grubbed out, soil bare
all winter. Rain washes the loosened soil downhill; the
helpers of \cref{ex:g5:biodiversity:orchard} have no home;
one pest can sweep the single crop unopposed. Hillside B:
smaller fields with hedges kept, soil covered through winter,
crops rotated year by year. The hedges hold the soil and
house the pest-eaters; the rotation rests the ground. Both
hillsides feed people --- one still will in a hundred years.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[The rules of lasting use]\label{prop:g5:people-and-nature:lasting}
Use of nature lasts when it respects three rules:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{take no faster than it renews}: harvest wood at
        the forest's growing pace, \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{fish} at the shoal's
        breeding pace --- the overfishing of
        \cref{prop:g5:biodiversity:loss} is this rule broken;
  \item \emph{keep the workers}: soil life, pollinators, pest
        eaters --- the free services of
        \cref{exo:g5:biodiversity:11} --- need their homes
        kept;
  \item \emph{return what you borrow}: the compost loop of
        \cref{rem:g4:what-plants-need:soil}, at every scale.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[The forester's arithmetic]\label{ex:g5:people-and-nature:forester}
A well-run forest is the oldest proof that rule 1 works:
foresters count what the forest grows each year, and cut no
more than that. The forest then yields timber every year,
forever --- while remaining a forest, with its owls, deer
and beetles. Some managed \omterm{prop:g5:people-and-nature:managed}{forests} in the world have been
harvested this way for many hundreds of years without
shrinking.
\end{example}

\section{Cities, and making room again}

\begin{example}[Nature in town]\label{ex:g5:people-and-nature:city}
A city looks like nature's opposite, yet it is a \omterm{def:g2:where-animals-live:habitat}{habitat} too
--- a strange rocky one. Swifts nest on ``cliffs'' of
concrete, foxes patrol the bins, wild \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:plant}{plants} split the
asphalt seams. And town-dwellers can widen the welcome:
parks, green roofs, balcony \omterm{def:g3:flowers-fruits-seeds:parts}{flowers} for the bees, the school
pond of \cref{ex:g2:where-animals-live:schoolpond}. The
methods of \cref{met:g5:biodiversity:help} work between
buildings too.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Giving a river its bends back]\label{ex:g5:people-and-nature:river}
The straightened river of the valley ran fast and flooded
badly downstream; its \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{fish} and dragonflies had gone with the
bends. Engineers now sometimes \emph{re-bend} such rivers:
restored curves and wet meadows slow the water, store the
floods, and the storks of \cref{ex:g5:biodiversity:storks}
find their meadows again. Management can run in reverse ---
repairing is managing too.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Judging a use of nature]\label{met:g5:people-and-nature:judge}
Given any use of land or water --- a farm, a fishery, a
planned road, a garden:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item ask what is \emph{taken}, and whether faster than it
        renews (rule 1);
  \item ask what \emph{homes and workers} are kept or lost
        (rule 2);
  \item ask what is \emph{returned} --- and what wastes are
        left (rule 3);
  \item then judge: could this go on for a hundred years?
        What one change would most improve the answer?
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:1}
Give four kinds of human-shaped landscape from
\cref{prop:g5:people-and-nature:managed}, each with what it
is managed for.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:2}
What happens to a meadow that stops being mowed or grazed?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:3}
State the three rules of lasting use.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:4}
In \cref{ex:g5:people-and-nature:twofarms}, list three
differences between the two hillsides, and what each
difference changes.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:5}
Explain the forester's arithmetic. Which rule is it, and
what does the forest yield in return --- for how long?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:6}
Name three wild inhabitants of cities, and what feature of
the city each uses as \omterm{def:g2:where-animals-live:habitat}{habitat}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:7}
What did re-bending the river restore --- for people, and
for the web?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:8}
Try it: pick one view near home --- street, field, park ---
and list everything in it that people manage, as in
\cref{ex:g5:people-and-nature:valley}. Anything left
unmanaged?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:9}
``Protecting nature means never touching it.'' Discuss using
the mowed meadow of
\cref{prop:g5:people-and-nature:managed} --- a \omterm{def:g2:where-animals-live:habitat}{habitat} that
\emph{disappears} without human touch --- and
\cref{rem:g5:people-and-nature:honest}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:10}
Run \cref{met:g5:people-and-nature:judge} on hillside A of
\cref{ex:g5:people-and-nature:twofarms}: which rules does
it break, and what one change would most improve it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:people-and-nature:11}
A fishing fleet doubles its catch for five splendid years;
in the sixth, the shoals are gone and the fleet is idle.
Explain the six years with rule 1 --- why did breaking it
\emph{look} like success at first? Compare with the
forester's opposite arithmetic.
\end{exercise}
