\chapter{Cellular Respiration and Fermentation}\label{ch:g12:respiration-fermentation}

A marathon runner's muscles spend, over three and a half hours,
about half a mole of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} every minute --- a quarter of a kilogram of
the molecule, made, used and remade thousands of times over from a
stock that would last five seconds. Every one of those \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} molecules is paid
for by a glucose or a fatty acid taken apart, atom by atom, and by an
oxygen molecule breathed in at the beginning and breathed out as water
at the end. \cref{ch:g10:cell-metabolism} gave the balance; this
chapter opens the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} and follows the glucose through the
three stages that turn its energy into \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} --- and shows what a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}
does when the oxygen runs out.

\section{ATP, the currency}

\begin{definition}[ATP]\label{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}
\emph{ATP}\index{ATP} (adenosine triphosphate) is a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide}
carrying three phosphate groups in a row. Removing the last one
releases about \qty{30}{kJ} per mole under \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} conditions, and every
energy-requiring process of the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} --- contraction, transport,
synthesis, the light of a firefly --- is driven by that removal.
The product, ADP, is recharged into ATP by the reactions of this
chapter. A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} holds a few seconds' worth of ATP and turns it over
continuously; a resting human recycles about its own body mass of ATP
in a day.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Where the energy is]\label{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:oxidation}
Glucose stores energy in its carbon--hydrogen bonds; releasing it
means transferring the hydrogen, with its electrons, to oxygen ---
oxidising the glucose to carbon dioxide and reducing the oxygen to
water. Done in one step, as in a flame, the energy would leave as
heat. The \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} does it in dozens of small steps, each run by an
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme}, and captures part of the energy at several of them: the
hydrogen is first loaded onto carrier molecules --- \emph{reduced
NAD} --- and only at the end handed to oxygen.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Three stages}

\begin{proposition}[Glycolysis]\label{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:glycolysis}
In the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cytoplasm}, without oxygen, a glucose (six carbons) is split
into two molecules of \emph{pyruvate} (three carbons each) by ten
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} steps: \emph{glycolysis}\index{glycolysis}. The balance: two
\omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} consumed to start, four produced, hence a net gain of 2 \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}, and
two reduced NAD loaded with hydrogen. The pyruvate still holds most of
the glucose's energy.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{proposition}[The mitochondrion completes the oxidation]\label{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:mitochondrion}
When oxygen is available, pyruvate enters the \emph{mitochondrion}\index{mitochondrion},
an \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{organelle} bounded by two membranes, the inner one folded into
\emph{cristae} that enclose the fluid \emph{matrix}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the matrix, each pyruvate is taken apart in a cycle of
        reactions, the \emph{Krebs cycle}\index{Krebs cycle}: its
        three carbons leave as three $\mathrm{CO_2}$, its hydrogen is
        loaded onto carriers (reduced NAD and a second carrier), and a
        little \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} is made directly --- one per pyruvate.
  \item In the inner membrane, the loaded carriers hand their
        electrons to a chain of \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins}, the \emph{respiratory
        chain}, that passes them down to oxygen, which combines with
        protons to form water. The energy released along the chain
        pumps protons across the membrane, and their return through a
        rotary \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} drives the synthesis of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} --- about 26 per
        glucose.
\end{itemize}
Total for one glucose: about 30 \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} (2 from \omterm{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:glycolysis}{glycolysis}, 2 from the
cycle, some 26 from the chain), six $\mathrm{CO_2}$ released, six
$\mathrm{O_2}$ consumed, and the rest of the \qty{2870}{kJ} as heat.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Isolated \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} in a sealed chamber with an oxygen probe
consume no oxygen when given glucose, but consume it rapidly when
given pyruvate --- the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} cannot start from glucose; the
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cytoplasm} must. Adding a poison of the respiratory chain (cyanide)
stops the consumption at once, whatever \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{substrate} is present. Adding
ADP speeds the consumption and its exhaustion slows it: oxygen use is
coupled to \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} synthesis. And the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} of a hummingbird's
flight muscle or a marathon runner's thigh have cristae packed
several times more densely than those of a resting tissue.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Reading an oxygen trace]\label{ex:g12:respiration-fermentation:trace}
Isolated \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} in the chamber: the oxygen stays at
\qty{8}{mg/L}; glucose is added, nothing; pyruvate is added, the
oxygen falls at \qty{0.5}{mg/L} per minute; ADP is added, the fall
steepens to \qty{1.5}{mg/L} per minute for a while, then returns to
\num{0.5}; cyanide is added, the fall stops. Four facts in one
trace: the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} needs pyruvate, not glucose; oxygen use is
coupled to \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} synthesis; the coupling is through the chain that
cyanide blocks; and a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} without work to do idles.
\end{example}

\section{Without oxygen: fermentation}

\begin{proposition}[Fermentation regenerates the carrier]\label{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:fermentation}
\omterm{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:glycolysis}{Glycolysis} loads two NAD carriers per glucose; with oxygen, the
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} unloads them. Without oxygen the carriers would all be
loaded within seconds and \omterm{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:glycolysis}{glycolysis} would stop. \emph{Fermentation}\index{fermentation}
is the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cytoplasm}'s way of unloading them: the pyruvate itself accepts
the hydrogen and becomes either \emph{lactate} (muscle \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, milk
bacteria) or, after losing a $\mathrm{CO_2}$, \emph{ethanol} (yeast).
No further \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} is made; the yield is the 2 \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} of \omterm{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:glycolysis}{glycolysis}, some
fifteen times less than respiration's, and the product --- lactate or
ethanol --- still holds most of the glucose's energy.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[The muscle's choice]\label{ex:g12:respiration-fermentation:muscle}
A sprinter's muscle fibre needs \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} faster than its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} and
its oxygen supply can make it; \omterm{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:glycolysis}{glycolysis} with lactic \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation}
delivers \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} three times faster, at fifteen times the glucose cost,
and the lactate accumulates until the pain stops the effort. A
marathon runner's fibres run on respiration, slowly and almost
indefinitely, burning fat as well as glucose; her \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} are
larger and more numerous, her capillaries denser, and her lactate
stays low. The fibre types of \cref{ch:g10:muscles-and-joints} are
these two strategies built into \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Balancing an energy budget]\label{met:g12:respiration-fermentation:budget}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Convert the power needed into \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}: at about \qty{30}{kJ} per
        mole of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} usable, \qty{1}{kW} of metabolic power is
        \qty{2}{mol} of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} per minute.
  \item Convert \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} into fuel: 30 \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} per glucose in respiration, 2 in
        \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation}.
  \item Convert fuel into oxygen: 6 $\mathrm{O_2}$ per glucose
        respired, \qty{24}{L} per mole of gas.
  \item Compare the oxygen needed with what the blood delivers
        (\cref{ch:g10:heart-lungs-effort}): the shortfall is met by
        \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation}, with its lactate.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[The mirror of photosynthesis]\label{rem:g12:respiration-fermentation:mirror}
\omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{Photosynthesis} loads electrons from water onto carriers with light and
uses them to reduce carbon dioxide into sugar, releasing oxygen;
respiration unloads electrons from sugar onto carriers and passes
them to oxygen, making water and releasing carbon dioxide, and uses
the energy to make \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}. The two are run by \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{organelles} that were both
once free bacteria (\cref{ch:g12:diversification-of-life}), the
\omterm{def:g12:photosynthesis:chloroplast}{chloroplast} supplying what the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion} consumes; between them
they turn sunlight into the currency of every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, and the
atmosphere's oxygen is the balance of their accounts.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Half a Mole of ATP a Minute}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a runner's energy followed from
the watt to the molecule: ATP counted, glucose and oxygen reckoned,
the mitochondria's share measured, and the sprint that the chain
cannot pay for}]
\label{pb:g12:respiration-fermentation:1}
A runner sustains a metabolic power of \qty{1000}{W}, of which about
a third is captured as \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}, the rest leaving as heat. Take
\qty{30}{kJ} of usable energy per mole of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}, 30 \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} per glucose in
respiration and 2 in \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation}, \qty{2870}{kJ} per mole of glucose,
\qty{24}{L} per mole of gas, and glucose \qty{180}{g/mol}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many moles of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} does the runner use per minute?
        (Only the third captured as \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} counts.)
  \item What mass of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} is that, at \qty{507}{g/mol}? Compare with the
        few grams the muscles hold at any moment.
  \item How many times per minute is each \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} molecule recharged, if
        the body's stock is \qty{50}{g}?
  \item If that \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} were made by respiration alone, how many moles of
        glucose per minute would be consumed?
  \item How many litres of oxygen per minute does that require?
        Compare with a $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max of \qty{4}{L/min}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria}'s share.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Of the 30 \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} per glucose, how many are made in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cytoplasm}
        and how many in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondrion}? What fraction of the
        runner's \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} is mitochondrial?
  \item How many moles of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ does she exhale per minute,
        and from which stage of respiration do they come?
  \item How much of the \qty{2870}{kJ} of a glucose ends in \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}? Where
        does the rest go, and what does the runner do about it?
  \item Her muscle \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} have a total inner-membrane area of
        some \qty{1000}{m^2}. Why does the chain need so much surface?
  \item \omterm{def:g10:sport-and-health:training}{Training} doubles the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} of her fibres. Which of the
        quantities above does that change, and which does it not?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The sprint.}
In the final sprint her muscles need \qty{800}{W} of power as \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} for
30 seconds, while respiration, limited by the oxygen the blood
delivers, can supply at most \qty{400}{W} as \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many moles of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} does the sprint require in total?
  \item How much of it can respiration supply in 30 seconds, and how
        much must come from \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation}?
  \item How many moles of glucose does the fermented part consume, and
        how many moles of lactate does it leave?
  \item Compare the glucose used per \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} in the two routes during the
        sprint. Why does the muscle accept the waste?
  \item After the race, the lactate is oxidised or turned back into
        glucose. Explain why her oxygen consumption stays high for
        several minutes after she stops.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The other fuel.}
Fat supplies about \qty{38}{kJ} per gram and, per gram, needs about 20\%
more oxygen than glucose for the same energy.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item If half her \qty{1000}{W} came from fat, what mass of fat would
        she burn per hour?
  \item Why does fat, though richer in energy per gram, give less
        power per litre of oxygen?
  \item Fatty acids enter respiration at the \omterm{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:mitochondrion}{Krebs cycle}, bypassing
        \omterm{prop:g12:respiration-fermentation:glycolysis}{glycolysis}. Which of the three stages, then, can fat not use,
        and what follows for a sprint?
  \item Why does the brain, which runs on glucose alone, keep working
        during the race although the muscles are taking most of the
        blood's glucose?
  \item State the result: the moles of \omterm{def:g12:respiration-fermentation:atp}{ATP} the runner uses per minute,
        the litres of oxygen that pays for them, and the stage of
        respiration that makes most of them.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
