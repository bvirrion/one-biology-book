\chapter{Reading Kinship: Phylogenetic Trees}\label{ch:g12:phylogenetic-trees}

In 1837, on a page of a private notebook, Darwin sketched a branching
line with a few twigs and wrote above it two words: "I think". The
sketch proposed that \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} are related as the twigs of a tree are ---
by descent from common branches --- and that their kinship could be
reconstructed. Today the sketch is a standard tool: a \omterm{def:g12:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{phylogenetic
tree}, built from shared characters and from \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, read according to
rules. This chapter teaches those rules: how to build a tree from a
table of characters, how to read one without misreading it, and how
molecules turn it into a clock.

\section{What a tree says}

\begin{definition}[Phylogenetic tree]\label{def:g12:phylogenetic-trees:tree}
A \emph{phylogenetic tree}\index{phylogenetic tree} is a diagram of the
kinship of a set of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} (or other groups). Its tips are the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
compared; each \emph{node} where two branches meet represents their
most recent common ancestor --- a hypothetical \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}, not a known
fossil; the \emph{root} is the common ancestor of all. Two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} are
closer kin than a third when their common ancestor (their node) is more
recent than the node they share with the third. Kinship is read from
the nodes, never from the left-to-right position of the tips.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Rules of reading]\label{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A tree can be rotated at any node without changing its
        meaning: the order of the tips is arbitrary.
  \item A node has no name and is not one of the tips: a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} is
        never the ancestor of another \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} on the tree; two tips
        are \emph{sister groups}, descended from a shared ancestor.
  \item A group made of an ancestor and \emph{all} its descendants ---
        everything beyond one node --- is a \emph{clade}\index{clade},
        the only kind of group a phylogenetic classification names.
  \item Branch lengths carry information only if the tree says so
        (time, or number of changes); otherwise only the branching
        order counts.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Misreadings to avoid]\label{ex:g12:phylogenetic-trees:misreadings}
"Humans descend from chimpanzees": no --- on the tree, humans and
chimpanzees are sister tips, descended from a common ancestor that was
neither. "The frog is more primitive": no --- every tip is a present
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} with the same length of history since the root; the frog
lineage has evolved for as long as ours. "The lizard is closer to the
frog than to the mouse" (from tree A): no --- lizard and mouse share
a more recent node.
\end{example}

\section{Building a tree from characters}

\begin{definition}[Ancestral and derived states]\label{def:g12:phylogenetic-trees:states}
A \emph{character} is a feature that varies among the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} compared
(the presence of a vertebral column, of four limbs, of an amniotic egg,
of hair). Each character has an \emph{ancestral} state --- the one the
common ancestor of the group had --- and one or more \emph{derived}
states that appeared later in some lineage. Only \emph{shared derived
states} indicate kinship: two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} sharing a derived state inherited
it from the ancestor in which it arose. A shared ancestral state says
nothing, since everyone's ancestor had it.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[From a character matrix to a tree]\label{met:g12:phylogenetic-trees:build}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Make a table: \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} in rows, characters in columns, 1 for the
        derived state and 0 for the ancestral one. Decide which state is
        ancestral by comparing with an \emph{outgroup}, a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} known
        to be outside the group studied.
  \item Find the derived state shared by the most \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}: it defines
        the first, largest \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}. Within it, find the next most widely
        shared derived state, and so on.
  \item Draw the nested \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clades} as a tree: each shared derived state
        marks one node, and the character is written on the branch
        where it appeared.
  \item Check for conflicts: a character shared by \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} that other
        characters place apart either arose twice (convergence) or was
        lost once. Choose the tree that requires the fewest changes in
        all --- the most \emph{parsimonious} tree.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[A conflict resolved by parsimony]\label{ex:g12:phylogenetic-trees:parsimony}
Add "wings" to a matrix with bat, pigeon and mouse. Wings would group
bat with pigeon; hair and milk group bat with mouse. A tree with bat
and mouse together needs wings to have arisen twice (two changes); a
tree with bat and pigeon together needs hair, milk, the mammalian jaw
and a dozen other characters to have arisen twice each. The first tree
is far more parsimonious: wings are a convergence, the mammalian
characters a common inheritance. Parsimony does not prove the tree; it
chooses the least improbable one.
\end{example}

\section{Trees from molecules}

\begin{proposition}[Molecular phylogeny]\label{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:molecular}
A \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} or \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} shared by all the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} compared is a character
matrix in itself: each position of the sequence is a character, and
each \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide} or \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid} a state. Aligning the sequences and
counting the differences between every pair gives a distance matrix,
from which a tree is built by joining the closest pairs first. The
molecular tree, computed from sequences that owe nothing to anatomy,
matches the anatomical tree in the great majority of cases --- the
strongest test of both.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
\cref{ch:g10:common-ancestry} gave the cytochrome c distances of the
\omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates}, which reproduce the boxes drawn from their skeletons.
Among the primates, sequences of dozens of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} agree: human and
chimpanzee differ by about 1.2\% of their \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, either from the gorilla
by 1.6\%, the three from the orangutan by 3\%, and the great apes from
the macaque by 7\%. A tree built from any one of these \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} has the
same shape; the few exceptions concern the most recent nodes, where
the differences are smallest.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The molecular clock]\label{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:clock}
For a given \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, differences between two lineages accumulate at a
roughly constant rate over long periods, since most substitutions are
neutral and fix by drift at the rate at which they arise. The number
of differences between two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} is therefore proportional to the
time since their common ancestor. Calibrated on one node dated by
fossils, the clock dates the others --- with the caution that rates
differ between \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} and between lineages, and that the calibration
carries its own uncertainty.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Dating a node]\label{ex:g12:phylogenetic-trees:dating}
Two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} differ by 4.5\% of their \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}; at 0.27\% per million years,
their common ancestor lived some 17 million years ago. The estimate
assumes the primate rate applies; for a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} evolving twice as fast,
or in a lineage with shorter generations, the same 4.5\% would mean
half the time. A clock is only as good as its calibration.
\end{example}

\section{Classification, from trees}

\begin{proposition}[Classifying by descent]\label{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:classification}
A phylogenetic classification names only \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clades}: groups defined by a
node and containing all its descendants. Familiar groups that fail
this test are not used: "fish" excludes the tetrapods that descend
from the same node; "reptiles" excludes the birds that descend from
within them; "invertebrates" is everything except a \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}. The
consequences are worth stating plainly: birds are dinosaurs, the
lungfish is closer to us than to the trout, and humans are one branch
of the apes, which are one branch of the primates, the mammals, the
amniotes, the tetrapods, the \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{remark}[What a tree is not]\label{rem:g12:phylogenetic-trees:not}
A tree is a hypothesis about history, built from present \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} and
tested by every new character and every new sequence; it is redrawn
when they disagree, and the redrawing is the work of the field. It
has no top and no direction of progress: humans sit at one tip among
millions, no higher and no more finished than the lamprey at another.
And it has no ancestor at any tip: the ancestors are the nodes,
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} gone, whose existence the tree infers and whose fossils,
when they are found, sit beside the branches rather than on them.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Six Species and a Clock}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]

  \item Date the crocodile--pigeon node and the amniote node

  \item Date the salmon node and the shark node.
  \item Fossils give about 250 million years for the crocodile--bird
        split and 310 for the amniote split. Compare with your dates

  \item The shark distances (30--31\%) hardly exceed the salmon's
