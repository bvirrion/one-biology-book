\chapter{The Stretch Reflex and the Nerve Message}\label{ch:g12:stretch-reflex}

A doctor taps the \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{tendon} just below your kneecap with a small rubber
hammer, and your foot kicks forward before you have felt anything.
Thirty milliseconds separate the tap from the kick: too little for the
brain, too little even for a decision. The signal went from the muscle
to the spinal cord and back --- a circuit of two \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} and one
connection between them --- and the same circuit is what holds you
upright, silently, every second you stand. This chapter uses it to
introduce the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, the electrical signal it carries, and the
chemical junction where one \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} speaks to the next.

\section{The reflex arc}

\begin{definition}[Reflex]\label{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}
A \emph{reflex}\index{reflex} is an involuntary, rapid and
stereotyped response of an effector (a muscle or a gland) to a
stimulus, produced by a fixed circuit of \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons}, the
\emph{reflex arc}\index{reflex arc}, that runs through the spinal
cord or brainstem without requiring the brain. The
\emph{stretch reflex}\index{stretch reflex} is the simplest: the
sudden stretching of a muscle makes that same muscle contract.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The circuit of the stretch reflex]\label{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:arc}
Five elements, in order:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the \emph{receptor}: \emph{muscle spindles}, small sensory
        organs lying among the \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{muscle fibres}, which are stretched
        when the muscle is and respond by firing signals;
  \item the \emph{sensory \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}}, whose fibre runs from the spindle
        to the spinal cord, entering by the dorsal root; its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} body
        sits in a ganglion beside the cord;
  \item the \emph{integration centre}: the grey matter of the spinal
        cord, where the sensory fibre connects directly --- through a
        single \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse} --- to the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} of the same muscle, and,
        through an intermediate \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, inhibits the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} of
        the antagonist muscle;
  \item the \emph{motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}}, whose fibre leaves by the ventral
        root and runs to the muscle;
  \item the \emph{effector}: the \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{muscle fibres}, which contract.
\end{enumerate}
A tap on the patellar \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{tendon} stretches the thigh muscle; its
spindles fire; the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neurons} of that muscle fire; it contracts and
the leg extends, while the hamstrings are told to relax.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Cutting the dorsal root abolishes the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} while the muscle can
still be made to contract by stimulating the ventral root; cutting the
ventral root abolishes it while the sensory fibres still carry signals
when the muscle is stretched: the arc has a sensory entrance and a
motor exit. The \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} persists in an animal whose spinal cord has
been severed from the brain: the brain is not needed. Recording from
single fibres shows the spindle's signals starting within a
millisecond of the stretch, and the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} firing about one
millisecond after the sensory signal arrives --- the delay of one
\omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[What the reflex is for]\label{ex:g12:stretch-reflex:posture}
Standing, you sway; each sway stretches the muscles of one side of the
ankle, whose spindles fire and whose \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} contraction pulls you back
before you notice. Carrying a tray, a sudden extra weight stretches the
arm muscles and the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} stiffens them within \qty{30}{ms}, before
the tray tips. The \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{stretch reflex} is a servomechanism: it opposes any
change of a muscle's length, and the brain adjusts its sensitivity to
set the body's tone --- higher when you brace, lower when you sleep.
\end{example}

\section{The neuron and its message}

\begin{definition}[Neuron]\label{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}
A \emph{neuron}\index{neuron} is a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} specialised for signalling: a
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} body with its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus}, branching \emph{dendrites} that receive
signals, and a single long fibre, the \emph{axon}, that carries the
neuron's own signal to its endings --- for a motor neuron of the leg,
a metre away. Many axons are wrapped in a fatty sheath, \emph{myelin},
laid down by companion \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, which speeds conduction. A nerve is a
bundle of hundreds or thousands of axons.
\end{definition}

branching dendrites that receive signals, and the single axon leaving

\begin{proposition}[The action potential]\label{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}
A \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}'s membrane holds an electrical voltage: the inside is about
\qty{-70}{mV} relative to the outside at rest. The
\emph{nerve message}\index{nerve message} is a train of
\emph{action potentials}\index{action potential}: brief reversals of
that voltage, each rising to about \qty{+30}{mV} and returning within
a millisecond, that travel along the axon without weakening. An \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action
potential} is \emph{all or none}: a stimulus below a threshold produces
nothing, any stimulus above it produces the same full-sized signal.
The intensity of a stimulus is therefore coded not in the size of the
signals but in their \emph{frequency}: a stronger stretch makes the
spindle fire more \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials} per second.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Microelectrodes inserted into an axon record the resting voltage and,
when the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} fires, identical spikes whose shape does not change with
the stimulus or with the distance travelled. Recordings from a single
spindle fibre during increasing stretches show spikes of constant
size at rates rising from a few to several hundred per second. A
stimulus just below threshold gives no spike at all; doubling a
stimulus already above threshold changes the rate, never the height.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Speed]\label{ex:g12:stretch-reflex:speed}
\omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{Action potentials} travel at \qty{1}{m/s} in thin unmyelinated fibres
and up to \qty{100}{m/s} in the thick myelinated fibres of the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{stretch
reflex}. From the knee to the spinal cord and back is about
\qty{1.5}{m}: some \qty{15}{ms} of travel, to which the spindle's
response, one \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse} and the muscle's activation add another
\qty{15}{ms} --- the \qty{30}{ms} of the knee jerk. A message from a
toe to the brain, \qty{1.8}{m} away, takes about \qty{20}{ms}; the
decision to move it, and its execution, some \qty{150}{ms} more.
\end{example}

\section{The synapse}

\begin{definition}[Synapse and neurotransmitter]\label{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}
A \emph{synapse}\index{synapse} is the junction where the ending of
one \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}'s axon meets the next \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} --- a \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} or a muscle fibre
--- across a gap of some \qty{20}{nm}, the \emph{synaptic cleft}. The
signal does not jump the gap electrically: the arriving \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action
potentials} make the ending release, from small vesicles, a chemical
messenger, the \emph{neurotransmitter}\index{neurotransmitter}, into
the cleft; it binds \emph{receptors} on the receiving \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s membrane,
which change that \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s voltage; it is then destroyed or taken back
within milliseconds. The synapse converts an electrical message into
a chemical one and back, and the amount of transmitter released ---
set by the frequency of the arriving \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials} --- codes the
message's intensity.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Excitation, inhibition, and the muscle's junction]\label{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:junction}
Depending on the transmitter and its receptor, a \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse}
\emph{excites} the receiving \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} (brings it towards its threshold)
or \emph{inhibits} it (drives it away). The \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{stretch reflex} uses both:
excitation of the muscle's own motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, inhibition of the
antagonist's through the interneuron. At the \emph{neuromuscular
junction}, the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse} between a motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} and a muscle fibre, the
transmitter is \emph{acetylcholine}: each \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potential} of the
motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} releases enough to fire the fibre, which contracts. A
motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} and the fibres it commands form a \emph{motor unit}; the
force of a muscle is set by how many units fire and how fast.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Acetylcholine applied to a muscle fibre makes it contract;
\emph{curare}, the arrow poison, binds the fibre's acetylcholine
receptors without activating them and paralyses every muscle while
the nerves keep firing; the toxin of botulism blocks the release of
the vesicles and paralyses likewise; an insecticide that blocks the
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} destroying acetylcholine makes the muscles contract
uncontrollably. Strychnine blocks the inhibitory \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapses} of the
spinal cord, so that every stretch triggers the contraction of both
muscle and antagonist: convulsions. Each poison names a step of the
junction by removing it.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Analysing a reflex or a poison]\label{met:g12:stretch-reflex:analyse}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Trace the arc: receptor, sensory \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, centre, motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron},
        effector; identify which element a lesion or a drug affects.
  \item At each \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}, ask what is coded --- the frequency of \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action
        potentials} --- and at each \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse}, what carries the message
        --- the transmitter and its amount.
  \item For a poison, find its step: release of the transmitter, the
        receptor, the destruction of the transmitter, or an inhibitory
        \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse}; predict paralysis (nothing gets through) or
        convulsion (nothing is stopped).
  \item Time it: distance divided by conduction speed, plus about a
        millisecond per \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[A message made of the same spikes]\label{rem:g12:stretch-reflex:spikes}
Every message in the nervous system --- the spindle's stretch, the
retina's light, the brain's command to move --- is made of the same
all-or-none \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials}, differing only in rate and in the
wires that carry them; meaning lies in which \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} fires, not in what
it fires. And at every \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse} the message becomes a dose of a
chemical, which is why a molecule --- a transmitter, a poison, a drug
--- can enter the conversation at any point. The final chapter follows
the same spikes and \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapses} up into the brain, and the command that
comes back down.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Thirty Milliseconds}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the knee jerk timed and taken
apart: the spindle's code, the fibres' speed, the synapse's delay, the
junction's chemistry, and the poisons that name each step}]
\label{pb:g12:stretch-reflex:1}
Electrodes record the electrical activity of a subject's thigh muscle
(an electromyogram) while the patellar \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{tendon} is tapped. The muscle's
signal begins \qty{30}{ms} after the tap; the leg moves \qty{20}{ms}
later. The distance from the \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{tendon} to the spinal cord along the
nerve is \qty{75}{cm}, the same back.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The timing.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the total length of the nerve path of the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex}.
  \item Take the spindle's response and the muscle's activation to
        need \qty{4}{ms} each, and the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse} \qty{1}{ms}. How much
        of the \qty{30}{ms} is conduction, and what is the conduction
        speed?
  \item In a subject \qty{20}{cm} taller, with the same fibres,
        predict the latency.
  \item Why does the leg move \qty{20}{ms} after the muscle's
        electrical signal begins?
  \item A subject is told to expect the tap and to "think about"
        stopping the kick; the kick is unchanged. Explain with the
        timing of a message to and from the brain (\qty{1.2}{m} more of
        fibres, and several \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapses}).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The code.}
The spindle fibre fires 20 \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials} per second at rest, 200
during the tap.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials} does the spindle send in the
        \qty{10}{ms} following the tap? What changes between rest and
        tap, and what does not?
  \item Each \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potential} arriving at the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:synapse}{synapse} releases a fixed
        quantity of transmitter. By what factor does the transmitter
        released per second rise during the tap?
  \item The motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron} fires only if enough transmitter arrives
        within a few milliseconds. Explain why the resting rate does
        not make the muscle contract, and the tap rate does.
  \item A gentler tap gives 100 per second. Predict the motor \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:neuron}{neuron}'s
        response and the strength of the kick, using motor units.
  \item Explain why a spindle's message could not be coded in the size
        of its \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The junction.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Name the transmitter at the neuromuscular junction and describe
        its fate after acting.
  \item Curare is injected: the electromyogram shows no signal after
        the tap, but the nerve still carries \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials}. Which
        step is blocked?
  \item Botulinum toxin, instead: same electromyogram, same nerve
        signal. Which step, and how would you tell the two apart in
        the laboratory?
  \item An anaesthetic blocks \omterm{prop:g12:stretch-reflex:actionpotential}{action potentials} in the sensory
        fibres. Which recordings disappear, and which remain?
  \item Explain why all three produce a limp leg but by three
        different mechanisms.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The centre.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item During the kick the hamstrings' electromyogram is silent, and
        drops below its resting level. Explain with the interneuron.
  \item A patient with a spinal cord severed above the level of the
        \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} still has a knee jerk --- exaggerated. What does the
        presence of the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} show, and what does its exaggeration
        suggest about the brain's normal role?
  \item In a patient whose \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} is absent on one side, list the
        elements of the arc that could be damaged, and one test to
        locate the damage.
  \item Why do doctors test this \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex} routinely, in a few seconds,
        on every patient?
  \item State the result: the conduction speed of the \omterm{def:g12:stretch-reflex:reflex}{reflex}'s fibres,
        the quantity that codes the strength of the stretch, and the
        one chemical step where curare acts.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
