\chapter{Muscle Contraction and Motor Function}\label{ch:b2:muscle-movement}

A sprinter leaves the blocks with a force on the track of three
times her weight, developed in a fifth of a second by muscles that
were slack a moment before. Nothing in the cell got shorter to do
it: the filaments inside slid past each other, pulled by millions
of molecular hands each taking a step of ten nanometres and letting
go, ten times a second, paid for one ATP at a time. This chapter
takes the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{muscle fibre} built in \cref{ch:b2:cell-differentiation}
and makes it work: the sliding of the filaments and the motor that
drives them, the calcium switch that turns the motor on and off,
the command from the nerve, the mechanics of force and speed, and
the way the nervous system grades the whole from a \omterm{def:b2:muscle-movement:motorunit}{twitch} to a
sprint.

\section{The sliding filaments}

\begin{proposition}[The sliding filament mechanism]\label{prop:b2:muscle-movement:sliding}
\index{sliding filament}\index{sarcomere!contraction}
When a \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{muscle fibre} shortens, its \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomeres} shorten
(\cref{ch:b2:cell-differentiation}), but neither the thick filaments
of myosin nor the thin filaments of actin change length: the thin
filaments slide inward along the thick ones, the I band and the H
zone narrow, and the Z discs approach. The overlap between the two
sets of filaments is where force is generated, and a \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomere}
generates force in proportion to the overlap: from a stretched
length of \qty{3.6}{\micro m}, with no overlap and no force, the
force rises linearly as the filaments overlap more, reaches a
plateau near \qty{2.2}{\micro m} where every myosin head faces actin,
and falls again below \qty{2.0}{\micro m} as the thin filaments
collide and the thick ones hit the Z discs. The resting length of a
muscle in the body sits on the plateau --- which is the first hint
of how the force is made.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
A.~F.~Huxley and Niedergerke, and H.~E.~Huxley and Hanson (1954),
measured the bands of single fibres and of isolated \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{myofibrils} by
interference and phase microscopy as they shortened: the A band kept
its length, the I band shrank, so the filaments slide. Gordon,
Huxley and Julian (1966) held a single fibre at set lengths with a
feedback device that kept one segment's \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomere} \omterm{thm:b2:neurons-synapses:cable}{length constant},
and found the force to be proportional to the overlap of thick and
thin filaments measured by electron microscopy --- the length--tension
curve with its plateau and two linear limbs, exactly as the geometry
of the filaments predicts.
\end{proof}

\section{The motor}

\begin{proposition}[The cross-bridge cycle]\label{prop:b2:muscle-movement:crossbridge}
\index{cross-bridge cycle}\index{myosin}\index{ATP!in contraction}
Each thick filament bristles with some three hundred \emph{myosin
heads}, and each head is a motor that walks along actin in a cycle
of four steps. (1) With ATP bound, the head is detached from actin;
it hydrolyses the ATP to ADP and phosphate and cocks itself into a
strained, high-energy shape. (2) The cocked head binds an actin
subunit, forming a \emph{cross-bridge}. (3) Phosphate is released
and the head snaps to its relaxed shape, pulling the thin filament
some \qty{10}{nm} toward the centre of the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomere} --- the
\emph{power stroke}; ADP leaves. (4) A new ATP binds and the head
lets go of actin; without ATP it cannot let go, which is the rigor
of death. The cycle takes about a twentieth of a second; a head
spends most of it detached, so that at any instant only a fraction
of the heads pull, and a filament sliding at speed is never dropped.
Force is the sum of the pulls of the attached heads; shortening is
their steps added up, half a micrometre per \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomere} per cycle at
most; and the energy is one ATP per step per head, about
\qty{50}{kJ/mol} --- \qty{8e-20}{J} per step, of which the head
converts up to half into work.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Force, speed and power of a muscle]\label{thm:b2:muscle-movement:hill}
\index{force--velocity curve}\index{muscle power}
A muscle's force falls as it shortens faster, because the faster
the filaments slide, the fewer heads are attached at any instant
and the more of them are dragged past their stroke before they let
go. Hill's relation describes it:
\[
(F + a)(v + b) = (F_0 + a)\,b,
\]
where $F_0$ is the isometric force (at $v = 0$), $v_{\max} =
F_0 b/a$ the speed at no load, and $a$, $b$ constants ($a \approx
F_0/4$). The power $Fv$ is zero at both ends and greatest at about a
third of $v_{\max}$ and a third of $F_0$ --- which is why a cyclist
changes gear and a sprinter's stride has a preferred rate. A human
muscle develops about \qty{30}{N} per square centimetre of
cross-section, shortens at up to ten lengths a second, and delivers
some \qty{100}{W} per kilogram at its best.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Hill's curve is empirical (1938), measured on isolated frog muscle
as the shortening speed against the load it lifts; the hyperbola
fits because the fraction of attached heads falls with sliding speed
and each attached head's force falls as it is carried through its
stroke. Power: with $F = (F_0 + a)b/(v + b) - a$, $Fv$ is zero at
$v = 0$ and at $v_{\max}$; differentiating gives the maximum at
$v = b(\sqrt{(F_0 + a)/a} - 1)$, which for $a = F_0/4$ is $v =
1.24\,b = 0.31\,v_{\max}$, where $F = 0.31\,F_0$.
\end{proof}

\section{The switch: calcium}

\begin{proposition}[Excitation--contraction coupling]\label{prop:b2:muscle-movement:coupling}
\index{excitation--contraction coupling}\index{troponin}\index{tropomyosin}\index{sarcoplasmic reticulum}
At rest the myosin heads cannot reach actin: the binding sites on
the thin filament are covered by \emph{tropomyosin}, a rod lying
in the groove of the actin helix, held there by \emph{troponin}.
The switch is calcium. An \omterm{prop:b2:neurons-synapses:ap}{action potential} on the fibre's membrane
runs down the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{T tubules} into the interior and, at the junctions
where a \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{T tubule} meets the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcoplasmic reticulum}, opens the
reticulum's calcium release channels; calcium floods the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{myofibrils},
rising from \qty{0.1}{} to \qty{10}{\micro mol/L} in a millisecond,
binds troponin, and troponin shifts tropomyosin off the binding
sites: the heads attach, and the fibre contracts as long as the
calcium stays. Pumps in the reticulum, burning ATP, take the
calcium back within tens of milliseconds; tropomyosin re-covers the
sites and the fibre relaxes. Relaxation is thus as active as
contraction, and a muscle deprived of ATP can neither release its
heads nor pump its calcium --- rigor mortis. In the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} the same
switch is used, but with calcium entering from outside through the
membrane's own channels to trigger the release, which is why the
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}, and not skeletal muscle, is sensitive to the calcium in the
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}.
\end{proposition}

\section{Command and gradation}

\begin{definition}[The motor unit]\label{def:b2:muscle-movement:motorunit}
\index{motor unit}\index{twitch}\index{tetanus}\index{recruitment}
A \emph{motor \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neuron}} in the spinal cord sends its \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{axon} to a
muscle and branches to innervate from a handful to a thousand
fibres, scattered through the muscle; the \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neuron} and its fibres are
a \emph{motor unit}, the smallest quantity of force the nervous
system can command. Every impulse in the \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neuron} makes every fibre
of the unit fire once and give a \emph{twitch}: a force that rises
in some \qty{30}{ms} and decays in \qty{100}{ms}, because the
calcium pulse is brief. Impulses in quick succession make twitches
sum, since the calcium of one has not been pumped away when the
next arrives; at some 30 to 50 impulses a second the force fuses
into a smooth \emph{tetanus}, three to five times the twitch. The
nervous system grades the force of a muscle in two ways: by the
\emph{rate} at which each unit fires, and by the \emph{number} of
units it recruits --- small, slow, fatigue-resistant units first
(the \emph{size principle}), large fast ones last, only for the
greatest efforts. A muscle in ordinary use is therefore never fully
on: a fraction of its units fire, in rotation, and the rest rest.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Reflexes and the spinal cord]\label{prop:b2:muscle-movement:reflex}
\index{reflex arc}\index{muscle spindle}\index{stretch reflex}\index{reciprocal inhibition}
The motor \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neuron} is the \emph{final common path}: every command,
from the cortex or from a reflex, ends on it. The simplest circuit
is the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:reflex}{stretch reflex}} of \cref{ch:b2:neurons-synapses}:
sensory endings wound round special fibres inside the muscle, the
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:reflex}{muscle spindles}}, fire when the muscle is stretched, excite
the motor \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neurons} of the same muscle through one \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:synapse}{synapse}, and
inhibit those of the antagonist through an interneuron
(\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:reflex}{reciprocal inhibition}}), so that the muscle resists the
stretch --- the basis of posture, since every sway is a stretch of
some muscle. A second receptor, the tendon organ, fires when the
muscle's force is high and inhibits its own motor \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neurons}: a safety
valve. Withdrawal from a painful stimulus is a reflex of several
\omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:synapse}{synapses} that flexes the limb and, on the other side, extends the
other one so that you do not fall. And the brain does not command
muscles but movements: the cortex sends a plan, the cerebellum
corrects it against what the spindles report, the basal ganglia
release it, and the spinal cord's circuits, which can run a
stepping rhythm on their own, execute it --- the subject of the
Year 3 volume.
\end{proposition}

\section{Fuel and fatigue}

\begin{proposition}[Paying for the work]\label{prop:b2:muscle-movement:energy}
\index{creatine phosphate}\index{muscle fatigue}\index{oxygen debt}
A fibre holds ATP for a second or two of full effort. The first
reserve is \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:energy}{creatine phosphate}}, which rephosphorylates ADP
within milliseconds and lasts some ten seconds --- the sprinter's
fuel. The second is \emph{glycolysis} of the fibre's glycogen,
without oxygen, yielding two ATP per glucose and lactate: fast,
enough for a minute or two, and self-limiting as acid and phosphate
accumulate. The third is \emph{oxidative} metabolism of glucose and
fatty acids in the mitochondria, thirty ATP per glucose, limited
only by the oxygen the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} delivers
(\cref{ch:b2:blood-pressure}): the marathon. Fast glycolytic fibres
rely on the first two and tire in a minute; slow oxidative fibres,
red with myoglobin and packed with mitochondria, run for hours.
\emph{Fatigue} in a short effort is not the exhaustion of ATP ---
which would leave the muscle in rigor --- but the accumulation of
phosphate and protons, which weaken the cross-bridges and the
calcium release; in a long effort it is the exhaustion of glycogen
and, in the end, of the will. After the effort the muscle repays
its \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:energy}{oxygen debt}}: it restores \omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:energy}{creatine phosphate}, converts
the lactate back (in the liver) and refills its stores, breathing
hard for minutes after standing still.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[A hundred metres]\label{ex:b2:muscle-movement:sprint}
Ten seconds of maximal effort by \qty{20}{kg} of leg muscle at
\qty{100}{W/kg} is \qty{2}{kW} of mechanical power and, at
\qty{25}{\%} efficiency, \qty{8}{kW} of ATP turnover --- some
\qty{160}{mmol} of ATP a second, or \qty{80}{g} of ATP in the race.
The muscle holds a few grams; \omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:energy}{creatine phosphate} supplies the first
five seconds, glycolysis the rest, and the sprinter finishes with
lactate at \qty{15}{mmol/L} in her \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} and a debt she pays over
the next quarter of an hour. A marathoner at \qty{300}{W} for two
hours burns \qty{2}{MJ} of work, \qty{8}{MJ} of fuel --- a kilogram of
glycogen and fat --- with oxygen delivered at \qty{4}{L/min} all the
way, and the wall she hits at the thirtieth kilometre is the bottom
of her glycogen.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Physiology of a Jump}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a standing jump analysed from
the sarcomere to the take-off: the sliding, the cross-bridges, the
calcium switch, the force--velocity limit, the motor units and the
fuel, ending on the number of cross-bridge strokes, the force at the
take-off speed, and the ATP the jump costs}]
\label{pb:b2:muscle-movement:1}
A person of \qty{70}{kg} jumps \qty{40}{cm} straight up, raising the
body's centre of mass by \qty{0.4}{m} during a push of \qty{0.3}{s}.
The leg extensors (\qty{20}{kg} of muscle) have a combined
cross-section of \qty{500}{cm^2} at \qty{30}{N/cm^2}; their fibres
are \qty{12}{cm} long (\num{48000} \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomeres}) and shorten by
\qty{4}{cm} during the push; the joints multiply the muscle's
shortening by \num{10} at the foot and divide its force by the
same factor. $v_{\max} = 8$ lengths a second, $a = F_0/4$. Each
thick filament carries \num{300} myosin heads; there are $6\times
10^{10}$ thick filaments per square centimetre of muscle; a stroke
is \qty{10}{nm} and costs one ATP (\qty{8e-20}{J}, \qty{50}{kJ/mol});
efficiency \qty{40}{\%}. The muscle holds \qty{5}{mmol/kg} of ATP and
\qty{20}{mmol/kg} of \omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:energy}{creatine phosphate}. Fibres are \qty{60}{\micro
m} across. \omterm{def:b2:muscle-movement:motorunit}{Motor units}: \num{800} per muscle group, from \num{50} to
\num{2000} fibres each.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The mechanics.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the take-off speed needed to rise \qty{40}{cm}
        ($v^{2} = 2gh$), and the kinetic energy at take-off.
  \item Compute the mean force the legs exert on the ground during
        the push (work over \qty{0.4}{m} equals the kinetic energy
        plus the work against gravity), and compare with the weight.
  \item Compute the maximal isometric force of the extensors and,
        with the lever ratio of 10, the maximal foot force it could
        give.
  \item Compute the fibres' shortening speed in lengths per second
        and as a fraction of $v_{\max}$.
  \item With Hill's relation, compute the force available at that
        speed as a fraction of $F_0$, and the foot force it gives.
        Can the muscle alone do the jump? Where does the rest come
        from?
  \item Compute the mean mechanical power during the push and the
        power per kilogram of extensor.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The cross-bridges.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How far does each \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomere} shorten, and how many
        \qty{10}{nm} strokes must be made along each \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{half-sarcomere}
        to slide its filaments that far?
  \item Compute the sliding speed in each \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{half-sarcomere} and the
        number of strokes per second a head would make if it were
        attached continuously.
  \item Compute the number of myosin heads in the extensors.
  \item Compute the work the muscle itself does (its force at the
        jump's speed times its shortening), the work per stroke at
        \qty{40}{\%} efficiency, and the number of strokes needed.
  \item How many strokes is that per head, against the number
        available from question 7? Comment on the reserve.
  \item Compute the ATP consumed by the jump, in moles and in grams
        (\qty{507}{g/mol}), and check the efficiency from the energy
        of that ATP.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The switch and the command.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The push takes \qty{0.3}{s} and the calcium pulse of one
        impulse \qty{30}{ms}. What firing rate keeps the fibres in
        \omterm{def:b2:muscle-movement:motorunit}{tetanus}, and how many impulses does each motor \omterm{def:b2:neurons-synapses:neuron}{neuron} send?
  \item The free calcium rises from \qty{0.1}{} to \qty{10}{\micro
        mol/L} in a fibre of \qty{5e-10}{L}. How many free calcium ions
        is that, and how many pump cycles (two ions per ATP) does
        taking them back cost?
  \item Compute the number of fibres in the extensors and the
        cross-bridge ATP per fibre per impulse, and compare with the
        pump cost of question 14. The real cost of pumping is about
        a quarter of the muscle's ATP: what does the free calcium
        understate?
  \item The nervous system recruits units by size. For the jump,
        which units are recruited and in what order? What fraction
        of the \num{800} would a gentle step use?
  \item Explain why the force can be graded finely at low effort and
        only coarsely near the maximum.
  \item A person on a drug that blocks the \omterm{prop:b2:neurons-synapses:integration}{neuromuscular junction}
        half-way: predict the \omterm{def:b2:muscle-movement:motorunit}{twitch}, the \omterm{def:b2:muscle-movement:motorunit}{tetanus} and the jump.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The fuel.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the ATP stored in the extensors and compare with the
        jump's consumption.
  \item Compute the \omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:energy}{creatine phosphate} store and the number of jumps
        it could pay for.
  \item Ten jumps in a row: which fuel takes over, and what
        accumulates?
  \item The \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} can deliver \qty{2}{L} of oxygen a minute to the
        extensors (6 ATP per $\mathrm{O_2}$). What ATP turnover does
        the jump's power require, and what oxygen supply would that
        take? Conclude.
  \item After the jumps the person breathes hard for a minute.
        Compute the oxygen needed to resynthesise the \omterm{prop:b2:muscle-movement:energy}{creatine
        phosphate} and ATP used (\qty{6}{ATP} per $\mathrm{O_2}$
        molecule) and compare with the resting consumption of
        \qty{250}{mL/min}.
  \item Why does a jumper's muscle not go into rigor even though ATP
        is turned over at such a rate?
  \item State the result: the strokes per \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{half-sarcomere}, the force
        fraction at the jump's speed, and the ATP the jump costs.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
