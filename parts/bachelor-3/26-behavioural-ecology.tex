\chapter{Behavioural Ecology}\label{ch:b3:behavioural-ecology}

A meerkat stands on a termite mound while the rest of its group digs
for food, and when a hawk appears it barks and the others bolt for
their burrows. The sentinel has spent an hour not eating and has made
itself the most conspicuous animal in the desert. Why? A peacock drags
a tail that costs it a fifth of its energy budget and slows its escape
from tigers. A honeybee returning from a field dances on a vertical
comb, and the angle of her dance to the vertical is the angle of the
food to the sun. A great tit hunting caterpillars in a patch of foliage
leaves it while there are caterpillars still to be found. None of these
animals has read this chapter, yet each behaves as if it had solved an
optimisation, a game, or a problem in kinship accounting. Behavioural
ecology is the study of behaviour as a set of evolved solutions:
what a behaviour is for, in terms of the genes it propagates, and what
its costs and benefits must be for natural selection to have produced
it. Its tools are the ones an economist uses --- optimisation, \omterm{prop:b3:behavioural-ecology:hawk-dove}{game
theory}, the accounting of relatedness --- and its test is the field.

\section{Four questions and the machinery of instinct}

\begin{definition}[Tinbergen's questions]\label{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:tinbergen}
\index{Tinbergen's four questions}\index{fixed action pattern}\index{sign stimulus}\index{imprinting}\index{critical period}
Tinbergen (1963) said a complete account of any behaviour must answer
four questions: its \emph{mechanism} (what stimuli and neural machinery
produce it), its \emph{development} (how it arises in the individual,
from genes and experience), its \emph{function} (how it contributes to
survival and reproduction) and its \emph{evolutionary history} (how it
arose from ancestral behaviour). The ethologists who framed them had
established the first two. Many behaviours are \emph{fixed action
patterns}: stereotyped sequences released by a simple \emph{sign
stimulus} and run to completion once begun --- a greylag goose rolls a
displaced egg back to the nest with its bill, and completes the motion
even if the egg is removed halfway; a herring gull chick pecks at a red
spot on any bill-shaped object, and pecks more at a stick with three red
bands than at a real gull's head; a male stickleback attacks anything
red on its underside, including a passing mail van seen through the
window. Others are learned within a window: \emph{imprinting}, by which
a gosling in its first day follows and thereafter treats as its parent
whatever moves --- a goose, Lorenz, a cardboard box --- is fast,
irreversible and confined to a \emph{critical period}; song learning in
birds, the Year~2 volume's example, is the same shape. The dichotomy
of innate and learned is false: a behaviour has a genetic programme
that specifies what is to be learned, when, and from whom, and the
programme is what evolves.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Tinbergen's field experiments set the method. Digger wasps
(\emph{Philanthus}) return to a nest hidden in sand; he moved a ring of
pine \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{cones} he had placed around one entrance while the wasp was away,
and she searched the centre of the displaced ring --- she had learned
the landmarks. Black-headed gulls remove eggshells from the nest after
hatching; he laid out eggs beside painted shells, natural shells and
other objects, and counted which the crows took: the white inner
surface of a shell drew predators, and the removal that had looked
like tidiness was camouflage. The gull-chick experiments quantified a
\omterm{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:tinbergen}{sign stimulus} by offering cardboard heads that varied one feature at a
time. Lorenz's imprinted geese followed him for life; ducks imprinted
in the first day on a moving box and could not afterwards be made to
follow a duck.
\end{proof}

\section{Deciding: optimal foraging}

\begin{theorem}[The marginal value theorem]\label{thm:b3:behavioural-ecology:mvt}
\index{optimal foraging}\index{marginal value theorem}\index{patch residence time}\index{currency}
A forager visits patches of food separated by travel time $\tau$. In a
patch it gains energy $g(t)$ after time $t$, with $g$ increasing and
concave (the patch depletes). If it leaves every patch after time $t$,
its long-run rate of gain is $R(t) = g(t)/(\tau + t)$, and the residence
time $t^{*}$ that maximises it satisfies
\[
g'(t^{*}) = \frac{g(t^{*})}{\tau + t^{*}} = R(t^{*}) :
\]
leave a patch when the instantaneous rate of gain in it has fallen to
the average rate over the whole habitat, travel included (Charnov,
1976). Consequences: the longer the travel time, the longer the stay
and the more thoroughly each patch is depleted; in a richer habitat
(higher $R$) every patch is left sooner and less depleted; and all
patches are left at the same marginal rate whatever their quality, so
that a poor patch is left almost at once and a rich one only when it
has been worked down to the habitat's average. For $g(t) = A(1 -
\mathrm{e}^{-t/T_{0}})$ the condition becomes $(\tau + t^{*})/T_{0} =
\mathrm{e}^{t^{*}/T_{0}} - 1$, solved numerically: with $\tau = T_{0}$,
$t^{*} \approx 1.15\,T_{0}$; with $\tau = 4T_{0}$, $t^{*} \approx
2.0\,T_{0}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$R(t) = g(t)/(\tau + t)$; $R'(t) = [g'(t)(\tau + t) - g(t)]/(\tau +
t)^{2} = 0$ gives $g'(t^{*}) = g(t^{*})/(\tau + t^{*})$. Geometrically:
plot $g$ against $t$ with the origin at $-\tau$; $R(t)$ is the slope of
the line from $(-\tau, 0)$ to $(t, g(t))$, maximal where that line is
tangent to the curve --- which is the condition. A larger $\tau$ moves
the origin left and the tangent point right; a richer habitat steepens
the tangent and moves it left. For the exponential, $g' = (A/T_{0})
\mathrm{e}^{-t/T_{0}}$ and $g/(\tau + t) = A(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/T_{0}})/
(\tau + t)$; equating and rearranging gives the stated equation, and
substituting $t^{*} = 1.15T_{0}$ with $\tau = T_{0}$: left $2.15$, right
$\mathrm{e}^{1.15} - 1 = 2.16$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Cowie (1977) let great tits hunt mealworms hidden in sawdust-filled
cups in an aviary, with the travel time between cups manipulated by
fitting lids that took longer or shorter to remove; the birds stayed
longer in each cup when travel was slower, tracking the theorem's
prediction, and overshot it slightly in the direction that accounting
for the energetic cost of travel explains. Starlings carrying leatherjackets
to their nestlings (Kacelnik, 1984) took larger loads from feeders
placed farther away, again as the rate-maximising solution to a
loading curve requires. Neither test proves that birds compute
tangents; both show that selection has produced rules of thumb whose
outcome is close to the optimum.
\end{proof}

\section{Contests: game theory}

\begin{proposition}[Hawk--Dove and the evolutionarily stable strategy]\label{prop:b3:behavioural-ecology:hawk-dove}
\index{game theory}\index{Hawk--Dove game}\index{evolutionarily stable strategy}\index{frequency-dependent selection}
When the best behaviour depends on what others do, there is no optimum,
only a \emph{game}. Two animals contest a resource worth $V$; a
\emph{Hawk} fights until injured (cost $C$) or victorious, a \emph{Dove}
displays and retreats if attacked. The average payoffs are
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & \text{vs Hawk} & \text{vs Dove}\\ \hline
\text{Hawk} & (V - C)/2 & V\\
\text{Dove} & 0 & V/2
\end{array}
\]
An \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:behavioural-ecology:hawk-dove}{evolutionarily stable strategy}} (Maynard Smith and Price, 1973)
is one which, once common, cannot be invaded by any rare alternative.
If $V > C$, Hawk is the ESS: fighting pays even against fighters. If $V
< C$, neither pure strategy is stable --- a population of Doves is
invaded by Hawks, who win every contest, and a population of Hawks by
Doves, who avoid the injuries --- and the ESS is to play Hawk with
probability
\[
p^{*} = \frac{V}{C},
\]
or, equivalently, a population in which that fraction are Hawks. At
the ESS the two strategies do equally well, each averaging $V(1 -
V/C)/2$, less than the $V/2$ a population of Doves would have shared:
the contest costs everyone, and no one can do better alone. Selection
here is \emph{frequency-dependent}: a strategy's fitness falls as it
becomes common, which is why animal contests are mostly display and
occasionally war, and why the mix persists.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let a fraction $p$ play Hawk. A Hawk's expected payoff is $W_{H} = p(V
- C)/2 + (1 - p)V$; a Dove's is $W_{D} = (1 - p)V/2$. $W_{H} - W_{D} =
p(V - C)/2 + (1 - p)V/2 = [V - pC]/2$, positive for $p < V/C$ and
negative above: Hawks spread when rare and are culled when common, and
the frequency settles where $W_{H} = W_{D}$, at $p^{*} = V/C$. That it
is stable follows from the sign change: a perturbation of $p$ above
$p^{*}$ favours Doves and is corrected, below it favours Hawks. If $V
\ge C$ the difference is positive for all $p \le 1$ and Hawk fixes.
The common payoff at $p^{*}$: $W_{D} = (1 - V/C)V/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Games in the field]\label{ex:b3:behavioural-ecology:games}
Male speckled wood butterflies contest sunspots on the forest floor:
the resident almost always wins a brief spiral flight, and when two
males are tricked into each believing themselves resident the fight
lasts ten times longer --- a convention (``resident wins'') that settles
contests cheaply, itself an ESS when fighting is costly and the
resource is not worth much. Male dung flies wait on cowpats for
females and leave when the rate of arrivals falls to what a fresh pat
would offer --- the marginal value theorem again, in a game where the
patch's value depends on how many rivals sit on it. Fig wasps, side-blotched
lizards with three male types that beat one another cyclically like
rock--paper--scissors, and the ``sneaker'' males of salmon and bluegill
sunfish, which fertilise eggs by darting past the territorial males
they cannot fight, are all mixtures held at frequencies where the
alternatives pay equally. The war of attrition --- two animals
displaying until one gives up, the ESS being to persist for a random
time drawn from an exponential distribution --- describes the staring
contests of many species, and predicts, correctly, that the loser's
persistence gives away nothing about how long it would have gone on.
\end{example}

\section{Kin: Hamilton's rule}

\begin{theorem}[Hamilton's rule]\label{thm:b3:behavioural-ecology:hamilton}
\index{Hamilton's rule}\index{kin selection}\index{relatedness}\index{inclusive fitness}\index{altruism}\index{haplodiploidy}
An allele causing its bearer to perform an act that costs it $c$
offspring and gives a relative $b$ extra offspring spreads if
\[
r\,b > c ,
\]
where $r$ is the \emph{coefficient of relatedness}: the probability
that a gene in the recipient is a copy, by descent, of the same gene in
the actor --- $1/2$ for parent and offspring or full siblings, $1/4$
for half-siblings, grandchildren, nieces and nephews, $1/8$ for first
cousins. The quantity the allele maximises is its bearer's
\emph{\omterm{thm:b3:behavioural-ecology:hamilton}{inclusive fitness}}: its own reproduction plus its effects on the
reproduction of relatives, each weighted by $r$. \emph{Altruism} ---
behaviour that lowers the actor's reproduction and raises another's
--- thus evolves toward kin, in proportion to relatedness, and the
rule predicts where it should be found: in the alarm calls of female
ground squirrels, who live among their sisters and daughters, and not
of the males, who disperse; in the sentinel duty of meerkats, whose
group is a family; in the helpers at the nest of birds that assist
their parents to raise siblings when territories are full. In the
\emph{haplodiploid} Hymenoptera --- males from unfertilised eggs with one
set of chromosomes, females with two --- full sisters share three
quarters of their genes ($r = 3/4$), more than a mother shares with her
daughters ($1/2$), which Hamilton proposed as a reason the sterile
worker castes of ants, bees and wasps arose there eleven times and
elsewhere almost never.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Consider the allele's copies. When the actor performs the act, its own
copies lose $c$ in expected transmission; the recipient carries the
allele with probability $r$ above the population average, so the
recipient's gain $b$ increases the allele's transmission by $rb$ on
average. The allele increases in frequency when $rb - c > 0$ (Hamilton,
1964; the general derivation, from the covariance between an
individual's genotype and its fitness, is the Price equation, admitted
here). Relatedness: a parent transmits each gene to an offspring with
probability $1/2$; two full siblings each received a given gene from
the same parent with probability $1/2$, and it is the same copy with
probability $1/2$, and there are two parents: $r = 2\times\tfrac{1}{2}
\times\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Haplodiploid sisters: from their
haploid father they receive identical genes (probability $1$); from
their mother, identical copies with probability $1/2$; averaging over
the two parents, $r = (1 + 1/2)/2 = 3/4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Sherman (1977) watched Belding's ground squirrels for three summers
and recorded who gave alarm calls at approaching predators: females
with kin nearby called far more often than females without, and than
males, who had dispersed from their birthplace and had no relatives
within earshot --- the caller drew the predator's attention, and paid
for it, only when there were relatives to warn. Emlen's white-fronted
bee-eaters help at the nest in proportion to relatedness, choosing
among available nests those of the closest kin. Clutton-Brock's
long-term study of meerkats found sentinels to be the well-fed
individuals who had least to lose, and that the group's survival ---
and so the sentinel's relatives' --- rose with sentinel effort; the
cost, though, proved smaller than the story supposed, since a sentinel
on a mound is often the first to reach a burrow.
\end{proof}

\section{Mates and messages}

\begin{definition}[Sexual selection]\label{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:sexual-selection}
\index{sexual selection}\index{Bateman gradient}\index{runaway selection}\index{handicap principle}\index{good genes}\index{sexual conflict}
Darwin's second mechanism: selection on traits that win mates rather
than survival. Its root is anisogamy --- a female's reproduction is
limited by the eggs she can make and provision, a male's by the females
he can fertilise --- so that in most species a male's fitness rises
steeply with his number of matings and a female's hardly at all (the
\emph{Bateman gradient}); males then compete for females and females
choose among males. Competition gives horns, antlers, tusks, large
size and the contests of the previous section. Choice gives ornaments,
and three accounts of why females prefer them. Fisher's \emph{runaway}:
a preference for a slightly longer tail and the longer tail spread
together, each making the other advantageous, until the tail's cost in
survival balances its gain in matings. Zahavi's \emph{handicap}: an
ornament that only a healthy male can afford to grow is an \omterm{prop:b3:behavioural-ecology:signals}{honest
signal} of his condition, precisely because it is costly --- a peacock
with a full train has, demonstrably, fewer parasites. \emph{Good genes}
and \emph{direct benefits}: the female gains, through her offspring's
inheritance or through the territory and food the male provides.
The two sexes' interests differ, and \emph{sexual conflict} --- over
mating frequency, over who cares for the young, over the fate of a
previous mate's sperm --- is an arms race run within a species: the
seminal fluid of male fruit flies shortens the female's life and
raises her egg-laying, and females evolve resistance.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Andersson (1982) cut and glued the tail feathers of male long-tailed
widowbirds in Kenya: males with tails lengthened to twice their natural
length attracted four times as many new nests to their territories as
males with shortened tails, while controls whose tails were cut and
re-glued unchanged were unaffected --- female preference for a longer
tail than any male grows, the signature of a preference selected beyond
the trait. Petrie (1994) found that peahens mated with the males whose
trains carried the most eyespots, and that those males' chicks survived
better in the woods where they were released --- a good-genes benefit
measured. Bateman (1948) counted matings and offspring of fruit flies
carrying marker mutations and found the gradient that bears his name:
male reproductive success rose linearly with matings, female success
plateaued after one.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Honest signals]\label{prop:b3:behavioural-ecology:signals}
\index{communication}\index{honest signal}\index{waggle dance}\index{referential signal}
A signal is an act that changes a receiver's behaviour and evolved to
do so; it is stable only if it pays both parties, on average, to send
and to respond. Where the two have common interests the signal can be
cheap and precise: a forager honeybee's \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:behavioural-ecology:signals}{waggle dance}} (von
Frisch, 1940s) encodes the direction of a food source as the angle of
the waggle run to the vertical --- the angle of the source to the sun
--- and its distance as the run's duration, about a second per
kilometre, and recruits fly to within a few tens of metres of the
target; vervet monkeys give distinct alarm calls for leopards, eagles
and snakes, and listeners respond appropriately to each call played
from a hidden speaker --- \emph{referential} signals. Where interests
conflict, a signal must be costly or unfakeable to remain honest ---
the roar of a red deer stag, whose rate only a fit stag can sustain;
the size of a toad's croak, set by its body; the ornaments of the last
paragraph --- or it is exploited: fireflies of one genus mimic the
females' flashes of another to eat the males that come, and cuckoo
chicks' begging outbids the host's own. Signalling theory thus predicts
the form of a signal from the relation between the parties: cheap and
informative among kin and mates whose interests coincide, expensive and
ritualised between rivals, and an arms race between predator and prey.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{remark}[Why animals seem to calculate]\label{rem:b3:behavioural-ecology:remark}
The great tit does not differentiate $g(t)/(\tau + t)$, the meerkat does
not compute $r$, and the peahen has never heard of a handicap. Each
carries rules --- leave when captures slow, call when the group is
family, prefer the brightest --- that selection has shaped because
animals following them left more descendants; the mathematics is ours,
a way of asking what the rules must achieve for that to be so, and of
predicting behaviour in situations the animals have never met. When
the predictions fail --- the tit stays too long, the sentinel's cost is
smaller than supposed --- the failure is informative: a currency was
wrong, a constraint was missed, a relative was miscounted. The subject
is a decade-by-decade conversation between a field notebook and a
line of algebra, and the four questions Tinbergen posed remain the
frame for both.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:1}
State \omterm{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:tinbergen}{Tinbergen's four questions} and answer each, in a sentence, for
the \omterm{prop:b3:behavioural-ecology:signals}{waggle dance}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:2}
What is a \omterm{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:tinbergen}{fixed action pattern} and a \omterm{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:tinbergen}{sign stimulus}? Give two examples
and explain what the ``supernormal'' stick with three red bands showed.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:3}
In words, what does the marginal value theorem say, and what does it
predict about patch use when travel time doubles?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:4}
Define an ESS. Why is a population of pure Doves not one, and why does
the Hawk--Dove mixture cost everyone?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:5}
Hawk--Dove with $V = 6$, $C = 10$: compute $p^{*}$, the payoff to each
strategy at the ESS, and the payoff a population of all Doves would
have. Repeat for $V = 12$, $C = 10$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:6}
A gain function $g(t) = A(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/T_{0}})$ with $T_{0} =
\qty{2}{min}$. Find $t^{*}$ for $\tau = 2$ and $\tau = 8$ minutes (use
the chapter's values), the fraction of each patch consumed, and the
long-run rate $R$ as a fraction of $A$ per minute.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:7}
Compute $r$ between: half-siblings; grandparent and grandchild; first
cousins; a haplodiploid worker and her brother; a haplodiploid queen
and her son. Explain the last two.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:8}
An alarm call costs the caller a \qty{2}{\%} chance of death and saves
each of $n$ nearby relatives from a \qty{1}{\%} chance. For which $n$
does Hamilton's rule favour calling if the relatives are full siblings?
Nieces? Unrelated?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:9}
A widowbird's tail is \qty{50}{cm}; lengthened to \qty{75}{cm} it
attracts twice the nests, shortened to \qty{25}{cm} half. If survival
falls by \qty{20}{\%} for each \qty{25}{cm} added, which tail maximises
the product of matings and survival, and why might the natural tail be
shorter than that?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:10}
Add a third strategy, Retaliator, which displays like a Dove but
fights back if attacked. Write its payoffs against Hawk, Dove and
itself (against Hawk it fights: $(V - C)/2$; against Dove and
Retaliator it shares: $V/2$). Show that a population of Retaliators
cannot be invaded by Hawks when $V < C$, and discuss whether Doves can
drift in.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:11}
In a war of attrition, each contestant persists for a time drawn from
a distribution; the ESS is exponential with mean $V/$(cost per unit
time). Explain why a fixed persistence time cannot be an ESS, and why
an exponential distribution makes the expected future persistence of a
still-displaying opponent independent of how long it has already
displayed.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:behavioural-ecology:12}
Hamilton's argument for haplodiploidy predicts workers should prefer to
raise sisters ($r = 3/4$) over brothers ($r = 1/4$), while the queen
values sons and daughters equally. Predict the sex ratio of the
reproductives a colony should produce if the workers control it, and if
the queen does; say what Trivers and Hare found, and what a queen who
mates with several males does to the argument.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Sentinel's Arithmetic}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a day in a meerkat group done as
behavioural ecology: the patch times of the marginal value theorem,
the contests of the Hawk--Dove game and its ESS, the kin accounting of
sentinel duty and of a haplodiploid hive, and the cost of an ornament,
ending on the patch residence time, the Hawk frequency and the
sentinel's Hamilton threshold}]
\label{pb:b3:behavioural-ecology:1}
Data: foraging patches with $g(t) = A(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/T_{0}})$, $T_{0}
= \qty{3}{min}$, $A = \qty{60}{kJ}$; travel between patches $\tau =
\qty{3}{min}$ (dense habitat) or \qty{12}{min} (sparse). Contests over
a burrow: $V = 10$, $C = 30$ (fitness units). Sentinel: an hour's duty
costs the sentinel $c = 0.03$ offspring-equivalents (lost feeding,
exposure); it lowers each groupmate's predation risk, worth $b = 0.01$
offspring-equivalents each; the group has $n$ members of average
relatedness $\bar r$. Hive: a worker can rear either $k$ sisters or $k$
own daughters with the same effort. Ornament: tail length $L$ gives
matings $m(L) = L/20$ and survival $s(L) = 1 - (L/100)^{2}$, $L$ in cm.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Patches.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write the long-run rate $R(t)$ and the marginal value condition
        for this $g$.
  \item Show the condition reduces to $(\tau + t)/T_{0} = \mathrm{e}^{t/T_{0}}
        - 1$, and solve it for $\tau = T_{0}$ (try $t = 1.15\,T_{0}$).
  \item Solve for $\tau = 4T_{0}$ (try $t = 2.0\,T_{0}$).
  \item For each case: residence time in minutes, energy per patch,
        fraction of the patch consumed, and rate $R$ in kJ/min.
  \item A rule of thumb: ``leave after a fixed \qty{5}{min}.'' Compute
        $R$ for both habitats and compare with the optimum. Is the rule
        good enough?
  \item Another rule: ``leave when \qty{20}{s} pass without a
        capture.'' Explain why this giving-up-time rule approximates the
        theorem, and in which direction it fails when patches differ in
        richness.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Contests.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Write the payoff matrix and compute $p^{*}$.
  \item Payoff to Hawk and to Dove when $p = 0.1$, $p = p^{*}$ and $p =
        0.6$; which strategy spreads in each case?
  \item Payoff at the ESS; compare with the all-Dove payoff. How much
        does the population lose per contest to the possibility of
        fighting?
  \item A drought raises $V$ to $40$ (burrows scarce). New ESS? What
        does this predict about fights in hard years?
  \item Bourgeois: ``Hawk if resident, Dove if intruder,'' each role
        half the time. Its payoff against itself is $V/2$ with no
        fights. Show Hawks cannot invade a Bourgeois population when $V
        < C$, and explain the speckled wood butterfly.
  \item Explain in a sentence why frequency dependence, and not an
        optimum, governs this behaviour.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Kin.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Hamilton's rule for the sentinel: condition on $n\bar r$. For
        what $n\bar r$ does duty pay?
  \item A group of $n = 8$ full siblings ($\bar r = 1/2$): does it pay?
        A group of 8 unrelated animals? A group of 8 half-siblings?
  \item The sentinel is well fed, so its $c$ falls to $0.01$. Threshold
        now? Why does this predict that sentinels are the well-fed
        individuals?
  \item The hive: inclusive-fitness value of $k$ sisters against $k$
        daughters. Which should a worker prefer, by what ratio?
  \item The queen has mated with three males equally: workers' average
        relatedness to sisters? Does the preference survive?
  \item Explain why Hamilton's rule does not require the actor to
        recognise kin, and what rule of thumb a ground squirrel might
        actually use.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Ornaments.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Fitness $W(L) = m(L)\,s(L)$. Compute $W$ for $L = 20$, $40$, $60$,
        $80$.
  \item Find the $L$ that maximises $W$ analytically, and $W$ there.
  \item Survival cost steepens to $s = 1 - (L/70)^{2}$ (a predator
        arrives). New optimum? In which direction, and why does this
        predict the tail's evolution to track predation?
  \item Andersson's experiment doubled tails to \qty{75}{cm} and doubled
        nests. Is the female preference for longer tails ``beyond the
        optimum'' consistent with the runaway account, the handicap
        account, or both?
  \item Why is a cheap ornament not an \omterm{prop:b3:behavioural-ecology:signals}{honest signal}, and how does the
        cost in question~21 make it one?
  \item \omterm{def:b3:behavioural-ecology:sexual-selection}{Bateman gradients}: males gain $0.8$ offspring per extra mating,
        females $0.1$. Explain in one paragraph how this asymmetry
        generates both the contests of Part~II and the ornaments of
        Part~IV.
  \item Summarise: $t^{*}$ in the dense habitat (question~4), $p^{*}$
        (question~7), and the sentinel's threshold $n\bar r$
        (question~13).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
