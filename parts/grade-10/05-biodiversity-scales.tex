\chapter{Biodiversity at Every Scale}\label{ch:g10:biodiversity-scales}

Lay a one-metre square of string on a lawn and count what grows inside
it: grass, of course, but also clover, daisies, a dandelion, plantain,
moss, and, if you look with a lens, a dozen insects and a snail. Move
the square onto the tarmac of the car park: nothing, or one weed in a
crack. Move it into an old meadow that has never been ploughed: forty
plant \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, and the insects beyond counting. The word for what the
square measures is \emph{\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{biodiversity}}, and this chapter is about how to
measure it, at what scales it exists, and why it is neither fixed nor
guaranteed.

\section{Three scales of diversity}

\begin{definition}[Biodiversity]\label{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}
\emph{Biodiversity}\index{biodiversity} is the diversity of living
things, considered at three scales:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the diversity of \emph{ecosystems}\index{ecosystem} --- of the
        living communities and the environments they occupy (a pond,
        a beech wood, a coral reef, a meadow), each with its own set of
        \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species};
  \item the diversity of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} within an
        ecosystem or on Earth --- the number of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} and their
        relative abundance;
  \item the \emph{genetic diversity}\index{genetic diversity} within a
        \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} --- the number of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} and the way
        they are shared out among its individuals and populations.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Species]\label{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}
A \emph{species}\index{species} is a group of living things that resemble one
another, can breed together, and produce fertile offspring; two
populations that cannot interbreed, or whose hybrids are sterile, are
distinct species. In practice --- for fossils, for bacteria, for
organisms never seen breeding --- species are recognised by their
shared characteristics, and \cref{ch:g12:selection-drift-speciation}
will show that the boundary is not always sharp.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Horse, donkey, mule]\label{ex:g10:biodiversity-scales:mule}
A horse and a donkey resemble each other and can breed: their
offspring is the mule. But the mule is sterile, so horse and donkey are
two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}. A poodle and a wolfhound look nothing alike, breed
readily, and give fertile puppies: one \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, the dog --- which can
also breed with the wolf, its ancestor.
\end{example}

\section{The diversity of species}

\begin{proposition}[Counting species]\label{prop:g10:biodiversity-scales:count}
About two million \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} have been described and named. Most are
insects (over a million), followed by plants (some 300\,000), fungi,
arachnids, molluscs; the vertebrates, the group we know best, number
about 70\,000, of which 6\,400 are mammals. The number of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
still undescribed is estimated, from the rate at which new ones are
found, at several million more --- perhaps eight to ten million
eukaryotic \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} in all, plus a vast, largely uncounted diversity of
bacteria.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Measuring the diversity of a site]\label{met:g10:biodiversity-scales:sampling}
A site is never counted whole; it is \emph{sampled}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Choose a sampling unit adapted to the organisms: a quadrat of
        \qty{1}{m^2} for meadow plants, a net sweep for insects, a
        sieve of soil for earthworms, a length of transect for birds.
  \item Place units at random or at regular intervals, enough of them
        that adding one more changes the total little.
  \item In each, identify and count. Two numbers describe the sample:
        the \emph{\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} richness}, the number of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} found, and
        the \emph{relative abundance} of each --- a site where one
        \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} makes 95\% of individuals is less diverse than one
        where ten \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} share them evenly, even at equal richness.
  \item Compare sites only with the same method and the same effort,
        at the same season.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Two lawns]\label{ex:g10:biodiversity-scales:lawns}
Ten quadrats on a mown school lawn: 6 plant \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, of which grass
covers 90\%. Ten quadrats on an unmown verge beside it: 23 \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species},
none above 30\%. The verge is richer and more even; the difference is
not the soil or the climate, which are the same, but the mowing, which
removes every plant that cannot flower close to the ground.
\end{example}

\section{Genetic diversity within a species}

\begin{proposition}[Individuals of a species are not identical]\label{prop:g10:biodiversity-scales:genetic}
Within a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, most \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} exist in several \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, and the
individuals carry different combinations of them. The
\emph{frequency} of an \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} in a population --- the fraction of all
copies of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} that are this \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} --- varies from one population
to another. \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{Genetic diversity} is what allows a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} to respond to a
changing environment: a population in which every individual is alike
has nothing to select from.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Blood-group alleles around the world]\label{ex:g10:biodiversity-scales:abo}
The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} of the ABO blood groups has three common \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, A, B and O.
In western Europe their frequencies are about 0.27, 0.07 and 0.66; in
eastern Asia about 0.20, 0.20 and 0.60; in some native populations of
South America the O \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} alone reaches 0.98. Same \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, same
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, different distributions of its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}: the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic diversity} of
humans is real, and it is spread across populations rather than
splitting them --- most of the diversity of the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} is found within
any one population.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A species with almost none]\label{ex:g10:biodiversity-scales:cheetah}
Cheetahs are so alike genetically that skin grafts between unrelated
animals are accepted as if between twins: the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} passed through a
bottleneck of very few individuals some ten thousand years ago and
has never recovered its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}. Whatever new disease or climate comes,
every cheetah meets it with the same equipment; the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} survives,
but with nothing in reserve.
\end{example}

\section{Biodiversity changes: past, present}

\begin{proposition}[Biodiversity is a state, not a constant]\label{prop:g10:biodiversity-scales:changes}
The set of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} alive at a given time is a stage in a history. The
fossil record shows \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} appearing, persisting for a few million
years on average, and disappearing; the total diversity of life has
increased over the last 540 million years, interrupted by five
\emph{mass extinctions}\index{mass extinction} in which more than
three quarters of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} vanished within a geologically short time.
The most recent, 66 million years ago, ended the non-bird dinosaurs
and opened the way to the diversification of mammals.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Counts of fossil families in dated rock layers, compiled for the whole
marine record, give the curve of the next figure: a rise from a few
hundred to nearly a thousand families, with sharp falls at 445, 370,
252, 201 and 66 million years ago. The 252-million-year event, the
largest, removed about 95\% of marine \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}; the layers just above
it are almost empty of fossils, and new groups fill them only over the
following millions of years.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The present change]\label{prop:g10:biodiversity-scales:present}
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{Species} are currently disappearing at a rate estimated at a hundred to
a thousand times the average of the fossil record, from the
destruction of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{ecosystems} (forest clearance, drainage of wetlands,
urbanisation), from overexploitation, from introduced \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, from
pollution and from climate change --- all of human origin. Half the
tropical forests of 1950 are gone; a third of amphibian \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} are
threatened; the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic diversity} of crops and livestock has narrowed
to a few varieties. \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{Biodiversity} is a resource --- food, medicines,
pollination, soil, the stability of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{ecosystems} --- and its loss is not
reversible on a human time scale.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Reversing a loss]\label{ex:g10:biodiversity-scales:reversing}
Where hedgerows removed in the 1960s have been replanted, bird and
insect richness returns within a decade; where wolves were reintroduced
into a national park in 1995, the deer they hunt stopped stripping the
riverside willows, the willows regrew, beavers returned, and the
birds and fish that depend on beaver ponds with them. Protecting or
restoring an \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{ecosystem} restores diversity at all three scales at once;
a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} kept alive in a zoo keeps only one of them.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Hedgerow's Dividend}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a field survey of two farms, one
with hedgerows and one without: species counted, abundances compared,
a snail's alleles followed, and the number a hedge is worth}]
\label{pb:g10:biodiversity-scales:1}
Two neighbouring farms grow the same wheat on the same soil. Farm A
kept its hedgerows; Farm B removed them in 1970. A class samples both
in June with the same method: 20 quadrats of \qty{1}{m^2} along the
field edges for plants, 20 net sweeps for insects, and 10 minutes of
listening at 5 points for birds.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Plants.}
Results, Farm A: 31 plant \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} in the 20 quadrats; Farm B: 9.
On Farm B, one grass makes 84\% of the plants counted; on Farm A the
commonest \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} makes 22\%.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Give the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} richness of each farm's edges and the ratio
        between them.
  \item Which farm's plant community is more even? Explain what
        "even" adds to "rich".
  \item On Farm A, the number of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} found after 5, 10, 15 and 20
        quadrats was 18, 25, 29 and 31. Is the sampling sufficient?
        Justify.
  \item The same series on Farm B was 6, 8, 9, 9. Compare the two
        series and say what shape a "complete" sampling curve takes.
  \item Why must both farms be sampled in the same month?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Insects and birds.}
Insects: Farm A 46 \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, Farm B 14. Birds: Farm A 17 \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, Farm B
5; on Farm B, 60\% of birds heard were of a single \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the ratio of insect richness and of bird richness
        between the farms. Do the three groups (plants, insects,
        birds) tell the same story?
  \item Propose a chain of causes linking the removal of hedges to the
        fall in insect diversity, then to bird diversity.
  \item The wheat yield per hectare is the same on both farms. Does
        this mean the hedges have no effect on the crop? Suggest one
        service a hedge may render that the yield figure does not
        show.
  \item Name one \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} the class would expect to find only on Farm A
        and explain why.
  \item Which scale of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{biodiversity} has been most obviously reduced on
        Farm B: \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{ecosystems}, \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}, or \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}? Justify.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- A snail's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}.}
The grove snail carries a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} with two common \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} for shell
colour, yellow (y) and brown (b). In Farm A's hedges, 400 snails were
sampled: 100 have two y \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, 200 have one y and one b, 100 have two
b. In Farm B's one remaining bank, 100 snails: 4 with two y, 32 with
one of each, 64 with two b.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Count the copies of y and of b in each sample (each snail
        carries two copies). Deduce the frequency of y on each farm.
  \item Which population is more genetically diverse at this \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}?
        Compare the proportion of snails carrying both \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}.
  \item Thrushes hunt snails by sight; on the bare bank of Farm B,
        brown shells are better hidden. Propose how the frequencies
        of Farm B came about.
  \item If a wet, shaded hedge favoured yellow shells, what would you
        predict for Farm B's snails if hedges were replanted and
        nothing else changed?
  \item Farm B's snails number a few hundred; Farm A's tens of
        thousands. Which population is more exposed to losing an
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} altogether by chance? Why?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The dividend.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Across the three groups, compute the total \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} richness
        of each farm, then Farm A's total divided by Farm B's.
  \item Farm A's hedges occupy 3\% of its area. State in one sentence
        what fraction of the land is holding what fraction of the
        \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}.
  \item Hedges replanted on Farm B in 1995 were resurveyed in 2010:
        plants 24, insects 35, birds 12 \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}. What fraction of Farm
        A's richness had each group recovered?
  \item Which of the three scales of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{biodiversity} recovers slowest
        after replanting, and why?
  \item State the result: the factor by which hedgerows multiplied the
        \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} richness of a farm, and the one scale of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{biodiversity}
        that a replanted hedge cannot bring back by itself.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
