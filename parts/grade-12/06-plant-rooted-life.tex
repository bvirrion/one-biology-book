\chapter{Plant Life: Thriving While Rooted}\label{ch:g12:plant-rooted-life}

An oak cannot walk to water, run from a caterpillar, or go looking for
a mate. It stands where its acorn fell for three centuries, and in that
time it lifts hundreds of tonnes of water from the soil, fends off
thousands of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} that would eat it, turns its leaves to follow the
light, and sends its pollen and its acorns across the countryside by
wind and by jay. A rooted life is not a passive one; it is a different
set of solutions to the same problems --- feeding, defending, moving,
reproducing --- and this chapter surveys them.

\section{A body built for exchange}

\begin{proposition}[Two systems, two surfaces]\label{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:surfaces}
A flowering plant is organised in two systems: the \emph{root system}
in the soil, which absorbs water and mineral ions and anchors the
plant, and the \emph{shoot system} in the air --- stems and leaves ---
which captures light and carbon dioxide. Each is a vast surface of
exchange: the leaves of a large tree spread several hundred square
metres of surface to the light, and its roots, extended by billions of
microscopic \emph{root hairs}\index{root hair}, several hundred square
metres to the soil. A plant is, above all, a way of spreading surface
in two directions from a fixed point.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
A single rye plant grown for four months in a box of soil was found to
have $\num{1.4e10}$ \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:surfaces}{root hairs} with a total length of over
\qty{10000}{km} and a surface of about \qty{400}{m^2}, ten times that
of its leaves. A mature oak carries some 250\,000 leaves; a hectare of
forest presents \qtyrange{5}{8}{ha} of leaf surface to the sky.
Cutting the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:surfaces}{root hairs} off a seedling, or coating the leaves, stops
its growth within days.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Stomata]\label{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}
A \emph{stoma}\index{stoma} is a pore in the skin of a leaf, bounded
by two \emph{guard \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}} that open it by swelling and close it by
shrinking. Through the open stomata carbon dioxide enters for
\omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} and water vapour leaves --- the \emph{transpiration}
that a plant cannot avoid while it feeds. A leaf carries one to three
hundred stomata per square millimetre, mostly on its lower surface;
they open in the light and close in the dark and in drought.
\end{definition}

\section{Moving water and food without a heart}

\begin{proposition}[Two saps]\label{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}
A plant has two transport systems, both made of long \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} joined into
tubes in the veins of every organ.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \emph{xylem}\index{xylem} carries \emph{raw sap} --- water
        and mineral ions absorbed by the roots --- upwards to the
        leaves. The driving force is transpiration: water evaporating
        from the leaf \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} pulls the continuous column of water in
        the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{xylem} up from the roots, as a wick draws oil. No pump, no
        energy spent by the plant: the sun does the lifting.
  \item The \emph{phloem}\index{phloem} carries \emph{elaborated sap}
        --- water with the sugars made by the leaves --- from the
        leaves to every organ that consumes or stores them: growing
        tips, roots, fruits, seeds, tubers. It flows from source to
        sink, in whichever direction the sinks lie.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
A cut stem placed in dyed water shows the dye rising in the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{xylem},
faster in a transpiring shoot than in one enclosed in a bag; a shoot
whose leaves are removed lifts nothing. A ring of bark (which contains
the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{phloem}) removed from a trunk stops sugar reaching the roots, which
starve within months, while the leaves above the ring stay supplied
with water; sugar accumulates above the ring and the bark swells. Aphids
feeding on the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{phloem} with their needle-like mouths yield, when cut off
from the needle, a drop of sugary sap under pressure.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The numbers of a tree]\label{ex:g12:plant-rooted-life:numbers}
A mature birch on a summer day transpires \qtyrange{300}{400}{L}
of water, lifted \qty{20}{m} through \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{xylem} tubes a tenth of a
millimetre wide at \qtyrange{1}{5}{m} per hour. Less than 1\% of
that water is kept for \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis}; the rest is the price of open
\omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata}. In return the leaves fix some \qty{5}{kg} of carbon dioxide
into \qty{3.5}{kg} of sugar, of which the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{phloem} delivers a share to
every root tip and every ripening seed.
\end{example}

\section{Standing one's ground}

\begin{proposition}[Defences of a rooted organism]\label{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:defences}
Unable to flee, plants defend themselves in place.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Structures}: a waxy cuticle and bark against drying and
        infection; thorns, spines, stinging hairs and silica against
        herbivores; a thick wall of cellulose in every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}.
  \item \emph{Chemistry}: tannins that make leaves indigestible,
        bitter alkaloids (caffeine, nicotine, morphine), toxins,
        resins and latex that seal wounds and poison insects; many
        are made only after an attack, and some are released as
        volatile signals that warn neighbouring leaves and attract the
        predators of the attacking insect.
  \item \emph{Tolerance and renewal}: growing points at every node,
        so that a grazed shoot regrows; dormancy through winter or
        drought; seeds that wait in the soil for years.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[A caterpillar's meal, answered]\label{ex:g12:plant-rooted-life:caterpillar}
Within hours of a caterpillar's first bites, a tomato plant makes,
throughout its leaves, \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins} that block the insect's digestive
\omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes}; within a day, a chemical it releases into the air has drawn a
parasitic wasp to the caterpillar. Neighbouring tomato plants,
receiving the same airborne signal, start making the digestion
blockers before any caterpillar reaches them. The plant does not move,
but it is not defenceless, and it is not alone.
\end{example}

\section{Growing towards, growing away}

\begin{proposition}[Growth as movement]\label{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:tropisms}
A plant moves by growing. Its shoots grow towards light and against
gravity, its roots towards gravity and water: \emph{tropisms}\index{tropism},
directed growth responses. They are controlled by \omterm{def:g11:hormones-and-reproduction:hormone}{hormones}, of which
the first identified, \emph{auxin}, is made in the growing tip of a
shoot and transported down the stem; \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} receiving more auxin
elongate more. Light falling on one side of a shoot shifts the auxin
to the shaded side, which grows faster and bends the shoot towards the
light.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Grass seedlings lit from one side bend towards the light; covered at
the tip they do not bend, though the bending region is lower down;
covered only at the base they bend normally: the tip perceives, the
base responds, and something travels between them. A tip cut off and
placed on a block of gel, then the gel placed on one side of a
decapitated seedling, makes it bend away from the block in the dark
--- the substance in the gel, auxin, is enough. Measured directly, the
shaded side of a lit seedling holds about twice the auxin of the lit
side.
\end{proof}

\section{Reproducing without moving}

\begin{proposition}[Flowers and their visitors]\label{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:flowers}
The \emph{flower} is the reproductive organ of most land plants:
\emph{stamens} that make pollen, the plant's male gametes, and a
\emph{pistil} whose ovules hold the female gametes. Since neither
parent moves, pollen must be carried from one flower to another ---
by wind, in the case of grasses and many trees, whose small, dull
flowers shed pollen by the million, or by animals, in the case of most
flowering plants, whose petals, scents and nectar are the payment
offered to the insects, birds and bats that carry pollen as they feed.
Flower and pollinator have often evolved together, each adapted to the
other's shape and habits.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Shapes that match]\label{ex:g12:plant-rooted-life:match}
A flower with a \qty{30}{cm} tube of nectar is visited by a moth with a
\qty{30}{cm} tongue, and by nothing else; a red, scentless, tubular
flower open by day is a bird's flower; a pale flower opening at night
with a heavy scent is a moth's; a bowl of yellow petals with a landing
platform is a bee's. The plant pays in sugar for a service it cannot
perform, and the pollinator's specialisation guarantees that the
pollen goes to another flower of the same \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Fruits and dispersal]\label{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:dispersal}
After fertilisation the ovule becomes a \emph{seed} --- an embryo with
a food store, dormant, in a protective coat --- and the pistil a
\emph{fruit} that carries the seeds away from the parent: on the wind
by wings and plumes; by water; on the fur of animals by hooks; and
inside animals, when a fleshy fruit is eaten and its seeds are dropped
elsewhere, unharmed, with a dose of fertiliser. The seed is the plant's
one mobile stage and its one means of waiting: it can travel
kilometres and sleep years.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Reading an adaptation to rooted life]\label{met:g12:plant-rooted-life:read}
For any feature of a plant, ask:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which need of a fixed organism does it serve --- capturing
        light or carbon dioxide, absorbing water, transporting,
        defending, orienting, dispersing pollen or seeds?
  \item What surface, structure or substance does it use, and at what
        cost (water lost through \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata}, energy spent on nectar or
        toxins)?
  \item Does it involve another organism (pollinator, disperser, root
        fungus, herbivore), and what does each partner gain?
  \item Is it built once (bark, thorns) or produced on demand (a
        toxin after attack, a bend towards light)?
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Half of life, standing still]\label{rem:g12:plant-rooted-life:half}
Plants make up most of the living mass of the Earth and feed almost
all the rest. The chapters on \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} and respiration that
follow describe their chemistry; this one has described the body
that runs it: a fixed point, from which surfaces spread into soil and
air, saps flow without a pump, chemistry replaces flight, growth
replaces movement, and animals are hired to do the travelling.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: An Oak's Summer Day}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a tree's exchanges reckoned:
leaves and stomata counted, water lifted and sugar made, the phloem's
deliveries, and the seeds sent out}]
\label{pb:g12:plant-rooted-life:1}
A mature oak carries 250\,000 leaves of \qty{25}{cm^2} each. Its lower
leaf surfaces bear 250 \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata} per square millimetre. On a summer day
each square metre of leaf transpires \qty{0.6}{L} of water and fixes
\qty{8}{g} of carbon dioxide into sugar. Leaves are 90\% water;
\omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} uses \qty{1}{g} of water per \qty{1.6}{g} of sugar
made.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Surfaces.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the total leaf area of the oak, in square metres.
  \item Compute the number of \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata} on the tree.
  \item The tree's crown covers \qty{150}{m^2} of ground. How many
        square metres of leaf are stacked above each square metre of
        ground?
  \item The \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:surfaces}{root hairs} of such a tree present about \qty{1000}{m^2} to
        the soil. Compare with the leaves and explain why the two
        surfaces are of the same order.
  \item Why must the leaf surface be spread thin and stacked, rather
        than concentrated into one thick organ?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Water.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the volume of water the oak transpires in the day.
  \item Compute the mass of sugar made, and the mass of water used in
        making it. What fraction of the transpired water is that?
  \item Explain why the tree cannot keep the rest: what would happen if
        it closed its \omterm{def:g12:plant-rooted-life:stomata}{stomata} to save it?
  \item The water rises \qty{20}{m} through the \omterm{prop:g12:plant-rooted-life:saps}{xylem}. What supplies
        the energy, and what would a pump of the tree's own have cost
        it?
  \item On a hot, dry afternoon transpiration falls to a third while
        the light is at its maximum. What has the tree done, and what
        does it cost in sugar?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Sugar.}
The oak's roots, trunk and growing shoots consume \qty{2}{kg} of sugar
a day in respiration and growth; the acorns take another \qty{0.5}{kg}
during their season.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the sugar made in the day and the fraction delivered
        to the roots, trunk and shoots, and to the acorns.
  \item Which tissue carries the sugar, and from what to what?
  \item What happens to the surplus sugar, and where can it be found
        the following spring?
  \item A ring of bark is cut in June. Which of the figures of question
