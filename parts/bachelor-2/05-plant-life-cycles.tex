\chapter{Life Cycles and Reproduction of Land Plants}\label{ch:b2:plant-life-cycles}

The green cushion of \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:moss}{moss} on a wall is one plant; the brown stalks
that rise from it in spring, each with a capsule of spores, are
another --- a different individual, with twice the chromosomes,
growing out of the first and living on it. A \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern} frond carries
spores on its underside, but the spores do not grow into \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns}: they
grow into a heart-shaped green scale a few millimetres wide, which
is where the sperm and eggs are made, and it is from that scale that
a new \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern} springs. Every land plant alternates in this way between
two bodies, one \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} and one \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid}. This chapter follows the
alternation from the algae to the seed, and shows how a sequence of
changes to it --- the shrinking of the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} body, the invention of
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen}, the enclosure of the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovule}, the seed --- freed reproduction
from water and let plants cover the continents.

\section{Three kinds of life cycle}

\begin{definition}[Haplontic, diplontic, haplo-diplontic]\label{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}
\index{life cycle}\index{alternation of generations}\index{gametophyte}\index{sporophyte}
Every sexual life cycle contains one \emph{fertilisation}, which
doubles the chromosome number, and one \emph{\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis}}, which halves
it; the cycles differ in what happens between them. In a
\emph{haplontic} cycle the zygote is the only \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid} cell and
undergoes \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} at once; the organism is \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} and its gametes
are made by mitosis (\emph{Chlamydomonas}, many algae and fungi).
In a \emph{diplontic} cycle \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} makes gametes directly, the
gametes are the only \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} cells, and the organism is \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid}
(animals, the brown alga \emph{Fucus}). In a \emph{haplo-diplontic}
cycle \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} makes \emph{spores}, which grow by mitosis into a
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} organism, the \emph{gametophyte}, that makes gametes by
mitosis; the zygote grows by mitosis into a \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid} organism, the
\emph{sporophyte}, that makes spores by \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis}. Two \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:unicellular}{multicellular}
bodies alternate --- the \emph{alternation of generations} --- and
this is the cycle of every land plant and of many algae.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[What alternation of generations means]\label{prop:b2:plant-life-cycles:alternation}
In a land plant the two generations are two organisms, not two
stages of one: the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} and the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} have different
genomes (\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} and \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid}), different bodies, often different
sizes by orders of magnitude, and each develops by mitosis from a
single cell --- a spore or a zygote. The \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} makes gametes in
\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:unicellular}{multicellular} organs, \emph{antheridia} (sperm) and \emph{archegonia}
(one egg each, in a flask whose neck the sperm swims down); the
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} makes spores in \emph{sporangia}. Across the land plants
the balance shifts: in mosses the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} is the plant and the
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} a dependent stalk; in \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns} the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} is the plant
and the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} a free-living scale; in seed plants the
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} is reduced to a few cells hidden inside the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte}'s
tissues --- the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} grain and the contents of the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovule}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Hofmeister (1851) germinated the spores of mosses, \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns},
horsetails and clubmosses, followed the development of the small
green bodies they produced, found on them the antheridia and
archegonia, and watched the embryo grow from the fertilised egg
inside the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:moss}{archegonium} into the spore-bearing plant. He then showed
that the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovule} of a conifer contains the same structures, reduced
--- archegonia within a tissue that is a retained \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} --- and
so that mosses, \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns} and seed plants are one series with one cycle.
The chromosome counts that explained the two generations (Strasburger,
1894) came forty years later.
\end{proof}

\section{Mosses: the gametophyte is the plant}

\begin{definition}[The moss life cycle]\label{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:moss}
\index{moss}\index{protonema}\index{archegonium}\index{antheridium}
A moss \emph{spore} germinates into a branching green filament, the
\emph{protonema}, from which buds grow into the leafy shoots --- the
\emph{\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte}}, a few centimetres tall, without true roots,
xylem or phloem, anchored by rhizoids and drawing water over its
whole surface. At the shoot tips it bears \emph{antheridia}, which
release biflagellate sperm into a film of rain or dew, and
\emph{archegonia}, each with one egg at the base of a neck; the
sperm swim a few centimetres at most, guided by chemical attractants,
and fertilise the egg in place. The zygote grows into the
\emph{\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte}}: a foot embedded in the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte}, a stalk
(\emph{seta}), and a \emph{capsule} in which \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} makes tens of
thousands of spores, shed through a ring of hygroscopic teeth that
open in dry air. The \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} photosynthesises a little but is fed
by the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} through its foot and never lives alone.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The cost of swimming sperm]\label{ex:b2:plant-life-cycles:sperm}
A \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:moss}{moss} sperm swims at about \qty{100}{\micro m/s}; to reach an
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:moss}{archegonium} \qty{2}{cm} away it needs \qty{200}{s} of continuous
water film --- a splash of rain, a heavy dew. Fertilisation is
therefore confined to wet weather and to short distances, and most
mosses, \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns} and their relatives live in damp places or reproduce
in the wet season; the antheridia of some mosses sit in a splash cup
whose shape throws raindrops, and the sperm they carry, half a metre.
It is this dependence that the seed plants escaped.
\end{example}

\section{Ferns: the sporophyte is the plant}

\begin{definition}[The fern life cycle]\label{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}
\index{fern}\index{prothallus}\index{sorus}\index{sporangium}
The fern is the \emph{\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte}}: a rhizome with roots, xylem and
phloem, and fronds. On the underside of fertile fronds, clusters of
\emph{sporangia} (\emph{sori}, often under a protective flap) each
make 64 spores by \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} and fling them by the snap of a drying
ring of thick-walled cells. A spore that lands on damp soil grows
into a \emph{prothallus}: a heart-shaped green \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} a few
millimetres across, one cell thick, with rhizoids, living free for a
few weeks. It bears antheridia and archegonia on its underside; sperm
swim in the soil film to the egg; the zygote grows into a young
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} that draws its first food from the prothallus and then
roots, and the prothallus dies. The two generations are both
independent, but unequal: the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} lives for decades and can
be metres tall, the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} for weeks and millimetres.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Dispersal of a spore]\label{thm:b2:plant-life-cycles:dispersal}
\index{spore dispersal}
A spore of radius $r$ and density $\rho_s$ released at height $h$
into a horizontal wind of speed $u$ falls at the Stokes terminal
speed $v_s = 2r^2(\rho_s - \rho_{\text{air}})g/9\eta_{\text{air}}$
and lands, in still-layered air, at a distance
\[
x = \frac{u\,h}{v_s}
\]
from the plant. A \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern} spore of radius \qty{15}{\micro m} and
density \qty{1100}{kg/m^3} ($\eta_{\text{air}} = \qty{1.8e-5}{Pa.s}$)
falls at \qty{3}{cm/s}; from a frond at \qty{0.5}{m} in a wind of
\qty{2}{m/s} it travels about \qty{30}{m}, and in the turbulent air
of a windy day, which lifts a fraction of the spores far above the
release height, a few travel hundreds of kilometres --- which is why
the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern} floras of oceanic islands are rich and their seed-plant
floras poor.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The fall time is $h/v_s$ (the spore reaches its terminal speed in
microseconds), during which the wind carries it $u\,h/v_s$. The
terminal speed follows from balancing the Stokes drag $6\pi\eta r
v_s$ against the weight minus buoyancy $\tfrac43\pi r^3(\rho_s -
\rho_{\text{air}})g$, as in \cref{ch:b2:unicellular-diversity}.
\end{proof}

\section{Seed plants: the gametophyte is hidden}

\begin{definition}[Heterospory, pollen, ovule]\label{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}
\index{heterospory}\index{pollen}\index{ovule}\index{microspore}\index{megaspore}
Mosses and most \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns} are \emph{homosporous}: one kind of spore, one
kind of \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} bearing both sexes. Seed plants (and a few
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns} and clubmosses) are \emph{heterosporous}: \emph{microspores},
small and many, grow into male \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophytes}; \emph{megaspores}, large
and few, into female ones. In seed plants both are reduced to
almost nothing and neither leaves the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte}'s tissues on its
own. The male \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} is the \emph{pollen grain}: a microspore
that has divided two or three times inside its wall, carried by wind
or animals to the female organ, where it grows a \emph{pollen tube}
and delivers its sperm --- no water needed. The female \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte}
develops inside the megasporangium, which stays on the parent
enclosed in one or two \emph{integuments} with a pore, the
\emph{micropyle}: the whole structure is the \emph{ovule}. After
fertilisation the ovule becomes the \emph{seed}: an embryo
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte}, a store of food, and a coat made from the integuments,
dormant until conditions are right.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The conifer cycle]\label{prop:b2:plant-life-cycles:conifer}
\index{conifer}\index{cone}\index{seed}
A pine bears two kinds of cone. Small \emph{male cones}, in clusters
in spring, hold microsporangia in which \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} makes \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{microspores};
each divides into a four-celled \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} grain with two air bladders
and is shed by the million into the wind. \emph{Female cones} carry
two \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovules} on the upper face of each scale. \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{Pollen} blown between the
scales is drawn to the micropyle by a drop of fluid; the female
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte}, which has not yet developed, then grows over the
following year from the single surviving \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{megaspore} into a tissue of
a few thousand cells with two or three archegonia; a \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} tube
grows slowly through the nucellus and, fifteen months after
pollination, delivers a sperm to an egg. The embryo develops in the
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte}, which becomes the food store of the seed; the seed,
winged, is shed in the third autumn when the cone opens. Seeds take
years, and cost a great deal; in return they are dispersed dry,
survive winter and drought, and carry the seedling's first weeks
of food.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Pollen in the wind]\label{ex:b2:plant-life-cycles:pollen}
A pine \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} grain, \qty{50}{\micro m} across with two bladders,
falls at about \qty{3}{cm/s}; a large tree releases some
$10^{11}$ grains in a season, enough to coat ponds yellow. The
chance that one grain lands on a given \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovule} is tiny, and the
strategy works only in numbers: wind pollination is cheap per
grain, costly in grains, and confined to plants that grow in stands
of their own species --- conifers, grasses, oaks. The flowering
plants (\cref{ch:b2:angiosperm-reproduction}) found a way to
deliver \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} by animals, one grain at a time to the right address.
\end{example}

\section{Trends across the land plants}

\begin{proposition}[Four trends]\label{prop:b2:plant-life-cycles:trends}
\index{reduction of the gametophyte}
From mosses to \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns} to seed plants: (1) the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} grows from a
dependent stalk to the whole plant, and the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} shrinks from
the plant to a scale to a few cells; (2) homospory gives way to
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{heterospory}, and the female \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} stays on the parent;
(3) fertilisation passes from swimming sperm in surface water to a
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} tube, so that reproduction no longer needs rain; (4) the
dispersal unit passes from the spore --- a single cell with a day's
reserves --- to the seed, an embryo with food and a coat, which can
wait. The \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid} generation is favoured on land because a \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid}
body masks \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive} \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations}, can grow larger and longer-lived
with a vascular system, and because a spore made by \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} on a tall
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} travels further than a gamete swimming from a low
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte}. The trend continues into the flowering plants, where
the female \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophyte} is seven cells and the seed is wrapped in a
fruit.
\end{proposition}

\section{Problem: From Spore to Seed}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the reproductive arithmetic of
a fern and a pine compared, from the number of spores and pollen
grains to their dispersal, the fate of gametophytes, and the cost of
a seed, ending on the number of propagules each plant must make for
one replacement}]
\label{pb:b2:plant-life-cycles:1}
A \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern} plant bears \num{10} fertile fronds, each with \num{200}
sori of \num{40} sporangia making \num{64} spores each. Spores:
radius \qty{15}{\micro m}, density \qty{1100}{kg/m^3}. A pine
produces $2\times 10^{4}$ female cones' worth of \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovules} over its
life --- take \num{100} \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovules} per cone --- and $10^{11}$ \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen}
grains per year. Seeds: \qty{5}{mg}, \qty{50}{\%} dry matter at
\qty{20}{kJ/g}. Air: $\eta = \qty{1.8e-5}{Pa.s}$, $\rho =
\qty{1.2}{kg/m^3}$; $g = \qty{9.81}{m/s^2}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern}'s spores.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the number of spores the plant sheds in a season.
  \item Compute the mass of one spore and of the whole crop.
  \item Compute the spore's settling speed in air.
  \item From fronds at \qty{0.5}{m} in a \qty{1.5}{m/s} wind, how far
        do the spores travel? Over how large an area (a disc of that
        radius) are they spread, and how many land per square metre?
  \item Explain why turbulence carries a few spores much further,
        and what this implies for the colonisation of a new island.
  \item Compute the energy content of one spore (\qty{50}{\%} dry
        matter at \qty{20}{kJ/g}) and of the whole crop, and compare
        with the energy of one pine seed.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{gametophytes}.}
One spore in $2\times 10^{4}$ lands on soil damp enough to grow into
a \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{prothallus}; a \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{prothallus} lives \num{6} weeks and needs a wet
night --- probability $0.2$ per week --- for fertilisation; a
fertilised \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{prothallus} gives a young \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} with probability
$0.5$; a young \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:cycles}{sporophyte} survives to adulthood with probability
$0.02$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many prothalli does the plant's crop produce?
  \item Compute the probability that a \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{prothallus} experiences at
        least one wet night in its six weeks.
  \item Compute the expected number of adult \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns} produced by the
        crop of one season.
  \item If the parent lives \num{30} years, how many adult offspring
        does it leave, and what does this say about the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern}
        population?
  \item A \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:moss}{moss} sperm swims at \qty{100}{\micro m/s} and a \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{prothallus}'s
        archegonia lie \qty{1}{mm} from its own antheridia. How long
        does self-fertilisation take? Why do many \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{ferns} nevertheless
        avoid it (give one mechanism)?
  \item In what sense is the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{prothallus} the weak link of the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern}
        cycle?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The pine's \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} grain is a sphere of radius \qty{25}{\micro m} of
        effective density \qty{600}{kg/m^3} (air bladders included).
        Compute its settling speed.
  \item From a cone at \qty{15}{m} in a \qty{3}{m/s} wind, how far
        does it travel?
  \item The $10^{11}$ grains are spread through \qty{1}{km^2} of
        forest to a depth of \qty{20}{m}. Compute the concentration.
  \item A micropyle presents an opening of \qty{0.1}{mm^2} to a wind
        of \qty{2}{m/s}. Compute the number of grains entering it per
        hour, and the time to receive the first grain.
  \item Explain why a lone pine \qty{5}{km} from the forest sets
        almost no seed, and why wind-pollinated species grow in
        stands.
  \item Compare the numbers: how many \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen} grains does the forest
        make per \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovule}, if it holds \num{1000} pines with \num{2000}
        \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovules} each?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The pine's seeds.}
Of the tree's \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{ovules}, \qty{60}{\%} are pollinated and \qty{80}{\%} of
those fill a seed; a seed becomes a seedling with probability $0.05$
and a seedling an adult with probability $0.01$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the number of seeds the tree makes in its life, and
        their total mass and energy.
  \item A winged seed descends at \qty{0.8}{m/s}. From \qty{20}{m} in
        a \qty{3}{m/s} wind, how far does it travel? Compare with the
        \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen}.
  \item Compute the expected number of adult offspring, and compare
        with the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern}'s.
  \item Compute the energy the tree invests per adult offspring, and
        the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern} per adult offspring (spores only), and compare.
  \item The \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern} spore can wait a few weeks; the pine seed several
        years. Explain, with the energy figures, why the seed can
        afford dormancy and the spore cannot.
  \item Give two advantages that make the seed worth its cost and two
        conditions under which the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern}'s strategy is the better one.
  \item State the result: propagules per adult offspring for the
        \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:fern}{fern} and for the pine, and the energy each spends per adult
        offspring.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
