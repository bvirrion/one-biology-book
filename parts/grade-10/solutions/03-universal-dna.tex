\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:4}
A \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is a segment of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} whose sequence carries the instructions for
one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}, at a fixed position on a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome}. An \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is one of
the versions of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, differing from the others by a few bases of
its sequence.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:5}
An organism into whose \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} from another species has been
inserted, and which reads it and transmits it. Examples: mice carrying
the jellyfish GFP \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}; \emph{E.~coli} carrying the human insulin \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:6}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:7}
$2 \times \num{3.2e9} \times \qty{0.34}{nm} \approx \qty{2.2}{m}$,

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:8}
A "purified" fraction might still contain traces of other molecules,
so its activity alone does not prove that \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is responsible.
Destroying \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} specifically, in the complete extract, and seeing the
activity vanish while protein- and RNA-destroying enzymes leave it
intact, pins the effect on \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} and on nothing else.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:9}
A fixed repeat would give the same composition (25\% of each base) in
every species. Chargaff found A+T ranging widely between species: the
order of bases is not a fixed pattern, so the sequence is free to
vary --- which is what makes it capable of carrying information.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:10}
The sequence is read in order to build the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}; a changed base at
a position that specifies an \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid} can substitute one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid}
for another and alter the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}'s shape or activity. If the changed
base lies in a part of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} that is not read, or specifies the
same \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid}, the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} is unchanged.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:11}
All the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} of the mouse descend by division from the fertilised
egg; at each division the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, transgene included, is copied
(\omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:helix}{complementarity}) and shared. Skin, liver and sperm \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} therefore
all carry the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} --- and the sperm passes it to the next generation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:12}
$\num{4.6e6} \times \qty{0.34}{nm} \approx \qty{1.6}{mm}$, some 800
times the length of the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}. But the molecule is only \qty{2}{nm}
wide: its volume, about $\pi \times 1^2 \times \num{1.6e6}\,
\unit{nm^3} \approx \num{5e-3}\,\unit{\micro m^3}$, is about 1\% of the
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s; folded and coiled, it fits with room to spare.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:13}
Human \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is 700 times longer but carries only about 5 times more
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}: most of it is not \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}. Part of the extra \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} regulates when
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} are used, part is repeated sequence with no known function. Base
count measures the size of the text, not the complexity of the
organism; the number of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} and, above all, how they are combined
and regulated matter more.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:14}
It would contradict the universality of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}
(\cref{prop:g10:universal-dna:universal}). Since a shared molecule and
code are inherited from a common ancestor, such a species would have to
descend from a separate origin of life: its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} could not be read
by our \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} and \omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:universal}{transgenesis} between the two lines would be
impossible.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:universal-dna:15}
The bacterial product is the exact human \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} (pig insulin differs
by one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid} and can provoke immune reactions), and it is made in
unlimited quantity, free of pig pathogens. A regulator would still ask
whether the purified \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} is free of bacterial contaminants and
whether it is correctly folded and active.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g10:universal-dna:1}
\textbf{1.} $720 \times 0.34 \approx \qty{245}{nm}$; $720/10 = 72$
turns.

\textbf{2.} \texttt{TACTCATTTCCTCTTCTTG}.

\textbf{3.} T $= 27\%$; G $=$ C $= (100 - 54)/2 = 23\%$.

\textbf{4.} $720/238 \approx 3.0$: three bases per \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid} (the
last three signal the end of the chain).

\textbf{5.} $4^{720}$ sequences, about $10^{433}$. The chemistry allows
any order, so the particular order chosen among all these
possibilities is a message --- information.

\textbf{6.} Universality: every living thing uses the same four
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} and reads a sequence by the same rules.

\textbf{7.} All the mouse's \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} descend from the egg by division, so
a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} inserted in the egg is copied into every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}. Inserted into
adult skin, it would be present only in the treated skin \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} and
their descendants.

\textbf{8.} Every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} type contains the whole \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, transgene
included: specialisation does not discard \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}.

\textbf{9.} Yes: the germ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} carry the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}. If the mouse has one
copy on one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome} of a pair, about half its offspring inherit it
and glow.

\textbf{10.} The \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} is not information: it is used up and never
renewed, so the glow fades. The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is copied at every division and
read continuously, so the glow is permanent and inherited.

\textbf{11.} The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} fraction; treatment with an enzyme that destroys
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}.

\textbf{12.} Bacteria took up \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} from another strain, integrated it
and expressed and transmitted the trait: a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} transferred from one
organism to another, which is what \omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:universal}{transgenesis} does deliberately.

\textbf{13.} A$\,=\,$T and G$\,=\,$C support pairing; the variable
A+T share shows that the order of the pairs is free.

\textbf{14.} A and G are large two-ring bases, T and C small one-ring
bases: a large paired with a small one always spans the same distance,
so the two backbones stay \qty{2}{nm} apart along the whole molecule.
Two large bases would bulge, two small ones leave a gap.

\textbf{15.} Because each strand is the complement of the other:
separate them, build a partner on each by the pairing rule, and two
identical molecules result.

\textbf{16.} $2 \times \num{2.7e9} \times \qty{0.34}{nm} \approx
\qty{1.8}{m}$.

\textbf{17.} $\qty{1.8}{m} / \qty{6}{\micro m} = \num{3e5}$: a
three-hundred-thousand-fold compaction.

\textbf{18.} $720 / \num{5.4e9} \approx \num{1.3e-7}$: about one
ten-millionth of the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}.

\textbf{19.} $\num{2e4} \times \num{3e4} = \num{6e8}$ base pairs, about
22\% of the genome; the rest lies between \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}.

\textbf{20.} A mouse \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} carries about \qty{1.8}{m} of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}; because
that \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is built and read exactly as the jellyfish's, the one foreign
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} among twenty thousand is read like the others.
\end{solution}
