\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g10:common-ancestry} --- Body Plans and Common Ancestry}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:1}
Bilateral symmetry with a front and a back end; an internal skeleton
with a vertebral column; a dorsal nerve cord above it; a ventral
digestive tube with the heart beneath it near the front (and paired
limbs or fins).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:2}
Organs with the same structure and position in the \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{body plan}, whatever
their function. The tetrapod forelimb: humerus, radius and ulna, wrist
bones, digits --- in an arm, a wing, a flipper.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:3}
Analogous: same function (flight), entirely different structure ---
bones, muscles and skin in the bird, a membrane of the external
skeleton in the butterfly. They say nothing about kinship.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:4}
The lizard: the smallest box containing frog and lizard is "tetrapods";
the trout is only in the larger "\omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates}" box.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:5}
Fossils with intermediate combinations of characters; the shared
stages of \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrate} embryos; the agreement of molecular sequence
comparisons with anatomical groupings.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:6}
Mammals (hair traces, milk; tetrapod limb; air breathing and, in
mammals, an amniotic development). The hip bones are the remnant of
hind limbs: whales descend from four-legged land mammals.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:7}
The crocodile goes into "amniotes" with lizard and pigeon (not into
"mammals"); the salamander into "tetrapods" outside "amniotes", with
the frog.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:8}
A kinship group must contain all the descendants of its common
ancestor. The ancestor common to trout and lungfish is also the
ancestor of the frog and of every tetrapod; a group holding trout and
lungfish but excluding the frog leaves out some descendants. "Fish"
names a way of life, not a lineage.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:9}
Chimpanzee (0), mouse (9), chicken (13), frog (18), tuna (21), yeast
(45): mammal, amniote, tetrapod, \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrate}, eukaryote --- the boxes
in order.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:10}
Structures kept in adult fish appear transiently in our embryos and are
reworked: the gill pouches become parts of the ear, throat and glands,
the tail is resorbed. An embryo running through an ancestral stage is
what inheritance from a common ancestor predicts and what design for
a purpose does not.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:11}
If birds descend from reptile-like ancestors, some ancestor must have
had reptile characters and the first bird characters at once; finding
one dated between the two groups is a confirmed prediction. Without
common ancestry, a creature mixing the traits of two separate kinds
has no reason to exist.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:12}
Analogous: same function and similar general design, but different
embryonic origin and reversed structure. \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:homology}{Homology} would imply the
common ancestor of molluscs and \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates} already had a camera eye;
analogy implies it had at most a simple light-sensitive patch, and the
two lineages built cameras separately.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:13}
Human and mouse share a common ancestor more recent than either shares
with the chicken; both lineages have been separate from the chicken's
for the same length of time, so both have accumulated about the same
number of changes relative to it. The human--chicken and mouse--chicken
distances measure the same interval.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:14}
Snakes have the amniotic egg, the skeleton and skull of lizards, and
some (boas, pythons) keep vestiges of hind limbs and a pelvis; fossil
snakes with small legs exist. "Tetrapod" means descended from the
four-limbed ancestor, whether or not the limbs are kept.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:common-ancestry:15}
Way of life explains a flipper's shape, not why a whale's flipper
contains the bones of an arm arranged like a bat's --- a flat paddle
does not need a wrist. It does not explain gill pouches in a human
embryo, which has no use for them. And it does not explain why a
respiratory \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}, invisible to the environment, differs between
species by exactly the amounts the skeletons predict. Only inheritance
from common ancestors accounts for all three.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g10:common-ancestry:1}
\textbf{1.} The vertebral column: the \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{vertebrates}.

\textbf{2.} Four limbs defines frog, lizard, pigeon, mouse: the
tetrapods. The amniotic egg defines lizard, pigeon, mouse: the
amniotes.

\textbf{3.} No: a character present in one species groups nothing. It
identifies the species (and would define a group if more relatives
--- other birds, other mammals --- were added).

\textbf{4.} \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:bodyplan}{Vertebrates} $\supset$ tetrapods $\supset$ amniotes, with the
trout outside the second box and the frog outside the third; the
lizard, pigeon and mouse inside the third.

\textbf{5.} The frog's: same bones, same order. It corresponds to the
"four limbs" character, which the fin lacks.

\textbf{6.} Lizard and pigeon (12). The trout (19--21 from all).

\textbf{7.} Pigeon (13), lizard (14), frog (18), trout (21).

\textbf{8.} Lizard (16), pigeon (17), mouse (18), trout (19): the
amniotes at about equal distance, the trout beyond --- consistent with
the boxes (the frog is closer to every amniote than to the trout).

\textbf{9.} That lizard and pigeon separated from each other after
their lineage had separated from the mouse's: within the amniotes,
lizard and pigeon are closer kin. The table cannot tell: no character
in it is shared by lizard and pigeon alone.

\textbf{10.} The trout's lineage separated from the common lineage of
the four others before those separated from one another: each has
been apart from the trout for the same time, so each has accumulated
about the same number of differences.

\textbf{11.} Trout outside all the others; the four tetrapods together;
the three amniotes together within them.

\textbf{12.} Anatomy and a respiratory \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} have no reason to agree
unless both record the same history. Two independent witnesses telling
the same story make an accidental resemblance implausible.

\textbf{13.} Into "mammals", beside the mouse (hair, milk, and the
smallest molecular distance). Its wing is a modified tetrapod
forelimb, analogous to the pigeon's, not \omterm{def:g10:common-ancestry:homology}{homologous} with feathered
flight; the molecule (13 from the pigeon, 8 from the mouse) confirms.

\textbf{14.} Cytochrome c changes too slowly to separate close
relatives, and it is not the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} responsible for visible differences.
It informs about deep kinship, not about the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} that make a species
what it is.

\textbf{15.} The eukaryotes: \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} with a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus} and \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria},
sharing this respiratory \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}.

\textbf{16.} The 13 differences were accumulated by two lineages over
320 million years: $13/2 \approx 6.5$ per lineage, i.e. about 2 per
100 million years along one lineage.

\textbf{17.} Frog--mouse 18, frog--pigeon 17: average 17.5, i.e. 8.75
per lineage; at 2 per 100 million years, about 430 million years.

\textbf{18.} Trout average about 20: 10 per lineage, about 500 million
years.

\textbf{19.} The estimates (430, 500) are older than the fossil dates
(370, over 420) but of the right order and in the right sequence; the
rate is not exactly constant and the counts are rounded, so agreement
within 20\% is as good as the method allows.

\textbf{20.} Both establish the same nested kinship --- trout, then
frog, then the three amniotes --- while the molecule adds a clock: an
estimate of how long ago each branch separated.
\end{solution}
