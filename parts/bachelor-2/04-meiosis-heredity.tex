\chapter{Meiosis, Genetic Mixing and Heredity}\label{ch:b2:meiosis-heredity}

Two brown-eyed parents have a blue-eyed child, and nobody is
surprised; two children of the same parents are never alike, and
nobody is surprised either. Both facts follow from one process. At
the making of every egg and every sperm, a cell with two copies of
each chromosome hands on exactly one, chosen at random, after
letting the two copies trade segments. Every fertilisation then
brings together two such half-sets that have never met. This
chapter describes that process --- \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} --- and the rules of
inheritance that follow from it: Mendel's ratios, their exceptions,
the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{linkage} of genes that share a chromosome and the maps that
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{linkage} draws, and the peculiar inheritance of the \omterm{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:sexlinked}{sex chromosomes}
and of the organelles.

\section{Meiosis}

\begin{definition}[Meiosis]\label{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}
\index{meiosis}\index{haploid}\index{diploid}
A \emph{diploid} cell ($2n$) carries two \emph{homologous}
chromosomes of each kind, one from each parent, alike in gene
content but not necessarily in \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles}. \emph{Meiosis} is the pair
of divisions that turns one diploid cell into four \emph{haploid}
cells ($n$), each with one chromosome of each kind. It is preceded
by one replication, so that each chromosome enters as two sister
chromatids; the first division separates \emph{homologues}
(reductional), the second separates \emph{sister chromatids}
(equational), as mitosis does. In animals meiosis makes gametes; in
plants and fungi it makes spores (\cref{ch:b2:plant-life-cycles});
in every sexual organism it is the step that halves the chromosome
number so that fertilisation can double it again.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The stages]\label{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:stages}
\index{prophase I}\index{synapsis}\index{chiasma}\index{crossing-over}
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:stages}{Prophase I}} is long and is where the mixing happens. The
replicated chromosomes condense; each finds its homologue and
pairs with it gene for gene (\emph{synapsis}), held by a protein
ladder, the \emph{synaptonemal complex}, to form a \emph{bivalent}
of four chromatids; non-sister chromatids break and rejoin at
matching points (\emph{crossing-over}), one to several times per
bivalent; when the complex dissolves, the homologues stay attached
only at these exchange points, visible as \emph{chiasmata}.
\emph{Metaphase I}: bivalents line up on the spindle, each pair
oriented at random --- the maternal homologue toward one pole or
the other independently of every other pair. \emph{Anaphase I}:
homologues separate, sister chromatids staying together; each pole
receives $n$ chromosomes of two chromatids each. \emph{Telophase I}
and, usually without replication, \emph{\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} II}: the $n$
chromosomes line up singly, sister chromatids separate, and four
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{haploid} nuclei result. \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{Meiosis} I takes hours to decades (a human
oocyte pauses in \omterm{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:stages}{prophase I} from before birth until ovulation);
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} II takes an hour.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[How much mixing meiosis makes]\label{thm:b2:meiosis-heredity:mixing}
\index{independent assortment}\index{genetic recombination}
For an organism with $n$ pairs of chromosomes, the random
orientation of the bivalents at metaphase I produces $2^n$ possible
combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes in a gamete ---
$2^{23} \approx 8.4$ million in a human --- and $2^{2n}$ in a
zygote. Crossing-over multiplies this: with about $k$ chiasmata per
bivalent, each at a variable position, the number of distinct
gametes is effectively unbounded, and no two gametes of one person
are ever the same. \emph{Recombination} --- new combinations of
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} --- arises from both: \omterm{thm:b2:meiosis-heredity:mixing}{independent assortment} reshuffles
whole chromosomes, crossing-over reshuffles genes within a
chromosome.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each bivalent orients independently with two outcomes, so $n$
bivalents give $2^n$ combinations, all equally likely. Each of two
parents contributes one such gamete, so the zygote has $2^n\times
2^n$. A crossover at a distinct position in a chromosome of $L$
base pairs can produce of order $L$ distinct recombinant chromatids
per bivalent, so the count of distinct products grows as $L^k$ per
chromosome, which is astronomical for $L \sim 10^8$.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Creighton and McClintock (1931) used a maize chromosome 9 that
carried two visible cytological marks (a knob at one end, a
translocated segment at the other) and two genes between them
(coloured/colourless kernel, waxy/starchy endosperm). Among the
offspring, every plant with a recombined pair of \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} also had a
recombined pair of cytological marks --- knob without translocation
or the reverse --- proving that \omterm{thm:b2:meiosis-heredity:mixing}{genetic recombination} is a physical
exchange of chromosome segments.
\end{proof}

\section{Mendelian analysis}

\begin{definition}[The vocabulary of crosses]\label{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}
\index{allele}\index{genotype}\index{phenotype}\index{dominant}\index{recessive}\index{homozygous}\index{heterozygous}
A \emph{gene} is a stretch of chromosome; its \emph{alleles} are its
sequence variants; the \emph{locus} is its position. A \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{diploid}
individual carries two alleles at each locus: its \emph{genotype}
is \emph{homozygous} ($AA$, $aa$) or \emph{heterozygous} ($Aa$).
The \emph{phenotype} is the observable character. An allele is
\emph{dominant} when the heterozygote shows its phenotype,
\emph{recessive} when it shows only in the homozygote; the
distinction is a property of the pair of alleles and of the
character observed, not of the allele alone --- most recessive
alleles are loss-of-function alleles, hidden because one working
copy suffices. A \emph{pure line} breeds true (homozygous); a
\emph{test cross} mates an individual of dominant phenotype with a
recessive homozygote, so that its offspring reveal the alleles of
its gametes directly.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Mendel's ratios]\label{thm:b2:meiosis-heredity:mendel}
\index{Mendel's laws}\index{monohybrid cross}\index{dihybrid cross}
Because the two \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} of a heterozygote $Aa$ segregate into
different gametes in equal numbers (anaphase I), a \emph{monohybrid}
cross $Aa \times Aa$ gives \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotypes} $\tfrac14 AA : \tfrac12 Aa :
\tfrac14 aa$, \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{phenotypes} $3 : 1$ if $A$ is \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{dominant}; the test cross
$Aa \times aa$ gives $1 : 1$. Because \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} of genes on different
chromosomes assort independently (metaphase I), a \emph{dihybrid}
$AaBb$ makes four kinds of gamete in equal numbers and the cross
$AaBb \times AaBb$ gives \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{phenotypes} $9 : 3 : 3 : 1$, the test cross
$AaBb \times aabb$ gives $1 : 1 : 1 : 1$. For $k$ independent
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{heterozygous} loci, the number of gamete types is $2^k$ and the
phenotypic ratio is the product of $k$ ratios $3 : 1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Segregation: the heterozygote's gametes are $\tfrac12 A$,
$\tfrac12 a$; with random fertilisation the zygote \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotypes} are the
products $(\tfrac12 A + \tfrac12 a)^2 = \tfrac14 AA + \tfrac12 Aa +
\tfrac14 aa$. Independence: the gametes of $AaBb$ are $(\tfrac12 A
+ \tfrac12 a)(\tfrac12 B + \tfrac12 b)$, four kinds at $\tfrac14$;
the dihybrid \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{phenotypes} multiply $(\tfrac34 A\_ + \tfrac14 aa)
(\tfrac34 B\_ + \tfrac14 bb)$, giving $\tfrac{9}{16}, \tfrac{3}{16},
\tfrac{3}{16}, \tfrac{1}{16}$. Each factor is one independent
segregation, so $k$ loci give a product of $k$ factors.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Mendel (1866) crossed pure lines of pea differing in one character
--- round or wrinkled seed, yellow or green cotyledon, purple or
white flower, tall or dwarf stem and three others --- and found in
every case a uniform first generation and a second generation
segregating close to $3 : 1$: 5474 round to 1850 wrinkled ($2.96 :
1$), 6022 yellow to 2001 green ($3.01 : 1$), 705 purple to 224 white
($3.15 : 1$). In two-character crosses he found $315 : 108 : 101 :
32$ ($9 : 3 : 3 : 1$), and by growing on the second generation he
showed that the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{dominant} class was of two kinds, one third pure and
two thirds segregating. The chromosomes that explain the ratios were
found forty years later.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Testing a ratio with $\chi^2$]\label{met:b2:meiosis-heredity:chisquare}
\index{chi-square test}
To decide whether observed counts $O_i$ fit a hypothesised ratio:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the expected counts $E_i$ from the ratio and the
        total.
  \item Compute $\chi^2 = \sum_i (O_i - E_i)^2/E_i$.
  \item Count the degrees of freedom: the number of classes minus
        one.
  \item Compare with the critical value at the \qty{5}{\%} level:
        $3.84$ for 1 degree of freedom, $5.99$ for 2, $7.81$ for 3,
        $9.49$ for 4. If $\chi^2$ is below it, the data are
        consistent with the ratio; if above, the ratio is rejected
        --- the deviation would arise by chance in fewer than one
        experiment in twenty.
\end{enumerate}
For Mendel's flowers, $705 : 224$ against $3 : 1$: $E = 696.75,
232.25$; $\chi^2 = 0.098 + 0.293 = 0.39 < 3.84$. (The distribution
behind the critical values is derived in the statistics of the
mathematics series; here the table is a tool.)
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[Beyond the simple ratios]\label{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:extensions}
\index{incomplete dominance}\index{codominance}\index{epistasis}\index{lethal allele}
The ratios change, but the mechanism does not, when: \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} show
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:extensions}{incomplete dominance}} (a red $\times$ white snapdragon gives
pink, and the second generation $1 : 2 : 1$); \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} are
\emph{codominant}, both expressed ($AB$ blood: the heterozygote
$I^AI^B$ makes both antigens; a locus may have \emph{multiple
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles}}, $I^A$, $I^B$, $i$); an \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{allele} is \emph{lethal} when
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{homozygous} (yellow mice: yellow $\times$ yellow gives $2$ yellow to
$1$ agouti, the $\tfrac14 YY$ dying as embryos); two genes act in
one pathway (\emph{epistasis}: in a $9 : 3 : 3 : 1$ cross the double
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive} and one single \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive} may be indistinguishable, giving
$9 : 3 : 4$, as for albino mice, or the two \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{dominants} may both be
needed for the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{phenotype}, giving $9 : 7$, as for purple sweet peas
that need two enzymes to make their pigment); many genes each add a
little to a continuous character (height, yield), which is the
quantitative genetics of \cref{ch:b2:population-genetics}. Reading
a ratio back to a mechanism is the everyday craft of genetics.
\end{proposition}

\section{Linkage and genetic maps}

\begin{definition}[Linkage and recombination frequency]\label{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}
\index{linkage}\index{recombination frequency}\index{centimorgan}
Two genes on the same chromosome are \emph{linked}: their \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles}
tend to travel together into the gametes, and a dihybrid
$AB/ab$ makes more \emph{parental} gametes ($AB$, $ab$) than
\emph{recombinant} ones ($Ab$, $aB$). Recombinants arise only when a
crossover falls between the two loci, so their frequency $r$ ---
counted directly in a test cross --- measures the distance: $r$
ranges from 0 (complete linkage) to $\tfrac12$ (genes so far apart,
or on different chromosomes, that they assort independently). The
\emph{map distance} unit is the \emph{centimorgan} (cM): \qty{1}{cM}
is \qty{1}{\%} recombination for closely linked genes; in humans
\qty{1}{cM} is about a million base pairs. Linkage was the first
proof that genes lie in a line on the chromosome.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Morgan (1911) found that the fly's white-eye and miniature-wing
genes, both on the X chromosome, did not assort independently: a
test cross gave \qty{37}{\%} recombinants instead of \qty{50}{\%},
and other pairs gave other, reproducible, percentages. Sturtevant
(1913), as an undergraduate, realised that if the frequencies
measure distances they should add: from the pairwise
frequencies of six X-linked genes he drew the first genetic map, and
the distances were, within the error, additive along a line.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Mapping two linked genes]\label{met:b2:meiosis-heredity:twopoint}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Cross two pure lines to get the dihybrid; note which
        \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} it received together (its parental combinations,
        \emph{coupling} $AB/ab$ or \emph{repulsion} $Ab/aB$).
  \item Test-cross it to the double \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive} and classify the
        offspring: the two most numerous classes are parental, the
        two least numerous recombinant.
  \item $r = $ recombinants $/$ total; the distance is $100\,r$ cM.
  \item Check independence first: if the four classes are equal
        ($\chi^2$ against $1 : 1 : 1 : 1$), the genes are unlinked.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{theorem}[Haldane's mapping function]\label{thm:b2:meiosis-heredity:haldane}
\index{mapping function}
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{Recombination frequency} underestimates distance for genes far
apart, because two crossovers between them restore the parental
combination. If crossovers fall independently along the chromosome
(no interference), with $d$ the map distance in Morgans (expected
number of crossovers per chromatid), the recombination fraction is
\[
r = \tfrac12\,\bigl(1 - e^{-2d}\bigr), \qquad d = -\tfrac12\ln(1 - 2r),
\]
so that $r \approx d$ for small $d$ and $r \to \tfrac12$ as $d$
grows; $r = 0.40$ corresponds to \qty{80}{cM}, and \qty{50}{cM}
gives $r = 0.32$. Real chromosomes show \emph{interference} --- a
crossover makes another nearby less likely --- which brings $r$
closer to $d$ than Haldane predicts over short distances.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let the map distance $d$ be the expected number of crossovers per
chromatid in the interval; since every crossover involves two of the
four chromatids, the bivalent as a whole carries a Poisson number of
crossovers of mean $2d$ in the interval, and the probability that it
carries none is $e^{-2d}$. If it carries at least one, each crossover
independently involves a given chromatid with probability
$\tfrac12$, so a given chromatid has been exchanged an odd number of
times --- is recombinant --- with probability exactly $\tfrac12$,
whatever the number of crossovers. Hence $r = \tfrac12\,(1 -
e^{-2d})$; inverting gives $d$. For small $d$, $r \approx d$; as
$d \to \infty$, $r \to \tfrac12$.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[The three-point test cross]\label{met:b2:meiosis-heredity:threepoint}
\index{three-point cross}\index{interference}
Cross a triple heterozygote $ABC/abc$ to $abc/abc$ and classify the
eight offspring classes.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The two largest classes are the parentals; the two smallest
        are the \emph{double crossovers}.
  \item The gene that switches between a parental class and the
        double-crossover class is the \emph{middle} one: this gives
        the order.
  \item For each adjacent pair, the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{recombination frequency} is
        (single crossovers in that interval + double crossovers) $/$
        total, since a double crossover recombines both intervals.
  \item Expected double crossovers $= r_1 r_2 \times$ total; the
        \emph{coefficient of coincidence} is observed/expected and
        the \emph{interference} is $1 -$ coincidence.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[A three-point cross worked]\label{ex:b2:meiosis-heredity:threepoint}
Offspring of $+++/abc \times abc/abc$: $+++$ 580, $abc$ 592, $++c$
45, $ab+$ 40, $+bc$ 89, $a++$ 94, $+b+$ 3, $a+c$ 5; total 1448.
Parentals $+++$, $abc$; doubles $+b+$, $a+c$; comparing $+++$ with
$+b+$, the gene that changed is $b$: order $a$--$b$--$c$. $r_{ab} =
(89 + 94 + 3 + 5)/1448 = 0.132$; $r_{bc} = (45 + 40 + 3 + 5)/1448 =
0.064$; map: $a$ --- \qty{13.2}{cM} --- $b$ --- \qty{6.4}{cM} ---
$c$. Expected doubles $0.132\times 0.064\times 1448 = 12.2$,
observed 8: coincidence $0.65$, interference $0.35$.
\end{example}

\section{Sex chromosomes and organelles}

\begin{proposition}[Sex-linked inheritance]\label{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:sexlinked}
\index{X-linked inheritance}\index{sex chromosomes}
In mammals and flies the female is $XX$ and the male $XY$: the $Y$
carries few genes, so a male has a single copy of every $X$-linked
gene (he is \emph{hemizygous}) and shows whatever \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{allele} he carries,
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{dominant} or \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive}. Consequences: reciprocal crosses differ
(white-eyed female $\times$ red-eyed male gives white-eyed sons and
red-eyed daughters; the reverse cross gives all red); a \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive}
$X$-linked trait (red--green colour blindness, haemophilia, Duchenne
muscular dystrophy) appears mostly in males, is passed by unaffected
carrier mothers, and is never passed from father to son (a son gets
his father's $Y$); a father passes his $X$ to all his daughters.
Birds, butterflies and some reptiles reverse the arrangement ($ZW$
females, $ZZ$ males); many reptiles let temperature decide; and in
mammals one $X$ of every female cell is silenced at random early in
development, so that a \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{heterozygous} female is a mosaic (the
tortoiseshell cat).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Morgan (1910) found one white-eyed male among thousands of
red-eyed flies. Crossed to red females, all offspring were red; but
in the next generation the white eyes reappeared only in males,
half of them. Crossing a white male to his red daughters gave white
eyes in both sexes. The pattern was exactly that expected if the
gene sat on the X chromosome, which females carry twice and males
once --- the first gene assigned to a chromosome.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Reading a pedigree]\label{met:b2:meiosis-heredity:pedigree}
\index{pedigree}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Two unaffected parents with an affected child: the trait is
        \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive} (both parents \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{heterozygous}).
  \item An affected child always has an affected parent, and the
        trait appears in every generation: \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{dominant}.
  \item Affected individuals mostly male, transmitted through
        unaffected mothers, never father to son: X-linked \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive}.
  \item An affected father has all daughters affected and no son
        affected: X-linked \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{dominant}.
  \item Transmitted by mothers to all their children, never by
        fathers: mitochondrial.
  \item Then compute probabilities from the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotypes} implied:
        a carrier $\times$ normal couple has a $\tfrac14$ chance of an
        affected son at each birth ($\tfrac12$ boy $\times$ $\tfrac12$
        receives the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{allele}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{definition}[Extranuclear inheritance]\label{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:extranuclear}
\index{maternal inheritance}\index{mitochondrial inheritance}
Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own small genomes, in
many copies per organelle and many organelles per cell, and they
come almost entirely from the egg: the sperm contributes a nucleus
and little else. Their genes are therefore \emph{maternally
inherited}, without segregation ratios: a mother passes the trait to
all her children, a father to none. Human mitochondrial diseases
(defects of the respiratory chain affecting muscle and nerve)
follow this pattern, and so does the variegation of some plants,
where a mother cell containing both normal and mutant chloroplasts
sorts them at random into daughter cells, producing green, white
and mixed sectors. Because they never recombine, mitochondrial
sequences trace maternal lineages back through time --- the tool of
\cref{ch:b2:phylogenetic-trees}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Tetrad analysis]\label{prop:b2:meiosis-heredity:tetrads}
\index{tetrad analysis}\index{ordered tetrad}
In some fungi (\emph{Neurospora}, \emph{Sordaria}) the four products
of one \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} stay together in a sac, the \emph{ascus}, and in
order: a final mitosis gives eight spores whose sequence records the
two divisions. For a heterozygote $A/a$, an ascus with the pattern
$4 : 4$ (\emph{first-division segregation}) had no crossover between
the gene and its centromere --- the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} parted at anaphase I; a
pattern $2 : 2 : 2 : 2$ or $2 : 4 : 2$ (\emph{second-division
segregation}) had one, so that the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} parted only at anaphase
II. The distance from gene to centromere is half the percentage of
second-division asci, since a crossover recombines only two of the
four chromatids. Tetrads also show directly that recombination is
reciprocal: every recombinant chromatid has its complementary
partner in the same ascus.
\end{proposition}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:1}
List four differences between mitosis and \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} (number of

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:2}
How many chromosomally distinct gametes can a person make by
\omterm{thm:b2:meiosis-heredity:mixing}{independent assortment} alone? A fruit fly ($n = 4$)? A pea ($n =
7$)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:3}
In peas, tall ($T$) is \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{dominant} over dwarf. Give the phenotypic and
genotypic ratios of $Tt \times Tt$ and of $Tt \times tt$. How do you
find out whether a tall plant is $TT$ or $Tt$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:4}
Define \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{linkage}, \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{recombination frequency} and the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{centimorgan}. Why

were independent?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:5}
Mendel's \omterm{thm:b2:meiosis-heredity:mendel}{dihybrid cross} gave $315$ round yellow, $108$ round green,
$101$ wrinkled yellow, $32$ wrinkled green. Test the $9 : 3 : 3 :
1$ hypothesis with $\chi^2$ (3 degrees of freedom, critical value
$7.81$).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:6}
A dihybrid $AB/ab$ test-crossed gives $412$ $AB$, $388$ $ab$, $103$
$Ab$, $97$ $aB$. Are the genes linked? Compute the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{recombination
frequency} and the map distance; what would the dihybrid $Ab/aB$ give?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:7}
Using Haldane's function, convert $r = 0.20$ and $r = 0.45$ to map

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:8}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:9}
Two white-flowered pure lines of sweet pea, crossed, give all

Explain with two genes, give the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotypes} of the two parental

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:10}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:meiosis-heredity:11}

\section{Problem: Crosses in the Fly Room}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- crosses of \emph{Drosophila}
analysed from the monohybrid ratio to a three-point map, and a human
pedigree computed, ending on the map of three X-linked genes and its
interference}]
\label{pb:b2:meiosis-heredity:1}
Vestigial wing ($vg$) and ebony body ($e$) are \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive} and lie on
different autosomes; black body ($b$) and $vg$ are \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive} and both
lie on chromosome 2; yellow body ($y$), white eye ($w$) and miniature
wing ($m$) are \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive} and X-linked. Critical values of $\chi^2$ at
\qty{5}{\%}: $3.84$ (1 d.f.), $7.81$ (3 d.f.).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Two autosomal genes.}
A pure vestigial fly is crossed to a pure ebony fly; the $F_1$ are
all wild type; $1600$ $F_2$ flies are scored: $892$ wild, $309$
vestigial, $296$ ebony, $103$ vestigial ebony.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Give the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotypes} of the parents, the $F_1$ and the gametes
        of the $F_1$.
  \item Compute the expected counts under \omterm{thm:b2:meiosis-heredity:mixing}{independent assortment}.
  \item Compute $\chi^2$ and conclude.
  \item What fraction of the wild-type $F_2$ flies are \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{homozygous} at
        both loci?
  \item The $F_1$ is test-crossed to a vestigial ebony fly: predict
        the four classes and their proportions.
  \item Ebony flies raised at \qty{29}{\celsius} are paler than at
        \qty{18}{\celsius}. What does this say about the relation of
        \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype} to \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{phenotype}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Two linked genes.}
A pure black fly is crossed to a pure vestigial fly; the $F_1$
females are test-crossed to black vestigial males: $965$ black,
$944$ vestigial, $206$ black vestigial, $185$ wild type.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Write the $F_1$ \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype}, showing which \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{alleles} are on the
        same chromosome.
  \item Identify the parental and recombinant classes and explain
        why the wild type is a \emph{recombinant} here.
  \item Compute $r$ and the map distance.
  \item Correct it with Haldane's function.
  \item The same cross with $F_1$ \emph{males} test-crossed gives only
        two classes, black and vestigial, in equal numbers. What does
        this show about \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} in male flies?
  \item Predict the classes and proportions if the $F_1$ female had
        come from a black vestigial $\times$ wild cross.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- A human pedigree.}
Haemophilia A is X-linked \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{recessive}. A man with haemophilia has a
daughter, who marries an unaffected man.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item What is the daughter's \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype}, and why is it certain?
  \item Probability that her first child is an affected son.
  \item Probability that a daughter of hers is a carrier.
  \item Probability that, among her three children, none is
        affected.
  \item Explain why the disease is never passed from father to son,
        and how a woman could be affected.
  \item The affected man's sister has two healthy sons; their mother
        (the man's mother) was a carrier. Compute the probability that
        the sister is a carrier, before and after taking her sons into
        account.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Three X-linked genes.}
A female $y\,w\,m/+\,+\,+$ is crossed to a $y\,w\,m$ male; her $2000$
sons are scored: $+++$ 853, $ywm$ 833, $y++$ 24, $+wm$ 26, $yw+$ 128,
$++m$ 132, $+w+$ 2, $y+m$ 2.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Why can the sons be scored directly, without a test cross?
  \item Identify the parental and double-crossover classes and
        deduce the gene order.
  \item Compute the two recombination frequencies and draw the map.
  \item Compute the expected number of double crossovers, the
        coefficient of coincidence and the interference.
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:linkage}{recombination frequency} between the two outer
        genes directly from the data, and explain why it is less than
        the sum of the two distances.
  \item What would the daughters of this cross look like, and why are
        they useless for mapping?
  \item State the result: the map of the three genes and the
        interference.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
