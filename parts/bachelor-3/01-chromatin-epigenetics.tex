\chapter{Chromatin and Epigenetics}\label{ch:b3:chromatin-epigenetics}

Two mice from the same litter, genetically identical down to the last
base, sit side by side: one is slim and brown, the other plump and
yellow, and it will become diabetic. A calico cat wears patches of
orange and black although every cell of her skin carries the same two
X chromosomes. A child inherits the same deleted stretch of chromosome
15 as another child, and one has Prader--Willi syndrome while the other
has Angelman syndrome --- the difference is only which parent the
deletion came from. None of this is written in the DNA sequence. It is
written \emph{on} it: in the way two metres of DNA are packed into a
nucleus ten micrometres across, in the small chemical groups attached to
the packing proteins and to the cytosines themselves, and in the
machinery that copies those marks from a cell to its daughters. This
chapter is about that second layer of information, its molecular
carriers, and the honest limits of what it can remember.

\section{Chromatin: DNA on a spool}

\begin{definition}[Nucleosome and chromatin]\label{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}
\index{nucleosome}\index{chromatin}\index{histone}\index{euchromatin}\index{heterochromatin}
\emph{Chromatin} is the complex of DNA with proteins in which the
genome exists in a eukaryotic nucleus. Its repeating unit is the
\emph{nucleosome}: \qty{147}{bp} of DNA wound in $1.65$ left-handed
turns around a \emph{histone} octamer --- two copies each of the small,
very basic proteins H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 --- followed by a
\emph{linker} of \qtyrange{20}{60}{bp} to the next nucleosome. A fifth
histone, H1, binds the linker where it enters and leaves the core and
helps the beads pack against one another. Each core histone carries an
unstructured amino-terminal \emph{tail} of some \numrange{20}{40}
residues that projects out of the particle. Chromatin that stains
lightly, is loosely packed and is transcribed is \emph{euchromatin};
densely packed, late-replicating and largely silent chromatin, at
centromeres, telomeres and repeated sequences, is \emph{heterochromatin}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Hewish and Burgoyne (1973) digested rat liver nuclei with an endogenous
nuclease and found that the DNA came out not as a smear but as a ladder
of fragments whose lengths were multiples of about \qty{200}{bp}: the
DNA was protected at regular intervals and cut only between them.
Kornberg (1974) showed that the protected unit was a particle of eight
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histones} with about \qty{200}{bp}, and electron micrographs of gently
spread \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} showed the ``beads on a string''. Luger and
colleagues (1997) solved the crystal structure of the core particle at
\qty{2.8}{\angstrom}: \qty{147}{bp}, an octamer with the tails passing
out between the DNA gyres.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Levels of compaction]\label{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:compaction}
\index{packing ratio}\index{chromatin loop}\index{topologically associating domain}
The \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:compaction}{packing ratio}} of a \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} structure is the length of the
DNA it contains divided by the length of the structure. Wrapping into
the \qty{10}{nm} fibre gives a ratio of about $6$: the \qty{68}{nm} of a
\qty{200}{bp} repeat is compressed into one \qty{11}{nm} bead. In the
interphase nucleus the fibre is not wound into a regular thicker fibre,
as textbooks long drew it, but folded into \emph{loops} of tens to
hundreds of kilobases that are held at their bases by ring-shaped
cohesin complexes and by the DNA-binding protein CTCF; the loops group
into \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:compaction}{topologically associating domains}} (TADs) of about a
megabase within which a gene meets its regulators far more often than
it meets anything outside. A mitotic chromosome, the most compact
state, has a \omterm{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:compaction}{packing ratio} near \num{10000}: the \qty{4}{cm} of DNA in
an average human chromosome are folded into a rod \qty{4}{\micro m}
long.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Two metres in ten micrometres]\label{ex:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:two-metres}
A human diploid nucleus holds $6.4\times 10^{9}$ base pairs. At
\qty{0.34}{nm} per base pair that is \qty{2.2}{m} of double helix, in a
nucleus about \qty{10}{\micro m} across. It is packed into
$6.4\times 10^{9}/200 \approx 3.2\times 10^{7}$ \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes}, each
carrying about \qty{108}{kDa} of \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} against \qty{96}{kDa} of DNA
(\qty{147}{bp} at \qty{650}{Da} per pair): the nucleus contains roughly
as much \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} as DNA by mass. The DNA itself, treated as a cylinder
\qty{2}{nm} across, has a volume of only about \qty{7}{\micro m^3},
some \qty{1}{\%} of a spherical nucleus of radius \qty{5}{\micro m};
the packing problem is one of entanglement and access, not of room.
\end{example}

\section{The histone code}

\begin{definition}[Histone modifications]\label{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}
\index{histone code}\index{acetylation}\index{histone methylation}\index{writer}\index{reader}\index{eraser}
The residues of the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} tails are covalently modified:
\emph{acetylation} of lysines (which neutralises their positive charge
and loosens the grip on DNA), \emph{methylation} of lysines and
arginines (one, two or three methyl groups, with no change of charge),
phosphorylation of serines, ubiquitination of lysines. A modification is
named by \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone}, residue and group: H3K4me3 is \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} H3, lysine 4,
trimethylated; H3K27ac is H3 lysine 27 acetylated. The \emph{\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone}
code} is the correlation between combinations of marks and the state
of the underlying gene: H3K4me3 and H3K27ac at active promoters and
enhancers, H3K36me3 along transcribed gene bodies, H3K9me3 on
constitutive \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{heterochromatin}, H3K27me3 on genes silenced during
development. Enzymes that add a mark are \emph{writers} (\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone}
acetyltransferases, HATs; methyltransferases), enzymes that remove it
are \emph{erasers} (\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} deacetylases, HDACs; demethylases), and
proteins that bind a mark and act on it are \emph{readers}: a
\emph{bromodomain} binds acetyl-lysine, a \emph{chromodomain}
methyl-lysine.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Marks are read, not merely felt]\label{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:readers}
\index{HP1}\index{Polycomb}\index{PRC2}\index{Trithorax}
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{Acetylation} acts partly by electrostatics --- an acetylated tail holds
its DNA less tightly and the fibre opens --- but most marks act by
recruiting \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{readers}. H3K9me3 is bound by the chromodomain of the
\emph{\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{heterochromatin} protein HP1}, which dimerises, bridges
neighbouring \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes} and recruits the very methyltransferase
(SUV39H) that writes H3K9me3 on the next \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosome}: the mark spreads
along the fibre until it meets a boundary, and compacts what it
covers. H3K27me3 is written by \emph{Polycomb repressive complex 2}
(PRC2, catalytic subunit EZH2), read by PRC1, which ubiquitinates H2A
and compacts the fibre, and the complex is itself stimulated by the
mark it makes; the \emph{Trithorax} group of proteins writes the
opposing H3K4me3 and keeps the developmental genes of a given cell type
on. Each such loop --- \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{reader} recruits \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{writer} --- is a positive feedback
that lets a state maintain itself once established, which is what a
heritable mark needs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)]\label{met:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:chip}
\index{ChIP}\index{chromatin immunoprecipitation}
To map where a mark or a protein sits on the genome: (1) cross-link
proteins to DNA in living cells with formaldehyde; (2) shear the
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} into fragments of a few hundred base pairs; (3) precipitate
the fragments with an antibody against the mark (say H3K27me3) bound to
beads; (4) reverse the cross-links and purify the DNA; (5) sequence it
(ChIP-seq) and count reads along the genome: peaks are the places
where the mark was. A control of input DNA without antibody sets the
background. The resolution is that of the fragments, about one
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosome}.
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Code or correlation]\label{rem:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:code}
The word ``code'' overstates the case. Many marks are consequences of
transcription rather than causes of it (H3K36me3 is deposited by a
methyltransferase riding with the polymerase), and removing a \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{writer}
often changes expression far less than the mark's distribution
suggests. What is well established is that a few marks --- H3K9me3,
H3K27me3, and \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{DNA methylation} below --- carry self-propagating
silencing that outlives the signal that set it up.
\end{remark}

\section{DNA methylation and the copying of a mark}

\begin{definition}[DNA methylation]\label{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}
\index{DNA methylation}\index{CpG}\index{CpG island}\index{DNMT1}\index{maintenance methylation}\index{de novo methylation}
In vertebrates a methyl group is added to carbon 5 of cytosine almost
only in the dinucleotide \emph{CpG} (a C followed by a G on the same
strand), a sequence that is its own complement, so that a methylated
CpG is normally methylated on both strands. Some \qtyrange{70}{80}{\%}
of the CpGs of a human cell are methylated, and methylated promoters
are silent: the methyl groups are read by methyl-CpG-binding proteins
(MeCP2, MBD1--4) that recruit HDACs and H3K9 methylases, and they
block several transcription factors directly. The exceptions are the
\emph{CpG islands}, stretches of about a kilobase, rich in G and C
and in CpG, that sit at the promoters of most housekeeping genes and
stay unmethylated in normal cells. The methyl group is written by
\emph{DNA methyltransferases}: DNMT3A and DNMT3B perform \emph{de novo
methylation} of unmethylated sites, and \emph{DNMT1}, which prefers a
hemimethylated CpG --- one strand methylated, the other not --- performs
\emph{maintenance methylation} behind the replication fork. Removal is
passive, by replication without maintenance, or active, by the TET
enzymes, which oxidise 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine and
beyond until base-excision repair replaces it with a plain cytosine.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Why the genome is short of CpG]\label{ex:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:cpg-deficit}
The human genome is \qty{42}{\%} G$+$C, so if bases were independent a
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG} would occur with frequency $0.21\times 0.21 \approx 0.044$, about
$2.8\times 10^{8}$ times in the diploid genome. The observed number is
about $5.6\times 10^{7}$, one fifth of that. The reason is the mark
itself: a methylated cytosine that loses its amino group becomes
thymine, a normal base that repair cannot recognise as wrong, whereas
an unmethylated cytosine deaminates to uracil, which is excised. Over
evolutionary time methylated \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpGs} have mutated into TpG and CpA, and
only the unmethylated islands have kept their \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpGs}. The mark has left
its signature in the sequence.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Maintenance of a methylation state through divisions]\label{thm:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:maintenance}
\index{epigenetic memory}\index{Markov chain!methylation}
Consider one \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG} site, followed along a lineage of cells. At each
division the new strand's cytosine is methylated with probability
$\mu$ if the template strand's cytosine is methylated (maintenance
efficiency) and with probability $\delta$ if it is not (de novo rate).
Let $p_{n}$ be the probability that the site is methylated after $n$
divisions. Then
\[
p_{n+1} = \mu\, p_{n} + \delta\,(1 - p_{n}),
\qquad
p_{n} = p^{*} + (p_{0} - p^{*})\,(\mu - \delta)^{n},
\qquad
p^{*} = \frac{\delta}{1 - \mu + \delta}.
\]
Every site drifts toward the same equilibrium $p^{*}$, whatever its
starting state, and the memory of the initial state decays
geometrically with ratio $\mu - \delta$. A mark is remembered for
about $1/(1 - \mu + \delta)$ divisions; faithful memory of a
\emph{difference} between sites therefore requires $\mu$ very close to
$1$ and $\delta$ very close to $0$, or a feedback that makes $\mu$ and
$\delta$ depend on the state of the neighbourhood.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The recurrence is the law of total probability over the two states of
the template. Its fixed point solves $p^{*} = \mu p^{*} + \delta(1 -
p^{*})$, giving $p^{*}(1 - \mu + \delta) = \delta$. Subtracting the
fixed-point equation from the recurrence, $p_{n+1} - p^{*} = (\mu -
\delta)(p_{n} - p^{*})$, which iterates to the closed form. Since
$0 \le \delta \le \mu \le 1$, the ratio $\mu - \delta$ lies in $[0,1]$
and the deviation shrinks; it is halved every $\ln 2 / (-\ln(\mu -
\delta)) \approx \ln 2/(1 - \mu + \delta)$ divisions when $\mu -
\delta$ is near $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Numbers]\label{ex:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:maintenance-numbers}
Measured values for mammalian cells are $\mu \approx 0.99$ and
$\delta \approx 0.02$ per division at a typical \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG}. Then $p^{*} =
0.02/0.03 \approx 0.67$, close to the genome-wide methylated fraction,
and $\mu - \delta = 0.97$: a fully methylated site left to the
machinery alone would be half-way back to the average after $\ln 2 /
0.03 \approx 23$ divisions. A silenced promoter that stays silent for
the hundreds of divisions of a lifetime therefore needs more than
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{DNMT1}: the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{methyl-CpG} \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{readers} recruit H3K9 methylation and the H3K9
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{readers} recruit DNMT3, so that a dense island of marks raises its own
$\mu$ toward $1$ and lowers the neighbouring $\delta$ toward $0$.
Conversely, a cell that loses \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{DNMT1} loses the mark passively: with
$\mu = \delta = 0$ the fraction of methylated strands halves at each
division.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Reading methylation: bisulfite sequencing]\label{met:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:bisulfite}
\index{bisulfite sequencing}
Sequencing alone cannot see a methyl group. Treat denatured DNA with
sodium bisulfite: unmethylated cytosine is deaminated to uracil, which
reads as T after PCR, while 5-methylcytosine is protected and still
reads as C. Sequence the converted DNA and compare with the reference:
every C that remains C was methylated, every C that became T was not.
Done genome-wide (whole-genome \omterm{met:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:bisulfite}{bisulfite sequencing}) this gives the
methylation state of each of the $2.8\times 10^{7}$ \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpGs} of a haploid
genome at single-base resolution, as a fraction of the cells in the
sample.
\end{method}

\section{X inactivation and genomic imprinting}

\begin{definition}[X-chromosome inactivation]\label{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}
\index{X inactivation}\index{Barr body}\index{Xist}\index{dosage compensation}
In female mammals one of the two X chromosomes of every somatic cell
is transcriptionally silenced early in development, a form of
\emph{dosage compensation} that equalises X-linked gene expression
between XX females and XY males. The silenced chromosome is compacted
into a dense body against the nuclear envelope, the \emph{Barr body}.
The choice of which X to silence is random in the embryo proper and,
once made, is inherited clonally by all descendants of the cell.
Inactivation is initiated by \emph{Xist}, a \qty{17}{kb} non-coding RNA
transcribed from the X-inactivation centre of the chromosome that will
be silenced: it coats that chromosome in cis, recruits Polycomb (H3K27me3),
the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} variant macroH2A and finally \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{DNA methylation} of the
promoters, and the silence is thereafter maintained without Xist
being needed for each division. About \qty{15}{\%} of human X-linked
genes escape inactivation, in part or wholly.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Barr and Bertram (1949) noticed a dense body in the nuclei of female
but not male cat neurons. Ohno (1959) showed it was one whole X
chromosome, condensed. Lyon (1961) put the pieces together: female
mice heterozygous for X-linked coat-colour genes have a mosaic coat of
patches expressing one allele or the other, so each patch is a clone
in which one X, always the same one, is silent; the same holds for the
orange-and-black patches of a tortoiseshell or calico cat, whose orange
gene is X-linked, and for the mosaic sweat glands of women carrying a
mutant allele of an X-linked gene. A male calico cat is almost always
XXY.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Genomic imprinting]\label{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:imprinting}
\index{genomic imprinting}\index{imprinting control region}\index{uniparental disomy}
A gene is \emph{imprinted} when only one of its two alleles is
expressed, and which one depends on the parent it came from: some
genes are expressed only from the paternal copy, others only from the
maternal. Mammals have some \numrange{100}{200} imprinted genes,
clustered, each cluster governed by an \emph{imprinting control
region} (ICR) that is methylated on one parental allele and not the
other. The methylation is set in the germ line --- one pattern in
sperm, another in eggs --- erased in the germ cells of the next
generation and set again according to the sex of that individual. A
child who receives both copies of a chromosome from the same parent
(\emph{uniparental disomy}) therefore has the wrong dose of the
imprinted genes on it although its sequence is entirely normal.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
McGrath and Solter, and independently Surani, Barton and Norris (1984),
made mouse zygotes carrying two maternal pronuclei, or two paternal
ones, by transferring pronuclei between fertilised eggs. Neither kind
developed to term, although each had two complete sets of chromosomes:
the gynogenetic embryos formed a reasonable embryo with a poor
placenta, the androgenetic ones a large placenta and almost no embryo.
The two parental genomes are not equivalent and both are required.
Cattanach and Kirk (1985) showed with chromosome-specific uniparental
disomies that the non-equivalence is confined to particular regions of
particular chromosomes, which are where the imprinted clusters were
later found.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The \emph{Igf2}--\emph{H19} cluster]\label{ex:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:igf2}
\emph{Igf2}, a fetal growth factor, is expressed from the paternal
allele; its neighbour \emph{H19}, a non-coding RNA, from the maternal.
Between them lies the ICR and, beyond \emph{H19}, a shared set of
enhancers. On the maternal chromosome the ICR is unmethylated and binds
CTCF, which acts as an insulator: the enhancers can reach \emph{H19}
but not \emph{Igf2}. On the paternal chromosome the ICR is methylated
(set in the sperm), CTCF cannot bind, and the enhancers reach across to
\emph{Igf2}; the methylation also spreads to silence the \emph{H19}
promoter. One mark, set in one germ line, decides both genes.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Prader--Willi and Angelman]\label{ex:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:pws}
The same deletion of about \qty{5}{Mb} in chromosome 15 (region
15q11--q13) causes \emph{Prader--Willi syndrome} --- poor muscle tone
at birth, then insatiable appetite and obesity --- when it is on the
paternal chromosome, and \emph{Angelman syndrome} --- severe
intellectual disability, absent speech, a happy demeanour and seizures
--- when on the maternal. The region carries paternally expressed
genes (\emph{SNRPN} and a cluster of small RNAs) whose only active copy
is lost in the first case, and the maternally expressed \emph{UBE3A},
lost in the second. Maternal \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:imprinting}{uniparental disomy} of 15, with no deletion
at all, also gives Prader--Willi: two maternal copies, silent for the
paternal genes.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Why imprint at all]\label{rem:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:kinship}
Imprinted genes are enriched for control of fetal growth and of
placental nutrient transfer, with paternally expressed genes
(\emph{Igf2}, \emph{Peg3}) tending to promote growth and maternally
expressed ones (\emph{H19}, \emph{Igf2r}, \emph{Cdkn1c}) to restrain
it. The \emph{kinship theory} reads this as a conflict: a father's
genes gain from extracting more from a mother who may carry other
males' offspring later, while the mother's genes gain from rationing
across all her litters. Imprinting occurs in placental mammals and in
flowering plants, whose endosperm is likewise fed by the mother, and
not in birds or reptiles, which is what the theory predicts.
\end{remark}

\section{Epigenetic inheritance and its limits}

\begin{definition}[Epigenetics]\label{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:epigenetics}
\index{epigenetics}\index{epiallele}\index{reprogramming}
\emph{Epigenetics} is the study of heritable changes in gene activity
that are not changes in DNA sequence: states carried by \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{DNA
methylation}, \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} marks, or self-sustaining transcription-factor
circuits, and copied through cell division. Two alleles of identical
sequence but different stable epigenetic state are \emph{epialleles}.
Inheritance through mitosis is the rule and is what makes a
differentiated cell stay differentiated. Inheritance through meiosis,
from parent to offspring, is the exception in mammals, because the
marks are erased in two waves of \emph{reprogramming}: in the
primordial germ cells, where imprints and most methylation are wiped
and then set afresh according to sex, and again in the zygote and early
embryo, where the paternal genome is actively demethylated by TET3 and
the maternal passively, before the embryo's own patterns are laid down
by DNMT3.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The agouti viable yellow mouse]\label{ex:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:agouti}
In the $A^{vy}$ allele a retrotransposon has inserted upstream of the
\emph{agouti} coat-colour gene. Where the transposon's promoter is
unmethylated it drives \emph{agouti} constantly in every cell; the
mouse is yellow, obese and prone to diabetes. Where it is methylated
the gene follows its normal control and the mouse is brown
(``pseudoagouti''). Genetically identical littermates range across the
whole spectrum, and a yellow mother has more yellow pups than a brown
one: the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:epigenetics}{epiallele} is incompletely erased through the maternal germ
line. Waterland and Jirtle (2003) fed pregnant $A^{vy}$ mothers a diet
rich in methyl donors (folate, vitamin B\textsubscript{12}, choline,
betaine) and shifted the pups' coats toward brown. The environment of
one generation had set a mark that the next generation carried.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Where heritable epigenetic states are found]\label{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:examples}
\index{position-effect variegation}\index{vernalization}\index{paramutation}
Three well-analysed systems illustrate the mechanisms above.
\emph{\omterm{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:examples}{Position-effect variegation}} in \emph{Drosophila}: a gene
relocated by a chromosome rearrangement next to \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{heterochromatin} is
silenced in some cells and not others, giving a mottled eye; each
patch is a clone, the silencing spreads from the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{heterochromatin} by the
HP1 loop, and the genes whose mutation suppresses the variegation
(\emph{Su(var)} genes) are HP1 and the H3K9 methyltransferase.
\emph{Vernalization} in \emph{Arabidopsis}: weeks of winter cold
silence the flowering repressor \emph{FLC} by Polycomb-deposited
H3K27me3, the silence holds through months of spring growth and
thousands of cell divisions, and it is reset in every generation so
that each plant must experience its own winter. \emph{Paramutation} in
maize: one allele of the \emph{b1} pigment gene, when it meets a
particular other allele in a heterozygote, converts it to its own
silent state, and the converted allele passes the state to its
descendants for generations, through a small-RNA-directed methylation
taken up in \cref{ch:b3:rna-regulation}. In each case the
inheritance is mitotic and robust; transmission across generations is
either actively reset (plants) or leaky and partial (mice).
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[The limits of transgenerational claims]\label{rem:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:limits}
Claims that a grandparent's famine or stress is ``inherited
epigenetically'' by human grandchildren are common and mostly
unproven. Two waves of erasure stand between a somatic mark and a
grandchild, the imprinted genes and a few transposons are the only
documented survivors of erasure in mammals, and in human cohorts the
effects of a shared environment, of the uterine environment, and of
genetic differences are hard to separate from a mark carried in the
germ line. The demonstrated cases --- $A^{vy}$, some transposon-linked
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:epigenetics}{epialleles}, RNA-mediated states in worms and plants --- are real,
molecularly understood, and narrow. The chapter's theorem says why:
without a feedback that pushes $\mu$ to $1$, a mark forgets itself in
a few dozen divisions, and the germ line adds an active reset on top.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Memory of a Silenced Gene}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a human genome packed into its
nucleus, a methylation mark followed through divisions, a calico cat
read as a clonal map, and an imprinted region's diseases counted,
ending on the number of divisions a mark survives without feedback and
the size of the cell population that chose its X}]
\label{pb:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:1}
Data: a human diploid nucleus holds $6.4\times 10^{9}$ base pairs, at
\qty{0.34}{nm} and \qty{650}{Da} per pair; the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosome} repeat is
\qty{200}{bp}, a \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} octamer weighs \qty{108}{kDa}, and a core
particle is a disc \qty{11}{nm} across and \qty{5.5}{nm} thick. The
nucleus is a sphere of diameter \qty{10}{\micro m}. The genome is
\qty{42}{\%} G$+$C. Methylation at an ordinary \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG}: $\mu = 0.99$,
$\delta = 0.02$ per division.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Packing.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the total length of DNA in the nucleus and the number
        of \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes}.
  \item Compute the total mass of \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} (octamers only) and of DNA,
        in picograms ($\qty{1}{Da} = \qty{1.66e-24}{g}$).
  \item Compute the volume of the nucleus and the total volume of the
        core particles, treated as cylinders. What fraction of the
        nucleus do they fill?
  \item If the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes} were laid end to end as a \qty{10}{nm}
        fibre, how long would the fibre be, and what is the \omterm{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:compaction}{packing
        ratio} relative to naked DNA?
  \item The fibre is folded into loops of \qty{200}{kb} anchored by
        cohesin. How many loops, and how many \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes} per loop?
  \item Under the independence assumption, how many \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG} dinucleotides
        would the diploid genome contain? The observed number is
        $5.6\times 10^{7}$. What fraction has been lost, and by what
        mutational mechanism?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- A mark through divisions.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Write the recurrence for $p_{n}$ and compute $p^{*}$.
  \item A promoter \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG} is fully methylated at $n = 0$. Compute $p_{n}$
        after $10$, $50$ and $200$ divisions.
  \item After how many divisions is the excess $p_{n} - p^{*}$ halved?
        A tissue stem cell divides about once a week: how many years is
        that?
  \item A drug inhibits \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{DNMT1} completely ($\mu = 0$) and DNMT3 as well
        ($\delta = 0$). Show that the fraction of methylated strands in
        the population halves at each division, and find the number of
        divisions to bring \qty{80}{\%} methylation below \qty{5}{\%}.
  \item In a silenced island the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{methyl-CpG} \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{readers} recruit H3K9
        methylation, which recruits DNMT3, so that within the island
        $\mu = 0.9995$ and $\delta = 0.0005$. Recompute the half-life
        of the excess, in divisions and in years at one division a
        week.
  \item Explain in one sentence why the result of question 12 and not
        that of question 9 is what a lifelong silenced gene requires,
        and what kind of molecular arrangement produces it.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The calico map.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A cat is heterozygous for the X-linked orange gene. Explain
        why her coat has orange and black patches and what one patch
        represents.
  \item \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{X inactivation} occurred when the cells destined to form the
        skin numbered $N$. Each chose an X at random. What is the
        probability that all $N$ chose the same one, giving a
        single-colour cat?
  \item No such single-coloured heterozygote has been documented
        among perhaps a billion cats; take the probability as
        $10^{-9}$ and estimate $N$.
  \item The cat's skin surface is about \qty{0.3}{m^2} and her patches
        average \qty{15}{cm^2}. Estimate the number of patches, and
        explain why it is larger than $N$.
  \item An XXY tomcat is also calico. How many Barr bodies do his
        cells show? How many in an XXX female and in an XY male?
  \item A gene that escapes inactivation is present in two active
        copies in females and one in males. Give one reason such
        escape may be tolerated and one situation where it matters.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Chromosome 15.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Prader--Willi syndrome affects about one birth in
        \num{15000}; \qty{70}{\%} of cases are a de novo deletion of
        the paternal 15q11--q13 and \qty{25}{\%} a maternal \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:imprinting}{uniparental
        disomy}. Compute the birth frequency of each cause.
  \item Angelman syndrome has about the same frequency; \qty{70}{\%}
        are maternal deletions, \qty{5}{\%} paternal disomies and
        \qty{10}{\%} point mutations of \emph{UBE3A}. Why are point
        mutations a cause of Angelman but almost never of Prader--Willi?
  \item A father carries a balanced translocation that gives
        \qty{20}{\%} of his sperm a 15q11--q13 deletion. What fraction
        of his children have Prader--Willi, and what fraction Angelman?
  \item \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:imprinting}{Uniparental disomy} arises when a trisomic zygote loses one
        chromosome. If a trisomy-15 zygote loses one of its three
