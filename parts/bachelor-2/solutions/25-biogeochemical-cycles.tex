\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b2:biogeochemical-cycles} --- Biogeochemical Cycles}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:1}
A \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} is a store of the element (a mass); a \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{flux} is a rate of
transfer between \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoirs} (mass per year); the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} is
the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir}'s mass divided by its total outflow, the mean time an
atom spends there. Land plants: $450/120 \approx 4$ years (a leaf's
carbon, roughly; \omterm{def:b2:plant-meristems:wood}{wood} stays for decades and the average hides the
spread). Deep ocean: $37000/100 \approx 400$ years --- the time
before a molecule that has sunk sees the surface again.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:2}
$2.5\times 2.13 = \qty{5.3}{GtC}$ a year. $10/2.13 = \qty{4.7}{ppm}$
--- if all of it stayed in the air; at the \qty{45}{\%} \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne
fraction}, \qty{2.1}{ppm}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:3}
Opening: biological fixation by nitrogenase (cyanobacteria,
rhizobia, \emph{Azotobacter}, \emph{Frankia}) and lightning. Return:
\omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{denitrification} (facultative anaerobes such as \emph{Pseudomonas}
and \emph{Paracoccus} in anoxic soil and sediment) and anammox
(planctomycetes). Between: ammonification of organic nitrogen to
ammonium by decomposers (fungi, bacteria); nitrification of
ammonium to nitrite (\emph{Nitrosomonas}, ammonia-oxidising
archaea) and of nitrite to nitrate (\emph{Nitrobacter}); and
assimilation of ammonium or nitrate by plants and microbes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:4}
Over geological time, nitrogen can always be made from the
inexhaustible $\mathrm{N_2}$ of the air by fixers, which are favoured
whenever nitrogen is short; phosphorus has no such \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} and
its supply is fixed by weathering, so the long-run ceiling on
productivity is phosphorus. Within a season, fixation is slow and
costly, and most plants cannot do it, so the nitrogen at hand is
what limits growth here and now --- which is why fertiliser is
mostly nitrogen.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:5}
$\tau = 1/0.2 = 5$ years; $M^{*} = F\tau = \qty{10}{t}$, that is
$\qty{1}{g/m^3} = \qty{1000}{mg/m^3}$, grossly eutrophic. Time to
\qty{90}{\%}: $\tau\ln 10 = 11.5$ years. Halving the input halves
the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{steady state}, to \qty{500}{mg/m^3}, reached on the same
time scale.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:6}
1960s: \qty{0.9}{ppm/yr}, \qty{1.9}{GtC/yr}. 2010s: \qty{2.3}{ppm/yr},
\qty{5.0}{GtC/yr}. The growth rate has increased two and a half
times, in step with emissions.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:7}
\qty{6}{ppm} is \qty{13}{GtC}, against a net primary production of
\qty{35}{GtC}: the sawtooth is a third of it. It is smaller because
respiration and decomposition continue through the summer (the
sawtooth records the net uptake, not the gross), because the
southern hemisphere's seasons are opposite and partly cancel, and
because the ocean damps the swing.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:8}
$\qty{200}{kg}/(\qty{28}{g/mol}) = 7100$~mol of $\mathrm{N_2}$; $16\times
7100 = \num{114000}$~mol ATP; $/30 = 3800$~mol glucose, \qty{690}{kg}.
A crop that builds \qty{10}{t} of dry matter fixes roughly
\qtyrange{15}{20}{t} of sugar once respiration is counted, so
fixation costs it some \qty{4}{\%} of its photosynthate --- the
price of independence from the soil's nitrogen.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:9}
Ratio available $\mathrm{N:P} = 32/2.2 = 14.5$, below the Redfield
16: nitrate runs out first, with \qty{0.2}{\micro mol/kg} of
phosphate left over. Carbon fixed: $32\times 106/16 =
\qty{212}{\micro mol/kg}$, about \qty{2.5}{mg} of carbon per
kilogram of water.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:10}
$E^{*} = Q\tau = \qty{500}{GtC}$, \qty{235}{ppm} above the
pre-industrial 285: about \qty{520}{ppm}. Worse in reality because
the sinks are not a single fast pool: the surface ocean saturates
(its capacity to take up $\mathrm{CO_2}$ falls as its carbonate is used),
the land sink weakens with warming, and the true removal has a
slow component --- deep-ocean mixing and rock weathering --- with
time constants of a thousand to a hundred thousand years, so a
fraction of the excess does not relax on any $\tau$ of decades.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:11}
What was measured is the exchange of atmospheric $\mathrm{CO_2}$ with the
ocean and biosphere, not decay: the bomb $\mathrm{{}^{14}C}$ was diluted
into the much larger pools of the surface ocean and the plants and
soils, which then returned ordinary carbon. The 11-year half-life
of the excess (an e-folding time of about 16 years) is the time
scale of that exchange --- the turnover of the atmosphere's carbon
with respect to the surface ocean and land, longer than the
four-year gross \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} because much of what leaves comes
straight back.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:12}
Nitrogen: natural fixation about 200~Tg a year; human fixation
(Haber--Bosch 120, crops 60, combustion 30) about 210: the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{flux} is
doubled, and the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoirs} affected --- soils, rivers, coastal
seas, the $\mathrm{N_2O}$ of the air --- are small and fast, so the change
shows within decades, while the $\mathrm{N_2}$ \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} is untouched.
Carbon: human emissions of 11~GtC a year are only \qty{5}{\%} of
the gross natural exchange of about 200, so the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{flux} is nudged;
but the natural \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{fluxes} cancel and the human one does not, and it
has raised the atmospheric \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} by \qty{50}{\%} --- the small
nudge has a large cumulative effect on a \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} with a slow
ultimate sink. Which cycle is ``more perturbed'' depends on whether
one counts \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{fluxes} or accumulation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:1}
\textbf{1.} $285\times 2.13 = \qty{607}{GtC}$; $412\times 2.13 =
\qty{878}{GtC}$; increase \qty{271}{GtC}.
\textbf{2.} $271/700 = 0.39$ (the fraction over recent decades,
with land-use emissions counted, is nearer $0.45$).
\textbf{3.} The ocean, about a quarter of emissions, and the land
(forest regrowth and $\mathrm{CO_2}$ fertilisation), about a third; the
remainder of the \qty{430}{GtC} not in the air.
\textbf{4.} $175/38000 = \qty{0.5}{\%}$ --- negligible for the whole
ocean; but the uptake has so far entered mainly the surface layer
(\qty{900}{GtC}), where it is a rise of a tenth or more, and the
carbonate chemistry turns a small rise in dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$ into a
larger rise in hydrogen-ion concentration: surface pH has fallen
by $0.1$, a \qty{30}{\%} rise in acidity.
\textbf{5.} With $Q = Q_0 e^{t/T}$, try $E = Ae^{t/T}$: $A/T = Q_0 -
A/\tau$, so $A = Q_0\tau T/(\tau + T)$ and the \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction}
$\dot E/Q = \tau/(\tau + T)$ is constant. Emissions growing at
\qty{2}{\%} a year ($T = 50$) with $\tau = 40$ give $0.44$: the
fraction is constant because sink and source grow together.
\textbf{6.} With $Q$ constant, $E \to Q\tau$ and $\dot E \to 0$: the
\omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction} falls toward zero as the sinks catch up --- in
the one-box model; in reality the sinks saturate and the fraction
falls more slowly.
\textbf{7.} \qty{271}{GtC}.
\textbf{8.} $E^{*} = 10\times 100 = \qty{1000}{GtC}$: $285 +
1000/2.13 = \qty{755}{ppm}$.
\textbf{9.} $E(t) = 1000 + (271 - 1000)e^{-t/100}$; at $t = 80$,
$E = 1000 - 729\times 0.449 = \qty{672}{GtC}$: \qty{600}{ppm}.
\textbf{10.} $Q = 10(1 - t/50)$: particular solution $E_p = at + b$
with $a = -20$, $b = 3000$ (from $a = 10 - b/\tau$ and $0 = -0.2 -
a/\tau$); $E = 3000 - 20t + (271 - 3000)e^{-t/100}$. At $t = 50$:
$2000 - 2729\times 0.607 = \qty{344}{GtC}$, \qty{447}{ppm}. Then $E$
decays freely: at $t = 80$, $344\,e^{-0.3} = \qty{255}{GtC}$,
\qty{405}{ppm}.
\textbf{11.} \qty{600}{ppm} against \qty{405}{ppm}: the second
scenario returns the air, by 2100, nearly to where it is today.
\textbf{12.} The single $\tau$ makes the sinks keep working at the
same rate on a shrinking excess; in reality the fast sinks (the
mixed layer, the growing forests) have already taken what they can
and the remaining removal is by slow deep-ocean mixing and
weathering with $\tau$ of centuries to millennia, so the decline
after net zero is far slower --- the concentration stays roughly
constant for centuries rather than falling.
\textbf{13.} Crop \qty{90}{kg}; denitrified \qty{37.5}{kg}; leached
\qty{22.5}{kg} of nitrogen per hectare per year.
\textbf{14.} $\qty{22.5}{kg}/\qty{14}{g/mol} = 1600$~mol N; carbon
$1600\times 106/16 = \num{10600}$~mol, \qty{128}{kg}.
\textbf{15.} \num{10600}~mol $\mathrm{O_2}$, \qty{340}{kg}.
\textbf{16.} $\num{10600}/0.25 = \num{42000}$~\unit{m^3}: one
hectare's leaching can strip the oxygen from forty thousand cubic
metres of bottom water each year --- and the Mississippi drains
a hundred million hectares.
\textbf{17.} $37.5\times 0.01 = \qty{0.375}{kg}$ of nitrogen as
$\mathrm{N_2O}$, that is $0.375\times 44/28 = \qty{0.59}{kg}$ of $\mathrm{N_2O}$;
$\times 270 = \qty{160}{kg}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ equivalent.
\textbf{18.} $150\times 4 = \qty{600}{kg}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$: the
manufacture outweighs the field's $\mathrm{N_2O}$ by four to one, and the
two together are three quarters of a tonne per hectare per year.
\textbf{19.} $\tau = 10$ years; $M^{*} = 5\times 10 = \qty{50}{t}$;
concentration $50/(5\times 10^{7}) = \qty{1}{g/m^3} =
\qty{1000}{mg/m^3}$.
\textbf{20.} Thirty times over the threshold: severely eutrophic.
\textbf{21.} $1/\tau = 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3$: $\tau = 3.3$ years; $M^{*} =
5/0.3 = \qty{16.7}{t}$, \qty{330}{mg/m^3}.
\textbf{22.} $M^{*} = 1/0.3 = \qty{3.3}{t}$, \qty{67}{mg/m^3} --- still
eutrophic; within \qty{10}{\%} after $\tau\ln 10 = 7.7$ years.
\textbf{23.} The sediment is not a one-way sink: phosphorus stored
in it over the eutrophic years is released back under the anoxic
conditions that eutrophication itself creates (iron-bound
phosphate dissolves when the iron is reduced), so the lake feeds
itself for years after the external input is cut --- internal
loading, a second \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} with a long \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time}.
\textbf{24.} $\qty{5000}{kg}/\qty{31}{g/mol} = 1.6\times 10^{5}$~mol P;
carbon $\times 106 = 1.7\times 10^{7}$~mol, \qty{205}{t} of carbon a
year --- \qty{4}{g/m^2} over the lake's surface if it is
\qty{5}{m} deep, a heavy bloom.
\textbf{25.} \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{Airborne fraction} $0.39$ over 1850--2020; in 2100,
\qty{600}{ppm} with emissions held at \qty{10}{GtC/yr} and
\qty{405}{ppm} with emissions cut to zero by 2070 (an optimistic
single-$\tau$ model); nitrogen leached \qty{22.5}{kg} per hectare
per year.
\end{solution}
