\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g12:photosynthesis} --- Photosynthesis}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:1}
A double-membraned \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{organelle} with a fluid stroma containing stacks of
\omterm{def:g12:photosynthesis:chloroplast}{thylakoid} membranes. The pigments and the light-converting \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{proteins}
are in the \omterm{def:g12:photosynthesis:chloroplast}{thylakoid} membranes; the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes} that build sugar are in
the stroma.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:2}
Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red and reflects green, which is what we
see. Blue and red light drive \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} best.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:3}
ATP, reduced NADP and oxygen; the oxygen is the by-product, released.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:4}
From the water that is split, not from the carbon dioxide: algae given
water labelled with heavy oxygen released heavy $\mathrm{O_2}$; given
labelled carbon dioxide, they did not.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:5}
Six $\mathrm{CO_2}$, 18 ATP and 12 reduced NADP.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:6}
At \qty{550}{nm}: absorption about 8\%, rate about 25\%. At
\qty{665}{nm}: absorption about 90\%, rate about 95\%. Green lamps
deliver the light the leaf uses least; the greenhouse would save
energy by growing almost nothing.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:7}
The bacteria swim towards oxygen; they gather where oxygen is being
released, i.e. where the alga photosynthesises; that is under the blue
and red parts of the spectrum. \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{Photosynthesis}, measured by its oxygen,
depends on the colour of the light and follows the pigments'
absorption.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:8}
Oxygen is released without any carbon being fixed: the light phase,
which splits water and moves electrons, is separate from the carbon
phase and does not need $\mathrm{CO_2}$; it needs light, water and
something to receive the electrons.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:9}
Without light there is no ATP and no reduced NADP, so the three-carbon
acid cannot be reduced and accumulates; the acceptor goes on capturing
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ for a while but is no longer regenerated, and is used
up.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:10}
Light intensity falls with distance (roughly as its square: four
times less at twice the distance), and the light phase runs slower.
In the dark no oxygen is released --- and the plant consumes some by
respiration.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:11}
$8/44 \approx \qty{0.18}{mol}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$: $0.18/6 \approx
\qty{0.030}{mol}$ of glucose, about \qty{5.5}{g}; \qty{0.18}{mol} of
$\mathrm{O_2}$, about \qty{5.8}{g}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:12}
It does not itself use light, only the light phase's products. But
in a plant kept in the dark it stops within a minute, once the ATP
and reduced NADP are spent: it runs in the light, fed by the light
phase, and "dark" describes what it needs, not when it happens.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:13}
$^{18}$O: in the released $\mathrm{O_2}$ within a minute, and there
after an hour and a day (some also in water made by respiration).
$^{14}$C: after a minute in the three-carbon acid and sugars of the
cycle; after an hour in sucrose and starch; after a day in cellulose,
\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids}, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acids}, and in the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ respired away.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:14}
$20/1000 = 2\%$. The rest is reflected (the green), absorbed by the
wrong wavelengths and turned into heat, spent in the transpiration
that evaporates water, or lost as heat in the light reactions
themselves.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:photosynthesis:15}
Respiration, combustion and the oxidation of rocks and iron consume
oxygen continuously; without \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} to renew it, the
atmosphere's stock would be used up in a few thousand years. The
early atmosphere had no free oxygen; it accumulated only after
photosynthetic organisms had produced it for a very long time --- the
first two billion years were oxygen-poor.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g12:photosynthesis:1}
\textbf{1.} $8/44 \approx \qty{0.18}{mol}$.

\textbf{2.} $0.18/6 = \qty{0.030}{mol}$, about \qty{5.5}{g}.

\textbf{3.} \qty{0.18}{mol} of $\mathrm{O_2}$, about \qty{4.4}{L}.

\textbf{4.} Two water molecules per $\mathrm{O_2}$: \qty{0.36}{mol},
about \qty{6.5}{g} --- one per cent of the \qty{600}{mL} transpired.

\textbf{5.} From the water molecules split in the \omterm{def:g12:photosynthesis:chloroplast}{thylakoids}: shown by
the heavy-oxygen labelling, which appears in the $\mathrm{O_2}$ only
when the water is labelled.

\textbf{6.} One turn per $\mathrm{CO_2}$: \qty{0.18}{mol} of turns.

\textbf{7.} Three ATP and two reduced NADP per turn: \qty{0.55}{mol}
of ATP and \qty{0.36}{mol} of reduced NADP.

\textbf{8.} Each water split gives two electrons, one reduced NADP:
\qty{0.36}{mol} of water --- the same figure as question 4, as it must
be.

\textbf{9.} Oxygen would stop at once; reduced NADP and ATP would stop
being made and be exhausted within seconds; sugar would stop as soon
as the carriers ran out.

\textbf{10.} The carriers would no longer be spent by the \omterm{prop:g12:photosynthesis:calvin}{Calvin
cycle}; with nothing to receive their electrons and energy, the light
phase would back up and slow within seconds --- the two phases are
coupled by the carriers.

\textbf{11.} $0.030 \times 2870 \approx \qty{86}{kJ}$ per hour, i.e.
$86000/3600 \approx \qty{24}{W}$.

\textbf{12.} $24/1000 = 2.4\%$ of the full sunlight; $24/450 \approx
5\%$ of the absorbed wavelengths.

\textbf{13.} $8 \times 176 \approx \qty{1400}{kJ}$ of photons per mole
of $\mathrm{CO_2}$; stored: $2870/6 \approx \qty{480}{kJ}$.

\textbf{14.} About a third; the rest is released as heat in the
successive energy transfers of the two phases.

\textbf{15.} Net gain $5.5 - 0.5 = \qty{5}{g}$ of glucose per hour.
Measured as the net $\mathrm{CO_2}$ uptake in the light; the
respiration alone is measured in the dark and added back to get the
gross \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis}.

\textbf{16.} $\num{8.5e11}/\num{1e11} \approx 8.5$ years --- with the
ocean's algae fixing as much again, about 4 years.

\textbf{17.} Respiration (of plants, animals, fungi and bacteria),
with fire, returns carbon dioxide at nearly the same rate; fixation
and return balanced, so the stock was steady until fossil-fuel burning
added a new source.

\textbf{18.} \qty{1e11}{t} of carbon is $\num{8.3e12}\,\unit{mol}$; the
same number of moles of $\mathrm{O_2}$ is about \qty{2.7e11}{t} of
oxygen.

\textbf{19.} $\num{1.2e15}/\num{2.7e11} \approx 4500$ years of
production (about 2000 with the ocean's share). But respiration
consumes oxygen at nearly the same rate as \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} makes it,
so the stock is a balance, not an accumulation: the oxygen in the air
was built up over hundreds of millions of years by the small excess
of production over consumption.

\textbf{20.} About 2\% of the full sunlight (5\% of the usable
wavelengths) is stored as sugar; the oxygen released is the oxygen of
the water split; roughly a quarter of the atmosphere's carbon passes
through \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:autotrophy}{photosynthesis} each year.
\end{solution}
