\chapter{Genome Stability: DNA Damage, Repair and Recombination}\label{ch:b3:dna-repair}

An afternoon on a beach puts some hundred thousand chemical \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions}
into the DNA of every skin cell exposed to the sun: adjacent
thymines welded into dimers by ultraviolet light, bases oxidised,
strands nicked. Without any sun at all, the warm water of the cell
knocks about ten thousand purines off their sugars every day and
turns hundreds of cytosines into uracil. Yet the mutation rate of a
human cell is about one change in ten billion base pairs per division
--- in a genome of six billion, less than one new mutation per
division. The gap between the damage a genome suffers and the damage
it keeps is closed by repair: a dozen enzyme systems that patrol the
double helix, recognise what is not supposed to be there, cut it out
and rebuild from the undamaged strand, and, when both strands are
broken, rejoin them or copy the missing piece from a sister. The
children who lack one of these systems --- burning at the first touch
of sunlight, or developing colon cancer in their thirties --- show
what the systems are worth. This chapter is about damage, the repair
pathways, the recombination machinery that repairs the worst breaks
and that meiosis borrows, and the cell's decision, when repair fails,
to stop dividing or to die.

\section{What goes wrong with DNA}

\begin{definition}[DNA lesions]\label{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}
\index{DNA lesion}\index{depurination}\index{deamination}\index{pyrimidine dimer}\index{double-strand break}\index{8-oxoguanine}
A \emph{lesion} is any chemical alteration of DNA away from the four
normal bases correctly paired on an intact backbone. Spontaneous
lesions arise from the chemistry of water and oxygen: \emph{depurination},
hydrolysis of the bond between a purine and its sugar, leaving an
abasic site (about $10^{4}$ per cell per day); \emph{deamination},
which turns cytosine into uracil, 5-methylcytosine into thymine, and
adenine into hypoxanthine (a few hundred per day); \emph{oxidation}
by reactive oxygen species, chiefly to \emph{8-oxoguanine}, which
pairs with adenine as readily as with cytosine; and \emph{replication
errors}, a misincorporated or slipped base. Environmental lesions
include the \emph{cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer} and the 6-4
photoproduct made by ultraviolet light between two adjacent
pyrimidines; alkylated bases from alkylating agents (O$^{6}$-methyl-
guanine pairs with thymine); bulky adducts from aromatic
hydrocarbons and aflatoxin; interstrand cross-links from cisplatin
and nitrogen mustards; and the single- and \emph{double-strand breaks}
made by ionising radiation, about \num{1000} single-strand and
\numrange{30}{40} double-strand breaks per cell per gray.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The fidelity ladder]\label{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}
\index{fidelity}\index{proofreading}\index{mismatch repair}
Replication achieves its error rate of about $10^{-10}$ per base pair
in three multiplicative steps. Base selection by the polymerase, on
geometry and hydrogen bonding, errs about once in $10^{5}$;
\emph{proofreading} by the polymerase's 3$'\to$5$'$ exonuclease, which
excises a mispaired terminal base before extension, removes
\qty{99}{\%} of those; \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair}}, acting behind the fork
on the newly made strand, removes \qty{99.9}{\%} of what remains. The
product $10^{-5}\times 10^{-2}\times 10^{-3} = 10^{-10}$ is the
observed rate, and a defect in either correction step raises it a
hundred- or a thousand-fold: such cells are \emph{mutators}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Lesions against mutations]\label{ex:b3:dna-repair:numbers}
A human cell of $6.4\times 10^{9}$ base pairs dividing once a day
accumulates on the order of $10^{4}$--$10^{5}$ \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} a day and
about $6.4\times 10^{9}\times 10^{-10} \approx 0.6$ new mutations per
division: fewer than one \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion} in ten thousand survives to become a
permanent change. The rest are repaired before replication reads
them, and the rate of survival to mutation --- not the rate of damage
--- is what selection has minimised. The same arithmetic shows why the
number of mutations in a tumour or in the sperm of an older father
counts divisions: the mutations a cell carries are, to first order,
the divisions it has undergone times the error per division.
\end{example}

\section{Repair of one damaged strand}

\begin{definition}[Excision repair]\label{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}
\index{base excision repair}\index{nucleotide excision repair}\index{glycosylase}\index{xeroderma pigmentosum}
Most \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} affect one strand and are repaired by cutting out the
damaged piece and copying the other strand. \emph{Base excision repair}
(BER) handles small, non-distorting \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions}: a \emph{DNA glycosylase}
specific for one kind of wrong base (uracil-DNA glycosylase, 8-oxoG
glycosylase, and several others) flips the base out of the helix and
cuts it off its sugar; an AP endonuclease nicks the backbone at the
abasic site; a polymerase (Pol~$\beta$) removes the sugar and inserts
one nucleotide; a ligase seals. \emph{Nucleotide excision repair}
(NER) handles bulky, helix-distorting \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} --- \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{pyrimidine dimers},
chemical adducts --- without recognising the chemistry of the \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion}
itself: the distortion is detected (by XPC scanning the genome, or by
a stalled RNA polymerase in the transcription-coupled branch), the
helix is opened by the helicase subunits of TFIIH, the damage is
verified (XPA), two endonucleases (XPF, XPG) cut the damaged strand
\numrange{24}{32} nucleotides apart, the oligonucleotide is released,
and polymerase and ligase fill the gap. Loss of any of the seven
XP proteins causes \emph{xeroderma pigmentosum}: extreme sensitivity to
sunlight, freckling, and a thousand-fold increased rate of skin
cancer.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Cleaver (1968) cultured skin fibroblasts from \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{xeroderma pigmentosum}
patients, irradiated them with ultraviolet light and gave them
radioactive thymidine outside S phase. Normal cells incorporated
label at many small sites --- the ``unscheduled DNA synthesis'' of
repair patches --- while the patients' cells incorporated almost none,
and died at doses normal cells survived: the disease is a failure to
excise the dimers. Fusing cells from two patients often restored
repair in the hybrid, each cell supplying what the other lacked; by
this test the patients were sorted into complementation groups A to
G, one per gene of the pathway, years before the genes were cloned.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Mismatch repair]\label{def:b3:dna-repair:mmr}
\index{mismatch repair!strand discrimination}\index{Lynch syndrome}\index{microsatellite instability}
\emph{\omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{Mismatch repair}} (MMR) corrects the mispairs and small
insertion--deletion loops that escape proofreading. The MutS protein
(MSH2--MSH6 in humans) recognises the distortion of a mismatch and,
with MutL (MLH1--PMS2), licenses an exonuclease to degrade the newly
synthesised strand from a nearby nick back past the mismatch; the
gap is refilled by polymerase. The difficulty is \emph{strand
discrimination}: a mismatch has two bases and only one is wrong. In
\emph{Escherichia coli} the new strand is identified because it is not
yet methylated at GATC sites, which the Dam methylase marks a few
minutes after synthesis; MutH nicks the unmethylated strand. In
eukaryotes the new strand is identified by the nicks between Okazaki
fragments and the free 3$'$ end of the leading strand. Loss of MMR
raises the mutation rate a hundred- to a thousand-fold, most visibly
at \emph{microsatellites} --- runs of a short repeat where the
polymerase slips --- whose lengths change from cell to cell:
\emph{microsatellite instability}. Inherited loss of one MMR allele is
\emph{Lynch syndrome}, with colorectal cancer in some \qty{70}{\%} of
carriers, often before fifty.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Sorting a repair disease into genes]\label{met:b3:dna-repair:complementation}
\index{complementation test}
To find how many genes a recessive phenotype involves, before any gene
is known: (1) take cell lines from unrelated patients, each mutant in
some gene of the pathway; (2) fuse pairs of lines into hybrid cells;
(3) test the hybrid for the function (here, unscheduled DNA synthesis
after ultraviolet light); (4) if the hybrid is repaired, the two
patients carry mutations in \emph{different} genes (each supplies the
other's missing protein: they \emph{complement}); if not, in the same
gene; (5) group the patients into complementation groups --- one per
gene. The count of groups is a lower bound on the number of genes.
\end{method}

\section{Repair of a broken chromosome}

\begin{definition}[Double-strand break repair]\label{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}
\index{non-homologous end joining}\index{homologous recombination}\index{Rad51}\index{Holliday junction}\index{BRCA}
A \emph{\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand break}} severs the chromosome and has no intact
strand to copy. Two pathways repair it. \emph{Non-homologous end
joining} (NHEJ): the Ku70--Ku80 ring binds each end, recruits the
kinase DNA-PKcs, nucleases trim overhangs, and ligase IV joins the
ends; it works at any stage of the cycle and in minutes, but loses or
adds a few nucleotides at the junction and can join the wrong ends.
\emph{Homologous recombination} (HR): the MRN complex and nucleases
resect the 5$'$ strands to leave long 3$'$ single-stranded tails,
which are coated by RPA and then, with the help of BRCA2, by the
recombinase \emph{Rad51}; the Rad51 filament searches for a homologous
duplex --- the sister chromatid, in S and G2 --- invades it, and the
invading 3$'$ end primes synthesis on the intact copy. The joint
molecules may be dissolved after a short synthesis (most mitotic
repair, no exchange of flanking DNA) or mature into two four-way
\emph{Holliday junctions}, whose resolution by endonucleases yields
either a crossover or a non-crossover product. HR is accurate because
it copies a sister, but is available only when a sister exists.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Damage load, repair rate and the fork]\label{thm:b3:dna-repair:load}
\index{residual damage}\index{competing pathways}
Let \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} of one kind arise at a constant rate $D$ per cell per
hour and be removed by first-order repair with rate constant $k$
(half-life $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k$). The number of \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} $L(t)$ obeys
$\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}t = D - kL$, so that at steady state
\[
L^{*} = \frac{D}{k},
\]
and a pulse of $N$ \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} delivered at $t = 0$ leaves $N e^{-kT}$
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} when a replication fork arrives at time $T$. If two pathways
compete for the same \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion} with rate constants $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$,
each \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion} is repaired by the first with probability $k_{1}/(k_{1} +
k_{2})$ and the overall half-life is $\ln 2/(k_{1} + k_{2})$. The
number of \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} that reach the fork is Poisson-distributed with mean
$N e^{-kT}$, so the probability that none does is $\exp(-N e^{-kT})$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The steady state is where $D = kL$. For the pulse, $D = 0$ and the
equation integrates to $L = N e^{-kt}$. With two pathways the loss
rate is $(k_{1} + k_{2})L$; in any short interval a given \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion} is
removed by pathway~1 with probability $k_{1}\,\mathrm{d}t$ and by
pathway~2 with $k_{2}\,\mathrm{d}t$, so the conditional probability
that its removal, when it happens, is by pathway~1 is $k_{1}/(k_{1} +
k_{2})$. Independent \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} each surviving with probability $e^{-kT}$
give a binomial count, Poisson in the limit of many \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} and small
survival, and the Poisson probability of zero is $e^{-\text{mean}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Why xeroderma is a disease of the fork]\label{ex:b3:dna-repair:xp}
A dose of sun leaves $N = 10^{5}$ \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{pyrimidine dimers} in a keratinocyte.
With a nucleotide excision half-life of \qty{2}{h} ($k =
\qty{0.35}{h^{-1}}$) and a fork arriving after \qty{8}{h}, $N e^{-kT} =
10^{5}\times e^{-2.8} \approx 6000$ dimers are still there to be
copied; with the near-absent repair of an XP cell ($k \approx
\qty{0.01}{h^{-1}}$), \num{92000} are. Each dimer met by the fork is
bypassed by \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:tls}{translesion synthesis} (below), which is error-prone: the
patient's mutation load per division is fifteen times the normal one,
and the skin cancers follow in childhood. The cure that works is to
keep $N$ small --- total avoidance of ultraviolet light.
\end{example}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Holliday (1964) proposed the four-way junction to explain a puzzle of
fungal genetics: in a cross, a tetrad of spores sometimes showed a
$3{:}1$ instead of the Mendelian $2{:}2$ segregation of a marker,
always for markers close to a crossover. His model --- the exchange of
single strands between two homologous duplexes, forming a
heteroduplex that \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair} then ``corrects'' in one direction
or the other (\emph{gene conversion}) --- explained both the odd
ratios and their association with crossing over. The junction itself
was later seen in the electron microscope in recombining plasmids and
in the structure of the resolvase RuvC bound to it.
\end{proof}

\section{Tolerance, alarm and the decision to stop}

\begin{definition}[Translesion synthesis]\label{def:b3:dna-repair:tls}
\index{translesion synthesis}\index{polymerase eta}
When a fork meets a \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion} that has not been repaired, the replicative
polymerase stalls. \emph{Translesion synthesis} (TLS) calls in a
specialised polymerase with a roomier active site and no proofreading
--- Pol~$\eta$ for \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{pyrimidine dimers}, Pol~$\iota$, $\kappa$, $\zeta$
and Rev1 for other \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} --- which inserts a few nucleotides
opposite the \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion} and steps aside. Pol~$\eta$ puts two adenines
opposite a thymine dimer, which is usually right; the others are
often wrong. TLS trades accuracy for the survival of the fork, and
is the source of most ultraviolet-induced mutations. The variant form
of \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{xeroderma pigmentosum} (XP-V) has intact excision repair and lacks
Pol~$\eta$: the dimers that escape excision are bypassed by the more
error-prone polymerases, and the cancers follow.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The bacterial SOS response]\label{prop:b3:dna-repair:sos}
\index{SOS response}\index{RecA}\index{LexA}
In \emph{E.~coli} the recombinase RecA, bound to the single-stranded
DNA that accumulates at stalled forks and resected breaks, becomes a
co-protease that induces the repressor LexA to cleave itself. LexA
represses some forty genes; as it falls, they are switched on in order
of their operators' affinity: first the excision-repair genes
\emph{uvrA} and \emph{uvrB}, then \emph{recA} itself and the cell-division
inhibitor \emph{sulA} (the cells stop dividing and grow into
filaments), and last the error-prone polymerases Pol~IV and Pol~V,
which bypass \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} at the cost of mutations. The graded response
tries accurate repair first and mutagenic tolerance only when the
damage persists; the mutations it induces are the raw material on which
antibiotic resistance evolves under treatment.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[The DNA damage response]\label{def:b3:dna-repair:ddr}
\index{DNA damage response}\index{ATM}\index{checkpoint}\index{p53}\index{gamma-H2AX}
Eukaryotes signal damage through two kinases: \emph{ATM}, recruited by
the MRN complex to \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand breaks}, and \emph{ATR}, recruited to
the single-stranded DNA of stalled forks. They phosphorylate hundreds
of targets. Within minutes \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} H2AX is phosphorylated
(\emph{$\gamma$-H2AX}) over megabases around each break, forming a
focus that recruits repair proteins and can be counted under the
microscope, one focus per break. The kinases Chk1 and Chk2 halt the
cell cycle --- the \emph{checkpoints} of \cref{ch:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis}
--- by inactivating the phosphatases that would drive entry into S or
M; and the transcription factor \emph{p53}, stabilised by
phosphorylation, induces the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21 (a
durable arrest in G1), repair genes, and, if the damage is heavy or
persists, the apoptotic genes that kill the cell. The response buys
time for repair, and when repair fails removes the cell rather than
let it divide with a broken genome.
\end{definition}

\includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{images/book5/ai/b5-03-gamma-foci.jpg}

{\small $\gamma$-H2AX foci (green) in nuclei (blue) an hour after
irradiation. Each focus marks a \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand break}; counting them
measures the damage and, over the following hours, its repair.}
\end{omfigure}

\begin{example}[Synthetic lethality: BRCA and PARP]\label{ex:b3:dna-repair:parp}
Women who inherit one defective copy of \emph{BRCA1} or \emph{BRCA2}
have a lifetime risk of breast cancer of \qtyrange{50}{80}{\%}; the
tumours arise in cells that have lost the second copy and can no
longer do \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{homologous recombination}. Such cells repair their
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand breaks} by end joining alone and accumulate
rearrangements. They also acquire a specific weakness. Single-strand
breaks, some \num{10000} a day, are repaired by a route that needs the
enzyme PARP; when PARP is inhibited by a drug, single-strand breaks
persist to S phase, where a fork converts each into a \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand
break} with only one end --- repairable only by recombination. A normal
cell, with one good \emph{\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{BRCA}} allele, copes; the tumour cell dies.
Two defects, each harmless alone, are lethal together: the drug kills
by \emph{synthetic lethality}, and spares the patient's other cells.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Recombination as a tool]\label{prop:b3:dna-repair:tools}
\index{site-specific recombination}\index{Cre-lox}
Site-specific recombinases cut and rejoin DNA at short defined
sequences without homology search or synthesis: the phage P1 enzyme
Cre acts on \qty{34}{bp} \emph{loxP} sites, the yeast enzyme Flp on
\emph{FRT} sites. Two sites in the same orientation on one molecule
excise the DNA between them as a circle; in opposite orientations they
invert it; sites on two molecules exchange their flanks. A gene
flanked by \emph{loxP} sites (``floxed'') is deleted only in the cells
where Cre is expressed, from a promoter of the experimenter's choice,
or at a time the experimenter chooses if Cre is made active by a drug:
this is how a gene essential to the embryo is studied in the adult
liver, or a neuron's, alone (\cref{ch:b3:genetic-engineering}). The
cell's own \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{homologous recombination} is what gene targeting and
CRISPR-directed editing borrow to write a chosen sequence into a
chosen place.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Repair and ageing]\label{rem:b3:dna-repair:ageing}
Cells that fail to repair accumulate mutations, senesce or die; the
inherited defects of repair (Werner syndrome, a helicase; Cockayne
syndrome, transcription-coupled repair; ataxia telangiectasia, \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:ddr}{ATM})
cause features of premature ageing, and the somatic mutation count of
normal tissues rises linearly with age at a rate of some tens of
mutations per cell per year. Whether accumulated damage \emph{causes}
ordinary ageing, or merely accompanies it, is not settled; that the
capacity to repair is a determinant of lifespan across species ---
longer-lived species repair more --- is well supported.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:1}
Classify these \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} as spontaneous or environmental and name the
repair pathway for each: a uracil in DNA; a thymine dimer; an
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{8-oxoguanine}; a G--T mismatch behind the fork; a \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand break}
in G1.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:2}
Why does \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{nucleotide excision repair} need no enzyme specific to each
kind of \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion}, whereas \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{base excision repair} needs a \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{glycosylase} for
each?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:3}
State the strand-discrimination problem of \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair} and how
\emph{E.~coli} solves it. What would happen in a \emph{dam} mutant,
which methylates no GATC sites?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:4}
A cell receives \qty{2}{Gy} of X-rays. How many \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand breaks}
does it suffer, roughly, and how many $\gamma$-H2AX foci would you
expect to count an hour later? Why fewer after six hours?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:5}
Using \cref{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}, compute the mutation rate
per base pair and the number of new mutations per diploid genome per
division in (a) a normal cell, (b) a cell lacking \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair},
(c) a cell whose polymerase has lost its exonuclease. Which is a more
dangerous mutator?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:6}
Abasic sites arise at $10^{4}$ per cell per day and are repaired with
a half-life of \qty{15}{min}. Use \cref{thm:b3:dna-repair:load} to find
the steady-state number of abasic sites in a cell. If a drug slows
repair tenfold, what is the new steady state, and how many sites would
a fork meet if it copies the genome in \qty{8}{h}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:7}
In G2 a break can be repaired by NHEJ (half-life \qty{30}{min}) or HR
(half-life \qty{4}{h}), both available. What fraction of breaks goes
by HR? What is the half-life of the breaks? Why is HR nevertheless
the pathway that matters at collapsed replication forks?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:8}
Predict the microsatellite behaviour, the tumour spectrum and the
sunlight sensitivity of a patient with (a) \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:mmr}{Lynch syndrome}, (b)
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{xeroderma pigmentosum} group~A, (c) the XP-V variant lacking
Pol~$\eta$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:9}
Two \emph{loxP} sites in the same orientation flank exon~3 of a gene;
Cre is expressed from a promoter active only in liver from birth.
Describe the genotype of liver cells and of neurons in the adult, and
what happens if the sites are placed in opposite orientations instead.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:10}
Explain why the \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:sos}{SOS response} switches on repair genes before the
error-prone polymerases, using the affinities of LexA for their
operators, and why a bacterium that lacked the error-prone
polymerases altogether might nonetheless be at a disadvantage in a
patient treated with antibiotics.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:11}
A PARP inhibitor kills \emph{\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{BRCA}}-deficient tumour cells but not the
patient's normal cells. Set out the causal chain, then predict two
ways a tumour could become resistant to the drug and how you would
detect each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:dna-repair:12}
Gene conversion at a crossover gives $3{:}1$ tetrads. Draw (or
describe) a \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{Holliday junction} with a heteroduplex spanning a marker,
and show how \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair} of the heteroduplex in one direction
yields $3{:}1$, in the other $1{:}3$, and no repair gives a spore that
is itself heterozygous (a post-meiotic segregation). Why is
conversion confined to markers near the crossover?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A Day in the Life of a Genome}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the daily damage of one human cell
counted, the fidelity ladder multiplied, a dose of sunlight followed
to the fork in a normal and a repair-deficient cell, a dose of X-rays
divided between two repair pathways, and a tumour's weakness turned
into a drug, ending on the number of dimers the fork meets, the
mutation rate per base and the fraction of breaks that recombination
repairs}]
\label{pb:b3:dna-repair:1}
Data: a diploid human cell has $6.4\times 10^{9}$ bp, \qty{1.5}{\%}
of them protein-coding. Spontaneous damage per day: $10^{4}$
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{depurinations}, $400$ cytosine \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{deaminations}, $10^{4}$ single-strand
breaks. Fidelity: polymerase $10^{-5}$, proofreading leaves
$10^{-2}$ of errors, \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair} $10^{-3}$. A sunbath leaves
$10^{5}$ \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{pyrimidine dimers} per skin cell; excision repair has
half-life \qty{2}{h} normally and \qty{70}{h} in \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{xeroderma
pigmentosum}; a fork arrives \qty{8}{h} later; translesion bypass of
a dimer causes a mutation with probability $10^{-3}$. X-rays produce
$35$ \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand breaks} per gray; NHEJ half-life \qty{30}{min}, HR
half-life \qty{4}{h}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Ordinary days.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many spontaneous \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesions} does the cell suffer per day,
        and per year? Compare with the size of the genome.
  \item Compute the mutation rate per base pair per division, and the
        expected number of new mutations per division.
  \item How many of those fall in protein-coding sequence? Over the
        roughly $50$ divisions from zygote to an adult skin cell, how
        many coding mutations does a skin cell carry from replication
        alone?
  \item A cell lacking \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair}: mutation rate and mutations
        per division? A microsatellite slips once in $10^{4}$
        divisions without \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair} and once in $10^{7}$ with
        it. In a tumour of $10^{9}$ cells derived through $40$
        divisions, how many cells carry a slipped allele at a given
        microsatellite in each case? (Estimate with rate $\times$
        divisions $\times$ cells.)
  \item \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{Deamination} of cytosine gives uracil, of 5-methylcytosine
        gives thymine. Which is repaired, by what, and which becomes a
        mutation? Connect to the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG} deficit of
        \cref{ch:b3:chromatin-epigenetics}.
  \item An oxidised guanine (8-oxoG) pairs with A during replication.
        The cell has a \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:excision}{glycosylase} for 8-oxoG opposite C, another for
        A opposite 8-oxoG, and a hydrolase that destroys oxidised dGTP.
        Explain what each prevents and what mutation results when all
        three fail.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- A day in the sun.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the excision rate constants $k$ for the normal and the
        XP cell.
  \item How many dimers remain at the fork in each?
  \item How many mutations does the sunbath cause per cell in each
        case, and how many of them are in coding sequence?
  \item Over a childhood of $500$ such exposures, how many coding
        mutations per skin cell in each case? Compare with the
        replication-only number of question 3.
  \item About $20$ genes, totalling \qty{40}{kb} of coding sequence,
        can drive a skin cancer when mutated. What is the probability,
        per cell, that at least one sunbath-induced mutation lands in
        one of them, in the XP child? (Use a Poisson with the right
        mean.) With $10^{9}$ exposed cells, how many are hit?
  \item Explain why the XP cells' problem is one of the fork, not of
        the \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{lesion} itself, and why the cancers appear in the skin only.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- A dose of X-rays.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A \qty{2}{Gy} dose: how many \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand breaks}, and how many
        $\gamma$-H2AX foci after one hour if NHEJ is the only pathway
        (G1 cell)?
  \item In a G2 cell both pathways operate. Compute the rate constants
        and the fraction of breaks repaired by HR.
  \item What is the half-life of the breaks in G2, and how many remain
        after one hour?
  \item Each NHEJ event alters a few base pairs at the junction. If a
        junction falls in coding sequence with probability $0.015$ and
        the alteration then disrupts the gene with probability $0.7$,
        how many genes does the \qty{2}{Gy} dose disrupt per G1 cell?
  \item With $n$ simultaneous breaks, the ends can be mis-joined into
        a translocation. Suppose each of the $n(n-1)/2$ pairs of breaks
        is joined wrongly with probability $3\times 10^{-4}$. Expected
        translocations per cell at \qty{2}{Gy}? At \qty{0.2}{Gy}? Why
        does the risk scale with the square of the dose while the break
        number scales linearly?
  \item The same \qty{2}{Gy} given over \qty{10}{h} instead of in a
        flash: how many breaks are present at any one time (steady
        state, NHEJ)? What does this imply for translocations, and for
        the practice of fractionating radiotherapy?
  \item A cell survives a dose $D$ with probability $e^{-D/D_{0}}$, with
        $D_{0} = \qty{1.5}{Gy}$ for a cell with intact repair and
        \qty{0.5}{Gy} for one lacking NHEJ. Compute the survival of
        each at \qty{2}{Gy} and at \qty{6}{Gy}, and interpret $D_{0}$.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The tumour's weakness.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A \emph{BRCA2}-deficient tumour cell repairs breaks by NHEJ only.
        Using question 14, what fraction of its S/G2 breaks is now
        repaired inaccurately that a normal cell would have repaired
        accurately? What does this do to its genome over years?
  \item PARP inhibition leaves the $10^{4}$ daily single-strand breaks
        unrepaired; \qty{1}{\%} of them are converted into one-ended
        \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand breaks} by forks. How many such breaks per day
        per cell, and why can NHEJ not repair a one-ended break
        correctly?
  \item Explain why the patient's normal cells, heterozygous for
        \emph{BRCA2}, survive the drug.
  \item A tumour becomes resistant through a second mutation that
        restores the \emph{BRCA2} reading frame. Explain, and say how
        you would detect it.
  \item Chemotherapy with an alkylating agent kills tumour cells
        through \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch repair}: the enzyme tries to repair the
        alkylated bases and triggers cell death. Predict the effect of
        the drug on a Lynch-syndrome tumour, which lacks \omterm{prop:b3:dna-repair:fidelity}{mismatch
        repair}.
  \item State the three results: the dimers met by the fork in the XP
        cell (question~8), the normal mutation rate per base pair
        (question~2), and the fraction of G2 breaks that HR repairs
        (question~14).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
