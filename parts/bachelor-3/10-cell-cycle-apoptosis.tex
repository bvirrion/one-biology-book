\chapter{Cell Cycle Control and Programmed Cell Death}\label{ch:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis}

About a hundred billion cells of your body will die today, on purpose,
and a hundred billion will be born to replace them; the lining of your
gut is renewed every five days, and your red cells every four months.
A tadpole loses its tail without bleeding; the webbing between a human
embryo's fingers vanishes in the eighth week; half the neurons ever
made in a brain are killed before birth. Every one of these deaths is
executed by the cell itself, on a programme, and every one of the
births is licensed by a control system that decides, at two or three
points in the cycle, whether the cell may proceed. The two programmes
--- division and suicide --- are the subject of this chapter. They were
worked out in yeast, frog eggs, sea urchins and a worm with exactly
$1090$ somatic cells of which exactly $131$ die, and they turned out to
be the same in humans, where their failures are cancer on one side and
degeneration on the other.

\section{The engine of the cycle}

\begin{definition}[Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases]\label{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}
\index{cyclin}\index{cyclin-dependent kinase}\index{anaphase-promoting complex}\index{cell cycle}
The cell cycle --- G1, S (replication), G2, M (mitosis) --- is driven by
a family of protein kinases, the \emph{cyclin-dependent kinases}
(CDKs), whose catalytic subunits are present throughout the cycle but
active only when bound to a \emph{cyclin}, a regulatory subunit whose
concentration rises and falls in a fixed order: cyclin D with Cdk4/6 in
G1 in response to growth factors, cyclin E with Cdk2 at the G1/S
transition, cyclin A with Cdk2 through S, cyclin B with Cdk1 at the
entry into mitosis. Each cyclin--CDK phosphorylates the proteins of its
phase --- replication origins, lamins, condensins, the enzymes of
spindle assembly --- and each is switched off by the destruction of
its cyclin: ubiquitin ligases (SCF in G1/S, the \emph{anaphase-promoting
complex}, APC/C, in mitosis) tag the cyclins for the proteasome. The
activity of Cdk1 is further gated by an inhibitory phosphorylation put
on by the kinase Wee1 and removed by the phosphatase Cdc25, and by
small inhibitor proteins (p21, p27, p16) that bind the complexes. The
cycle is thus an ordered sequence of kinase waves, each wave ending in
the proteolysis of what produced it.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Three lines converged. Hartwell (1970s) isolated temperature-sensitive
\emph{cdc} mutants of budding yeast, each arrested at one stage with a
characteristic bud size, and defined \emph{Start}, the point in G1 after
which a cell is committed to a cycle. Nurse (1980s) found in fission
yeast that \emph{cdc2} controlled the timing of mitosis and that the
human gene, put into the yeast, rescued the mutant: the kinase was
Cdk1, conserved from yeast to man. Hunt (1983) found in sea urchin
eggs a protein that accumulated through each cycle and was destroyed
abruptly at every division, and called it \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin}. The frog egg had
meanwhile yielded a ``maturation-promoting factor'' that drove any
nucleus into mitosis; it was \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} B bound to Cdk1. Rao and Johnson
(1970) had shown by fusing cells that an S-phase cell drives a G1
nucleus into replication, while a G2 nucleus waits --- the cytoplasm
carries the signal, and a replicated nucleus cannot be made to
replicate again.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[A switch and a delayed brake make an oscillator]\label{thm:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:oscillator}
\index{relaxation oscillator}\index{bistability!cell cycle}\index{hysteresis}
Let $A$ be the activity of \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} B--Cdk1 and $C$ the amount of \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin}
B. Suppose (i) for a fixed $C$, $A$ relaxes quickly to a steady state
that depends on $C$ through a positive feedback (active Cdk1 activates
its activator Cdc25 and inhibits its inhibitor Wee1), so that the
steady-state curve $A^{*}(C)$ is S-shaped: for $C$ between two
thresholds $C_{1} < C_{2}$ both a low and a high state exist, and the
system stays on whichever branch it is on (\emph{hysteresis}); (ii) $C$
changes slowly, synthesised at a constant rate $k_{s}$ and degraded at
a rate $k_{d}A\,C$ through the APC/C, which active Cdk1 switches on
after a delay. Then the system has no stable steady state and executes
a \emph{relaxation oscillation}: $C$ accumulates on the low branch until
it passes $C_{2}$, $A$ jumps to the high branch (mitotic entry),
degradation outruns synthesis and $C$ falls until it passes $C_{1}$, $A$
collapses to the low branch (mitotic exit), and the cycle repeats. The
period is set mainly by the slow variable: about $C_{2}/k_{s}$ for the
rise, plus the time to degrade from $C_{2}$ to $C_{1}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Partial proof]
A steady state of the pair would need $\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = 0$,
that is $k_{s} = k_{d}A^{*}(C)\,C$, at a point on the S-shaped curve.
On the low branch $A^{*}$ is small, so $k_{d}A^{*}C < k_{s}$ for $C$ up
to $C_{2}$: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} rises, and the system is carried off the branch's
end. On the high branch $A^{*}$ is large, so $k_{d}A^{*}C > k_{s}$ for
$C$ down to $C_{1}$: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} falls, and the system is carried off that
end. The only candidate steady state would lie on the middle,
unstable branch, and a point there repels; hence there is no stable
steady state, and the trajectory, confined to the two stable branches
and the jumps between them, cycles. The time on the low branch is
$\int \mathrm{d}C/(k_{s} - k_{d}A^{*}C) \approx C_{2}/k_{s}$ when
$A^{*}$ is small, and the time on the high branch is the shorter time
to degrade $C_{2} - C_{1}$ at rate $k_{d}A^{*}_{\text{high}}C$. The
existence of the S-shaped curve from the Cdc25/Wee1 feedback is
admitted here; it is the same bistability as the self-activating gene
of the Year~2 volume, with the fast variable now a kinase and the slow
one its \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Why the frog egg is a clock]\label{ex:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:frog}
A fertilised frog egg divides twelve times in six hours with no
growth, no transcription and no \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoints}, at intervals of
\qty{30}{min}: it is the oscillator of the theorem running bare, and an
extract of its cytoplasm in a test tube goes on cycling, \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} rising
and falling, with nothing to divide. Adding a non-degradable \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} B
locks the extract in mitosis, since $C$ can never fall below $C_{1}$;
blocking \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} synthesis locks it in interphase. In a somatic cell the
same engine is wrapped in the controls of the next section, which hold
it at the thresholds until conditions are met, so that the period
becomes a day rather than half an hour and can be indefinitely long.
\end{example}

\section{Checkpoints}

\begin{definition}[Checkpoints]\label{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}
\index{checkpoint}\index{restriction point}\index{spindle assembly checkpoint}\index{replication licensing}
A \emph{checkpoint} is a control that halts the cycle at a transition
until a condition is satisfied, by acting on the engine --- inhibiting
a CDK or an ubiquitin ligase. Three matter most. The \emph{restriction
point} in late G1 (Start in yeast): the cell proceeds only if growth
factors, nutrients and size permit; beyond it the cycle completes
without them. The \emph{G2/M checkpoint}: damaged or unreplicated DNA,
signalled by the \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:ddr}{ATM}/ATR kinases of \cref{ch:b3:dna-repair}, keeps
Cdc25 inhibited and so keeps Cdk1 phosphorylated and inactive. The
\emph{spindle assembly checkpoint}: a single kinetochore not attached
to \omterm{def:b3:cytoskeleton-motility:filaments}{microtubules} produces a diffusible inhibitor (Mad2 bound to Cdc20)
that keeps the APC/C off, so that anaphase waits for the last
chromosome. A fourth control has no waiting step but is equally
strict: \emph{replication licensing}. Origins are loaded with the MCM
helicase only in G1, when CDK activity is low, and the loading factors
are destroyed or inhibited once S phase begins, so that each origin
fires once and only once per cycle --- the reason a G2 nucleus in Rao
and Johnson's fusions could not be made to replicate.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The restriction point is a switch]\label{prop:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:rb}
\index{retinoblastoma protein}\index{E2F}
In early G1 the transcription factor \emph{E2F}, which drives the genes
of S phase (\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} E, \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} A, the replication enzymes), is held
inactive by the \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:rb}{retinoblastoma protein}} Rb. Growth factors induce
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} D, and \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} D--Cdk4/6 begins to phosphorylate Rb, releasing a
little E2F; E2F then induces \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} E, and \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} E--Cdk2 phosphorylates
Rb much more --- a positive feedback that, once past a threshold,
completes itself without further growth factor. The cell has crossed
the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{restriction point}; E2F also induces its own gene. Loss of Rb, or
of the Cdk4/6 inhibitor p16, or amplification of \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} D, removes
the requirement for growth factor altogether, and each is common in
cancer (\cref{ch:b3:cancer-biology}); drugs that inhibit Cdk4/6 are now
used against breast cancers that depend on this switch.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Anaphase is a proteolytic decision]\label{prop:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:anaphase}
\index{securin}\index{separase}\index{cohesin}
Sister chromatids are held together from S phase by rings of
\emph{cohesin}. At metaphase, when every kinetochore is attached and
the spindle \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint} is silenced, the APC/C with its activator Cdc20
ubiquitinates two proteins: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} B, whose loss inactivates Cdk1 and
allows the cell to exit mitosis, and \emph{securin}, whose loss frees the
protease \emph{separase}, which cuts cohesin. All sister pairs separate
within seconds of each other, pulled to the poles by the spindle, and
the cell divides by a ring of actin and \omterm{def:b3:cytoskeleton-motility:motors}{myosin}~II assembled where the
spindle midzone tells the cortex (through RhoA). The decision is
irreversible because it is proteolytic: a cut cohesin cannot be
reassembled, and the degraded \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} must be resynthesised. Errors
here --- a chromosome pulled to the wrong pole, an anaphase begun
before the last attachment --- produce aneuploid daughters, the
commonest chromosomal abnormality of tumours and of miscarriages.
\end{proposition}

\section{Apoptosis}

\begin{definition}[Apoptosis]\label{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}
\index{apoptosis}\index{necrosis}\index{caspase}\index{phosphatidylserine}
\emph{Apoptosis} is programmed cell death by an intrinsic sequence: the
cell shrinks and rounds up, its \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} condenses and its DNA is cut
between \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes} into a ladder of fragments, the nucleus breaks up,
the membrane blebs into sealed vesicles, phosphatidylserine appears on
the outer face of the membrane as an ``eat me'' signal, and the
fragments are engulfed by neighbours or macrophages within an hour ---
without leakage, and therefore without inflammation. It is executed by
\emph{caspases}, cysteine proteases that cut after aspartate, made as
inactive zymogens: \emph{initiator} caspases (8 and 9) are activated by
being brought together on a platform, and they cleave and activate the
\emph{executioner} caspases (3 and 7), which cut several hundred
substrates --- the lamins, the cytoskeleton, the inhibitor of the
DNA-cutting nuclease, the enzyme that keeps phosphatidylserine inside.
\emph{Necrosis}, by contrast, is death by injury: the cell swells,
bursts, spills its contents and provokes inflammation. Apoptosis was
named and its morphology described by Kerr, Wyllie and Currie (1972);
its genes were found in a worm.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Every \emph{Caenorhabditis elegans} hermaphrodite makes $1090$ somatic
cells, of which the same $131$ die, each at a fixed time and place.
Horvitz and colleagues (1980s) isolated mutants in which they survived:
\emph{ced-3} and \emph{ced-4} were needed for every death, and in their
absence the $131$ cells lived and differentiated; \emph{ced-9}
protected --- its loss killed cells that should have lived, its
over-activity saved cells that should have died; \emph{egl-1} was
needed for particular deaths. CED-3 was a \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{caspase}, CED-4 its
activating platform (Apaf-1 in humans), CED-9 a Bcl-2 protein, EGL-1 a
BH3-only protein: the human pathway, gene for gene. The human
\emph{BCL2} gene had itself been found at a chromosome translocation in
follicular lymphoma, a cancer of cells that fail to die.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[The two pathways]\label{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:pathways}
\index{intrinsic pathway}\index{extrinsic pathway}\index{Bcl-2 family}\index{cytochrome c}\index{apoptosome}\index{death receptor}
The \emph{intrinsic} (mitochondrial) pathway integrates the cell's
internal state. The \emph{Bcl-2 family} has three classes: guardians
(Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, Mcl-1) that sit on the mitochondrial outer membrane and
keep it sealed; effectors (Bax, Bak) that, when activated, oligomerise
into pores in that membrane; and \emph{BH3-only} sensors (Bim, Bid,
Puma, Noxa, Bad), each induced or activated by a particular insult ---
DNA damage through p53 (Puma, Noxa), growth-factor withdrawal (Bim,
Bad), detachment, unfolded proteins --- which bind the guardians and
free or activate the effectors. When effectors win, the outer membrane
is permeabilised, \emph{cytochrome~c} leaks into the cytosol, binds
Apaf-1 and forms the \emph{apoptosome}, which activates \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{caspase}~9. The
\emph{extrinsic} pathway starts outside: \emph{death receptors} (Fas,
TNF receptor, TRAIL receptors) bound by their ligands on a killer
lymphocyte or a neighbour cluster, recruit adaptors and activate
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{caspase}~8, which cleaves \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{caspase}~3 directly and, through Bid, also
opens the mitochondrial route. Both pathways converge on the
executioner \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{caspases}, and inhibitor proteins (IAPs, FLIP) set the
threshold along the way.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The point of no return]\label{prop:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:mompt}
\index{mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation}
The permeabilisation of the mitochondrial outer membrane is sudden
--- all the mitochondria of a cell open within about five minutes,
after hours of deliberation --- and complete: once cytochrome~$c$ is
in the cytosol, \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{caspase} activation follows in minutes and cannot be
undone by removing the stimulus. Two features make it a switch. The
Bax/Bak pore forms by oligomerisation, so its rate rises steeply with
the amount of activated effector; and the guardians and sensors bind
one another with high affinity, so that up to a threshold every sensor
is neutralised and beyond it every extra sensor is free --- a titration,
like a buffer being exhausted. The cell therefore does not die
gradually; it accumulates BH3-only proteins until a threshold is
crossed, and then dies all at once. Drugs that mimic BH3 proteins
(venetoclax, which occupies Bcl-2) push cells that are ``primed'' ---
already near the threshold, as many leukaemic cells are --- over it,
while sparing cells that are not.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Deaths that build a body]\label{ex:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:development}
The fingers are carved from a paddle by the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} of the cells
between them; in the duck the same cells live and the webbing stays.
The tadpole's tail is resorbed at metamorphosis on the signal of
thyroid hormone. About half of the neurons produced in the vertebrate
nervous system die before birth, those that fail to receive enough
trophic factor from their targets --- a competition that matches the
number of neurons to the size of the field they serve. Ninety-five per
cent of the T~cells made in the thymus die there, selected away because
they recognise nothing or recognise the self (\cref{ch:b3:adaptive-immunity}).
And every day the intestinal epithelium sheds its oldest cells at the
villus tips, the skin its keratinocytes, the blood its aged neutrophils
after a life of a day: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} is the routine, and the tissue's size
is the difference between two large rates.
\end{example}

\section{Other exits, and the failure to exit}

\begin{definition}[Regulated necrosis and senescence]\label{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:other}
\index{necroptosis}\index{pyroptosis}\index{senescence}
Cells have other programmed deaths, all inflammatory where \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis}
is silent. \emph{Necroptosis}, triggered by \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:pathways}{death receptors} when
caspase-8 is blocked (as some viruses block it), assembles the kinase
RIPK3 with MLKL, which punctures the plasma membrane. \emph{Pyroptosis},
in infected macrophages, is executed by caspase-1 of the inflammasome
(\cref{ch:b3:innate-immunity}), which cleaves gasdermin into a
pore-former and releases interleukin-1. \emph{Ferroptosis} is death by
iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. A cell can also stop dividing
without dying: \emph{senescence} is a permanent arrest, enforced by p16
and p21 after telomere erosion (\cref{ch:b3:stem-cells}), persistent
DNA damage or oncogene activation, in which the cell stays metabolically
active and secretes inflammatory factors. Senescence removes damaged
cells from the dividing pool --- a tumour suppressor --- and accumulates
with age, where its secretions contribute to the degeneration of
tissues; clearing senescent cells in mice extends healthy life.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Too little and too much]\label{rem:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:disease}
The diseases of these programmes are of dose. Too little death:
cancer, in which the apoptotic threshold is raised by Bcl-2
overexpression or p53 loss, so that cells with damaged genomes survive
to accumulate more damage; autoimmunity, when lymphocytes that should
have died in selection persist. Too much: the loss of T~cells in HIV
infection, the neurons of a stroke's penumbra dying by \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} hours
after the ischaemia (a window for treatment), the slow \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} of
neurons in neurodegenerative disease, the cardiac cells lost after a
heart attack. Cancer treatment is largely the deliberate induction of
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} in cells whose thresholds are lower than their neighbours',
and the drugs that target Bcl-2 and Cdk4/6 are the first designed
from the maps of this chapter.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:5}

\section{Problem: The Life and Death of an Epithelium}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the intestinal lining renewed cell
by cell, the cycle timed from the cyclin oscillator, the errors of a
checkpoint counted across a body, and the apoptotic disposal of a
hundred billion cells a day weighed, ending on the cycle period, the
aneuploid cells produced per day and the mass the body recycles by
apoptosis}]
\label{pb:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:1}
Data: the small intestine has $10^{7}$ crypts, each renewing a villus
of \num{3500} cells every $5$ days; each crypt holds about $22$ stem
cells dividing once a day, whose progeny divide $5$ more times before
differentiating. Oscillator: $k_{s} = 2$ units of \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} per hour,
$C_{2} = 40$ units, $C_{1} = 8$, mitotic \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} half-life
\qty{10}{min}. \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{Checkpoint}: without the spindle \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint} one division
in $50$ missegregates a chromosome; with it, one in \num{50000}. The
body replaces $10^{11}$ cells a day of mass \qty{1}{ng} each, protein
\qty{20}{\%} of the mass; a macrophage clears one apoptotic cell in
\qty{1}{h}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Renewal.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many cells does the small intestine shed per day, and per
        second?
  \item How many cells does one crypt produce per day? Check: $22$ stem
        divisions a day, each committed daughter dividing $5$ more
        times.
  \item If a stem cell divides once a day for a lifetime of $80$ years,
        how many divisions? With a mutation rate of $0.6$ per genome per
        division (\cref{ch:b3:dna-repair}), how many mutations does it
        accumulate?
  \item The transit cells divide every \qty{12}{h}. Why can the tissue
        afford fast, short-lived transit divisions but keeps the stem
        cell slow?
  \item A dose of radiation kills all the dividing transit cells but
        spares the stem cells, which resume within a day. How long
        before the villus, unreplenished, is bare, and how long to
        rebuild it? Why is the gut an early casualty of radiation?
  \item In the colon the shedding at the top is by \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} (anoikis,
        death on detachment). Why is this the right mode of death for a
        cell in contact with gut bacteria?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The clock.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item From the oscillator data, how long does \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} take to rise
        from $C_{1}$ to $C_{2}$?
  \item How long does mitotic degradation take to bring it from $C_{2}$
        back to $C_{1}$?
  \item Period of the bare oscillator, and fraction of the period spent
        with Cdk1 active.
  \item The crypt's transit cells cycle in \qty{12}{h}, the stem cells
        in \qty{24}{h}, the frog egg in \qty{30}{min}. Which of the
        parameters of the model differs, and what supplies the
        difference in a somatic cell?
  \item A cell arrested at the G2/M \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint} by DNA damage keeps
        making \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} B. Where is it on the phase plane, and what holds
        it there? What happens when the damage is repaired?
  \item A drug inhibits the APC/C. Describe the fate of a cell entering
        mitosis in its presence.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Errors.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many divisions per day does the body perform to replace
        $10^{11}$ cells?
  \item How many aneuploid daughters per day with the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint}, and
        without?
  \item Most aneuploid cells die or arrest (p53 responds to
        missegregation). If \qty{1}{\%} survive and divide, how many
        aneuploid dividing cells does a checkpoint-competent body
        produce per day, and over a lifetime?
  \item A tumour of $10^{9}$ cells has lost the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint} and p53.
        How many missegregations per day does it perform, and why does
        this make it evolve fast?
  \item Explain why complete loss of the spindle \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint} is
        lethal to an embryo although partial loss promotes cancer.
  \item Down syndrome arises from a missegregation in meiosis, not
        mitosis. Explain why an error in one cell there affects every
        cell of the child, while one mitotic error affects one lineage.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Disposal.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item What mass of cells does the body dispose of by \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} per
        day, and per year? Compare with body mass.
  \item How much protein is that per day? Compare with a dietary
        intake of \qty{70}{g}: what fraction of the body's protein
        turnover is the recycling of dead cells?
  \item How many macrophages, working continuously, are needed to
        clear the daily dead? The body has about $10^{11}$
        macrophages: what fraction of their time?
  \item Why must the corpse be removed before it lyses, and what
        signals ``eat me''?
  \item A patient's Bcl-2 is overexpressed in B~cells. Which pathway is
        blocked, does the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:pathways}{extrinsic pathway} still work, and why is the
        result a slowly growing lymphoma rather than a fast one?
  \item Neurons in the penumbra of a stroke die by \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} six to
        twenty-four hours after the insult, those in the core by
        \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{necrosis} within minutes. Why does the first offer a window for
        treatment and the second none?
  \item Summarise: the period of the bare oscillator (question~9), the
        aneuploid dividing cells per day in a normal body
        (question~15), and the mass recycled by \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} per day
        (question~19).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
