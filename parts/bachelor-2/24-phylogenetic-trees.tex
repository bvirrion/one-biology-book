\chapter{Phylogenetic Trees and the Molecular Clock}\label{ch:b2:phylogenetic-trees}

In 1965 two chemists, Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling, compared
the amino-acid sequences of haemoglobin from a handful of
vertebrates and noticed something no anatomist could have seen:
the number of differences between two species was proportional to
the time since their ancestors had parted, as read from fossils.
A molecule kept time. Within a decade Carl Woese had used one gene,
the RNA of the small ribosomal subunit, to draw the tree of all
life and to find in it a third domain, the archaea, that no
microscope had distinguished from bacteria. This chapter is about
reconstructing history from resemblance: what a tree means, how one
is built from characters and from distances, how sequences are made
to tell time, and the traps --- convergence, unequal rates,
saturation, long branches --- that a reader of trees must know.

\section{Reading a tree}

\begin{definition}[Phylogeny, clade, homology]\label{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}
\index{phylogeny}\index{clade}\index{monophyletic group}\index{paraphyletic group}\index{polyphyletic group}\index{homology}\index{analogy}\index{synapomorphy}
A \emph{phylogeny} is a tree whose tips are the species (or genes,
or individuals) compared, whose internal nodes are their common
ancestors, and whose root is the oldest ancestor of all. Its
branching order alone carries the history: nodes may be rotated
freely, and only the nesting matters. A \emph{clade} or
\emph{monophyletic group} is an ancestor with \emph{all} its
descendants (birds; mammals; birds and crocodiles together);
a \emph{paraphyletic} group is an ancestor with some descendants
left out (``reptiles'' without birds, ``fish'' without tetrapods,
``\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{prokaryotes}''); a \emph{polyphyletic} group joins descendants of
different ancestors by a resemblance they evolved separately
(``warm-blooded animals''). A character shared by two species
because their ancestor had it is a \emph{homology} (the bones of
the bat's wing and the whale's flipper); one they evolved
independently is an \emph{analogy} or \emph{homoplasy} (the wings
of bats and of birds as wings, the camera eyes of vertebrates and
octopuses). Only homologies record history, and among them only
the \emph{derived} states shared by a group --- its
\emph{synapomorphies}, Hennig's word --- define a clade: feathers
define birds, the ancestral state ``no feathers'' defines nothing.
\end{definition}

\section{Building trees from characters}

\begin{method}[Parsimony]\label{meth:b2:phylogenetic-trees:parsimony}
\index{parsimony}\index{outgroup}\index{bootstrap}
Score the characters (morphological states or aligned sequence
positions) for every taxon. For each possible tree, count the
minimum number of character changes needed to explain the data on
it; the most parsimonious tree needs fewest. Root the tree with an
\emph{outgroup}, a taxon known to lie outside the group studied.
Since the number of unrooted trees for $n$ taxa is $1\cdot 3\cdot
5\cdots(2n - 5)$ --- three for four taxa, $2\times 10^{6}$ for ten,
$10^{20}$ for twenty --- the search is exhaustive only for small
sets and heuristic beyond. Confidence is measured by the
\emph{bootstrap}: resample the characters with replacement a
thousand times, rebuild the tree each time, and record how often
each \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{clade} recurs; a \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{clade} found in \qty{95}{\%} of the resamples is
well supported, one found in half is not.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Four taxa, five sites]\label{ex:b2:phylogenetic-trees:parsimony}
Sequences $A = \mathrm{AAGCT}$, $B = \mathrm{AAGCC}$, $C =
\mathrm{GGACT}$, $D = \mathrm{GGATC}$. On the tree $((A,B),(C,D))$,
sites 1, 2 and 3 each change once (on the internal branch), site 4
once ($\mathrm{C}\to\mathrm{T}$ in $D$) and site 5 twice
($\mathrm{T}\to\mathrm{C}$ in $B$ and in $D$): six steps. On
$((A,C),(B,D))$, sites 1--3 each need two changes, sites 4 and 5
one each: eight. On $((A,D),(B,C))$: nine. The first tree is most
parsimonious, by two steps; the three sites that support it are
its synapomorphies, and site 5 is a homoplasy on it --- the same
change occurring twice, a fact of life in every real data set.
\end{example}

\section{Building trees from distances}

\begin{method}[Clustering by distance: UPGMA]\label{meth:b2:phylogenetic-trees:upgma}
\index{UPGMA}\index{neighbour joining}\index{distance matrix}
From the pairwise distances between $n$ taxa (differences per site,
corrected as below), join the closest pair into a cluster placed at
a node of height half their distance; replace the pair by the
cluster, whose distance to every other taxon is the average of its
members' distances; repeat until one cluster remains. The result
is a rooted tree with all tips at the same height --- it assumes
a constant rate of change on every branch, a \omterm{thm:b2:phylogenetic-trees:clock}{molecular clock}. When
rates differ between lineages, the method groups fast-evolving
lineages together whatever their history; \emph{\omterm{ex:b2:phylogenetic-trees:parsimony}{neighbour joining}},
which at each step joins the pair whose union shortens the whole
tree most, does not assume a clock and is the standard fast method
for large data sets.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[UPGMA]\label{ex:b2:phylogenetic-trees:upgma}
Distances (in differences per hundred sites): $AB = 4$, $AC = 8$,
$AD = 10$, $BC = 8$, $BD = 10$, $CD = 6$. Join $A$ and $B$ at height 2;
then $d(AB, C) = 8$, $d(AB, D) = 10$, $d(C,D) = 6$: join $C$ and $D$ at
height 3; finally $d(AB, CD) = (8 + 10 + 8 + 10)/4 = 9$: join the
two clusters at height $4.5$. The tree is $((A,B),(C,D))$, and if
the rate is $10^{-9}$ substitutions per site per year the root is
$0.045/10^{-9} = 45$ million years old.
\end{example}

\section{The molecular clock}

\begin{theorem}[Substitutions as a Poisson process, and the correction for multiple hits]\label{thm:b2:phylogenetic-trees:clock}
\index{molecular clock}\index{Jukes--Cantor correction}\index{saturation}
If substitutions at a site occur at a constant rate $k$ per year,
independently across sites, the number that accumulate along a
lineage in time $t$ is Poisson with mean $kt$, and the expected
number separating two lineages that diverged $t$ years ago is
\[
d = 2kt
\]
substitutions per site --- the \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:phylogenetic-trees:clock}{molecular clock}}. The observed
proportion $p$ of sites that differ is smaller than $d$, because a
site hit twice may return to its original base or match by chance;
with four bases changing at equal rates,
\[
p = \tfrac{3}{4}\bigl(1 - e^{-4d/3}\bigr), \qquad
d = -\tfrac{3}{4}\ln\bigl(1 - \tfrac{4}{3}p\bigr),
\]
the \emph{Jukes--Cantor} correction. As $d$ grows, $p$ saturates at
$3/4$ --- two random sequences agree at a quarter of their sites
--- and beyond $p \approx 0.5$ the correction magnifies every
sampling error: a gene that has changed that much has stopped
keeping time. Slow genes (ribosomal RNA, histones) date the deep
branches, fast ones (mitochondrial DNA, introns) the recent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $q(t)$ be the probability that a site still carries its
original base after time $t$. It is lost at rate $k$ and regained,
from any of the three other bases, at rate $k/3$:
$\mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}t = -kq + \tfrac{k}{3}(1 - q) = \tfrac{k}{3} -
\tfrac{4k}{3}q$, whose solution with $q(0) = 1$ is $q = \tfrac14 +
\tfrac34 e^{-4kt/3}$. Two lineages separated by total time $2t$ ---
by symmetry, the same as one lineage evolving for $2t$ --- agree at
a site with probability $\tfrac14 + \tfrac34 e^{-4d/3}$ where $d =
2kt$, so $p = 1 - q = \tfrac34(1 - e^{-4d/3})$; inverting gives the
correction. The Poisson count follows from constant, independent
events, as in the physics of radioactive decay, and its standard
deviation $\sqrt{kt}$ sets the precision of any date: a gene of $L$
sites with $dL$ observed substitutions dates a divergence to a
relative precision of about $1/\sqrt{dL}$.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Zuckerkandl and Pauling (1965) found the differences between the
haemoglobins of horse, human, cow and others proportional to the
fossil ages of their splits, and proposed the clock. Fitch and
Margoliash (1967) built a tree of twenty species from cytochrome
$c$ alone and recovered, from a single protein, the classical
groupings of vertebrates, insects and fungi. The decisive test of
method was Hillis and colleagues' experimental \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{phylogeny} (1992):
they propagated bacteriophage T7 through a known, branching series
of eight lineages under a mutagen, sequenced the products, and gave
the sequences to the tree-building methods blind --- parsimony,
distance and likelihood methods all recovered the true tree, and
parsimony reconstructed the ancestral sequences with over
\qty{98}{\%} accuracy.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Pitfalls of the clock and of trees]\label{prop:b2:phylogenetic-trees:pitfalls}
\index{long-branch attraction}\index{rate heterogeneity}\index{incomplete lineage sorting}\index{likelihood (phylogenetics)}
The clock is only approximately constant: rates differ between
genes by a hundredfold (with function --- histone H4 has changed
two residues in a billion years, fibrinopeptides change every few
million), between sites within a gene (third codon positions,
introns and loops change fastest), and between lineages (rodents
tick faster than primates, with their shorter generations and
higher metabolic rates). Every clock therefore needs
\emph{calibration} by a fossil or a dated geological event, and
dates carry the Poisson error above and the calibration's own.
Trees have their own traps. \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:phylogenetic-trees:pitfalls}{Long-branch attraction}}: two
lineages that have each changed a great deal share many sites by
chance, and parsimony joins them whatever their history --- the
cure is denser sampling to break the branches and a method that
models the rates. \emph{Convergence} produces false synapomorphies.
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:phylogenetic-trees:pitfalls}{Incomplete lineage sorting}}: a gene's tree need not be the
species' tree when speciations follow one another quickly, because
the ancestral polymorphism sorts differently into each descendant
--- so a species tree is built from many genes, not one. And in
\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{prokaryotes}, where genes move sideways
(\cref{ch:b2:genome-diversification}), different genes tell
different stories and the history is a web with a tree of ribosomal
genes running through it. Modern practice replaces parsimony and
distance by \emph{likelihood}: given a model of substitution (the
rates of each change, their variation among sites), compute the
probability of the observed sequences on each tree with its branch
lengths, and choose the tree that makes the data most probable;
its Bayesian form yields a probability for each \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{clade} directly.
The Year 3 volume gives the methods; here it is enough to know that
every tree is a hypothesis with a measured support, not a fact.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Woese and Fox (1977) compared small-subunit ribosomal RNA across
\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{prokaryotes} and found the methanogens as distant from the bacteria
as either is from eukaryotes: the three domains, confirmed by every
gene of the transcription and translation machinery since, and
invisible to morphology. The same ribosomal trees, read naively,
placed the fast-evolving microsporidia at the base of the
eukaryotes; genes with slower rates, and models allowing for rate
variation, moved them to the fungi, where they belong --- a
textbook case of \omterm{prop:b2:phylogenetic-trees:pitfalls}{long-branch attraction} detected and cured.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Dating with a fossil]\label{ex:b2:phylogenetic-trees:dating}
A gene of \num{1000} sites differs at \qty{10}{\%} of them between
cow and whale, whose common ancestor the fossils put at 60 million
years. Corrected, $d = -0.75\ln(1 - 0.133) = 0.107$; the rate is $k
= d/2t = 0.107/(1.2\times 10^{8}) = 9\times 10^{-10}$ per site per
year. The same gene differs at \qty{6}{\%} between whale and hippo:
$d = 0.0625$, $t = d/2k = 35$ million years, with about $\pm
\qty{13}{\%}$ from the Poisson count of 62 substitutions and a
further uncertainty from the fossil date. The molecular result
that whales are the hippos' closest living relatives, obtained this
way in the 1990s against every anatomical tree, was confirmed by
the discovery of early whales with the hippo-like ankle bone
predicted --- the clock does not only date; it predicts what the
rocks should hold.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:1}
Define \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{homology}, \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{analogy} and \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{synapomorphy}, with one example of
each among vertebrate limbs, wings and eyes.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:2}
Classify as monophyletic, paraphyletic or polyphyletic: birds;
reptiles (without birds); fish (without tetrapods); flying
vertebrates; mammals; \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{prokaryotes}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:3}
On the tree $(\text{mammals},(\text{turtles},(\text{lizards},
(\text{crocodiles},\text{birds}))))$, name the sister group of
birds, the sister group of mammals, and the smallest \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{clade}
containing lizards and birds. Does swapping the positions of
crocodiles and birds change the tree?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:4}
How many unrooted trees are there for 4, 6 and 10 taxa? How many
rooted trees for 4 (each unrooted tree can be rooted on any of its
branches)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:5}
Sequences $A = \mathrm{CCTAG}$, $B = \mathrm{CCTGG}$, $C =
\mathrm{TTCAG}$, $D = \mathrm{TTCGA}$. Count the steps on each of the
three unrooted trees and give the most parsimonious one. Which
sites are homoplastic on it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:6}
Distances (per hundred sites): $AB = 6$, $AC = 14$, $AD = 16$,
$BC = 14$, $BD = 16$, $CD = 10$. Build the UPGMA tree with its node
heights.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:7}
Correct $p = 0.05$, $0.30$ and $0.60$ for multiple hits. Why is the
third value nearly useless?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:8}
A gene evolves at $10^{-9}$ substitutions per site per year. Two
species differ by $d = 0.02$: when did they split? What $p$ would
you observe between two species that split 500 million years ago,
and what does that imply for dating deep splits with this gene?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:9}
A \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{clade} appears in \qty{52}{\%} of \num{1000} bootstrap resamples;
another in \qty{99}{\%}. Interpret both. What would you do about the
first?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:10}
Explain \omterm{prop:b2:phylogenetic-trees:pitfalls}{long-branch attraction} with the quarter-by-chance argument
and say why adding taxa that break the long branches helps.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:11}
Human and chimpanzee mitochondrial DNA differ at \qty{9}{\%} of
sites; the mitochondrial rate is about $2\times 10^{-8}$ per site
per year. Date the split, give the Poisson uncertainty for a
\qty{16000}{}-site genome, and name two reasons why the answer might
still be wrong by a factor of two.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:phylogenetic-trees:12}
``A gene tree is not a species tree.'' Discuss with \omterm{prop:b2:phylogenetic-trees:pitfalls}{incomplete
lineage sorting}, horizontal transfer and \omterm{prop:b2:genome-diversification:duplication}{gene duplication}, and say
how a species tree is nonetheless obtained.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: Dating the Whales}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- whale, hippo, cow, pig and
camel sequenced for one gene: the tree built by parsimony and by
distance, the clock calibrated on a fossil, every split dated with
its Poisson error, and the anatomical tree confronted with the
molecular one, ending on the whale--hippo divergence time and the
gene's substitution rate}]
\label{pb:b2:phylogenetic-trees:1}
A gene of \num{1000} sites is sequenced in whale ($W$), hippo
($H$), cow ($C$), pig ($P$) and camel ($M$, the outgroup). Observed
proportions of differing sites: $WH = 0.06$; $WC = HC = 0.10$; $WP =
HP = CP = 0.12$; every distance to $M$ is $0.14$. Fossils put the
split between cow and the whale--hippo line at 60 million years.
Six informative sites, with the camel's state given last:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l@{\quad}cccccc}
site & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ \hline
$W$ & A & G & T & C & A & G \\
$H$ & A & G & T & C & G & G \\
$C$ & G & A & T & T & G & G \\
$P$ & G & A & C & T & G & A \\
$M$ & G & A & C & T & G & A \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Parsimony.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which sites are synapomorphies of $\{W, H\}$? Of $\{W, H,
        C\}$? Which site is uninformative, and which is a homoplasy
        or autapomorphy?
  \item Count the steps of the six sites on the tree
        $(M,(P,(C,(W,H))))$.
  \item Count them on the anatomists' tree $(M,(P,(W,(C,H))))$,
        which puts hippo with cow among the even-toed ungulates and
        whales outside.
  \item Which tree is preferred, and by how many steps? Why is that
        margin weak, and what would strengthen it?
  \item The bootstrap gives the $\{W, H\}$ \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{clade} in \qty{83}{\%} of
        resamples of the whole gene. Interpret.
  \item Why is the camel used as the outgroup, and what would go
        wrong if a fish were used instead?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Distances.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Correct the four distinct $p$ values ($0.06$, $0.10$, $0.12$,
        $0.14$) for multiple hits.
  \item Run UPGMA on the five taxa with the corrected distances and
        give the node heights.
  \item Does the distance tree agree with the parsimony tree?
  \item Explain why UPGMA would fail if the whale lineage had
        evolved twice as fast as the others.
  \item Compute the number of substitutions the gene has
        accumulated between whale and hippo, and its Poisson
        standard deviation.
  \item Give the relative precision of a date based on that count.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The clock.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Calibrate: from the cow--(whale, hippo) distance and the
        60-million-year fossil, compute the rate $k$ per site per
        year.
  \item Date the whale--hippo split.
  \item Date the pig split and the camel split.
  \item Give the whale--hippo date with its Poisson uncertainty.
  \item The fossil calibration is itself uncertain by $\pm 5$
        million years. Propagate it to the whale--hippo date.
  \item A different, faster gene gives $p = 0.45$ between whale and
        camel. Correct it and comment on its reliability for this
        split.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Confrontation.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The anatomists' tree rests on the double-pulley ankle bone
        shared by cow, pig, hippo and camel but not by living
        whales, which have no hind limbs. Explain why a lost
        character cannot count against the whale--hippo \omterm{def:b2:phylogenetic-trees:tree}{clade}.
  \item Fossil whales with hind limbs were found in 2001; their
        ankle bone has the double pulley. What does that do to the
        argument?
  \item If whales are inside the even-toed ungulates, is the group
        ``even-toed ungulates without whales'' monophyletic,
        paraphyletic or polyphyletic?
  \item A second gene gives the tree $(M,(P,(H,(W,C))))$. Name two
        reasons a gene tree may differ from the species tree, and
        say how to decide.
  \item The mitochondrial genome of whale and hippo differs at
        \qty{25}{\%} of sites. Correct it, and estimate the
        mitochondrial rate from the date found in question 14.
  \item Why is that rate so much higher than the nuclear gene's?
  \item State the result: the tree, the nuclear rate $k$, and the
        whale--hippo divergence time with its uncertainty.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
