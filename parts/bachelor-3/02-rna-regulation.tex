\chapter{Non-coding RNAs and Post-transcriptional Regulation}\label{ch:b3:rna-regulation}

In 1990 two plant biologists tried to make petunias a deeper purple by
giving them extra copies of the gene for the pigment enzyme. Nearly half
of the transgenic plants came out white, or purple with white sectors:
the added gene had switched off the plant's own copy as well as itself.
Eight years later, two worm geneticists injecting RNA into
\emph{Caenorhabditis elegans} found that a double-stranded RNA matching
a muscle gene silenced that gene, in the injected worm and in its
progeny, far more potently than either strand alone. The two puzzles
had one answer: cells possess a machinery that uses short RNAs to find
and silence RNAs of matching sequence. That machinery, and the wider
world of regulation that happens to a messenger RNA after it has been
transcribed --- how it is spliced, where it is sent, how long it lives,
whether it is translated --- is the subject of this chapter. The Year 1
volume treated the gene as a unit that is on or off at its promoter;
here the transcript itself becomes the object of control.

\section{The RNA census of a cell}

\begin{definition}[Non-coding RNA]\label{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}
\index{non-coding RNA}\index{microRNA}\index{siRNA}\index{piRNA}\index{long non-coding RNA}\index{small nuclear RNA}
A \emph{non-coding RNA} (ncRNA) is a transcript that functions as RNA
rather than as a template for protein. Beyond the ribosomal and
transfer RNAs of translation and the \emph{small nuclear RNAs} (snRNAs)
of the spliceosome, a eukaryotic cell contains: \emph{microRNAs}
(miRNAs), \numrange{21}{23} nucleotides, cut from hairpins in the cell's
own transcripts and guiding the repression of partly complementary
messengers; \emph{small interfering RNAs} (siRNAs), the same length,
cut from long double-stranded RNA and guiding the cleavage of perfectly
matching targets; \emph{Piwi-interacting RNAs} (\omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:pirna}{piRNAs}),
\numrange{26}{31} nucleotides, active in the germ line against
transposons; small nucleolar RNAs that guide the modification of rRNA;
and \emph{long non-coding RNAs} (lncRNAs), transcripts longer than
\qty{200}{nt} with no open reading frame of consequence, of which
\emph{\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{Xist}} of \cref{ch:b3:chromatin-epigenetics} is the best
understood. Protein-coding exons are about \qty{1.5}{\%} of the human
genome; a large fraction of the rest is transcribed at some time in
some cell, but how much of that transcription is functional is an open
question.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Abundance is not function]\label{rem:b3:rna-regulation:function}
A transcript can be detected because polymerase is leaky, not because
the RNA does something; most lncRNAs are present at fewer than one copy
per cell, poorly conserved, and dispensable when deleted. The test of
function is genetic: a phenotype when the RNA, and not merely its DNA,
is removed. \emph{\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{Xist}}, the miRNAs and the \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:pirna}{piRNAs} pass it decisively;
of the tens of thousands of annotated lncRNAs, a few hundred have so
far.
\end{remark}

\section{RNA interference and the microRNAs}

\begin{definition}[RNA interference]\label{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}
\index{RNA interference}\index{Dicer}\index{Argonaute}\index{RISC}
\emph{RNA interference} (RNAi) is the sequence-specific silencing of a
gene by a small RNA derived from double-stranded RNA. The enzyme
\emph{Dicer}, an RNase III, cuts long double-stranded RNA into
\numrange{21}{23}-nucleotide duplexes with two-nucleotide 3$'$
overhangs; one strand of each duplex, the guide, is loaded into an
\emph{Argonaute} protein to form the \emph{RNA-induced silencing
complex} (RISC). The guide pairs with a complementary target RNA, and
Argonaute's endonuclease domain cuts the target opposite nucleotides 10
and 11 of the guide; the cut RNA is then degraded and the RISC is
released to find another. In plants, worms and fungi an RNA-dependent
RNA polymerase copies the target into new double-stranded RNA, so the
signal is amplified and spreads; mammals lack this step.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Napoli, Lemieux and Jorgensen (1990) introduced an extra chalcone
synthase gene into petunia to intensify the flower colour; \qty{42}{\%}
of the transformants were white or variegated, with both the transgene
and the endogenous gene silenced --- ``cosuppression''. Guo and
Kemphues (1995) found that antisense RNA against a \emph{C.~elegans}
gene silenced it, but so did the sense control. Fire, Mello and
colleagues (1998) resolved the paradox: their preparations of single
strands were contaminated with double strands, and purified
double-stranded RNA of \emph{unc-22} injected into a worm gave the
twitching \emph{unc-22} phenotype at concentrations of a few molecules
per cell, whereas pure sense or antisense RNA gave almost nothing; the
silencing crossed into tissues far from the injection site and into
the progeny. Hamilton and Baulcombe (1999) detected the
\qty{25}{nt} RNAs in silenced plants, and Zamore and colleagues (2000)
showed in a fly extract that the double-stranded RNA is cut into them
and that the target is cleaved at \qty{21}{nt} intervals.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[MicroRNAs]\label{def:b3:rna-regulation:mirna}
\index{microRNA!biogenesis}\index{Drosha}\index{seed sequence}
A \emph{\omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA}} is encoded in the genome --- in its own gene or in an
intron of a protein-coding gene --- and transcribed by RNA polymerase II
as a long primary transcript (pri-miRNA) containing a hairpin of about
\qty{70}{nt}. In the nucleus the RNase III \emph{Drosha}, with its
partner DGCR8, cuts the hairpin out (the pre-miRNA); exportin-5 carries
it to the cytoplasm, where \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{Dicer} removes the loop, leaving a
\qty{22}{nt} duplex. One strand is loaded into \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{Argonaute}. The mature
miRNA recognises its targets chiefly through its \emph{seed}, nucleotides
2 to 8, pairing with sites usually in the 3$'$ untranslated region of a
messenger; the rest of the miRNA pairs imperfectly, so the target is
not cleaved but repressed: the \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{RISC} recruits proteins that shorten the
poly(A) tail, remove the cap and inhibit translation, and the messenger
is degraded faster. Because a seed is only seven nucleotides, one miRNA
has hundreds of targets, and more than half of human protein-coding
genes carry conserved sites for at least one of the several hundred
confidently identified human miRNAs.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
\emph{lin-4}, a gene needed for the worm to progress from its first
larval stage to the second, was found by Lee, Feinbaum and Ambros (1993)
to encode not a protein but a \qty{22}{nt} RNA, complementary to seven
sites in the 3$'$ untranslated region of the messenger of \emph{lin-14},
the gene it was known to repress. As the larva enters the second stage
the LIN-14 protein disappears while its mRNA remains: repression is
post-transcriptional. Reinhart and colleagues (2000) found a second such
RNA, \emph{let-7}, controlling the later larval transitions; Pasquinelli
(2000) found \emph{let-7} in flies and humans, with the same sequence,
which made a worm curiosity a general mechanism.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[What a microRNA does to a messenger's level and speed]\label{thm:b3:rna-regulation:mirna-kinetics}
\index{mRNA half-life}\index{response time}
A messenger is synthesised at a constant rate $k$ and degraded with
first-order rate $\gamma$; a \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA} present at level $R$ adds a decay
term $\gamma' R\, m$. Then the mRNA level $m(t)$ obeys
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = k - (\gamma + \gamma' R)\, m,
\qquad
m^{*} = \frac{k}{\gamma + \gamma' R},
\qquad
m(t) = m^{*} + (m_{0} - m^{*})\,e^{-(\gamma + \gamma' R)t}.
\]
The \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA} lowers the steady state by the factor $1 + \gamma' R/
\gamma$ and shortens the time constant of every change in $m$ from
$1/\gamma$ to $1/(\gamma + \gamma' R)$ by the same factor. A
microRNA-regulated gene is therefore both quieter and faster: it
reaches a new steady state sooner after its promoter changes, and
fluctuations in its transcription are damped.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The equation is linear with constant coefficients; its fixed point is
$m^{*}$ where the right-hand side vanishes, and writing $u = m - m^{*}$
gives $\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = -(\gamma + \gamma' R)\,u$, whence the
exponential. The half-time of the approach is $\ln 2/(\gamma + \gamma'
R)$, and $\gamma = \ln 2 / t_{1/2}$ where $t_{1/2}$ is the messenger's
natural half-life.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A four-fold repression]\label{ex:b3:rna-regulation:fourfold}
A messenger with a natural half-life of \qty{30}{min} ($\gamma =
\qty{0.023}{min^{-1}}$), transcribed at $k = 2$ molecules per minute,
sits at $m^{*} = 2/0.023 \approx 87$ copies per cell and takes
\qty{30}{min} to move half-way to a new level after its promoter
changes. A \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA} that triples its decay rate ($\gamma' R = 3\gamma$)
brings it to $22$ copies and cuts the half-time to \qty{7.5}{min}. The
same numbers, read backwards, show why miRNA knockouts often have mild
phenotypes: a four-fold change in a messenger that is itself buffered
downstream can leave the organism nearly normal, and the effect shows
under stress or in timing.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Knocking a gene down with RNAi]\label{met:b3:rna-regulation:knockdown}
\index{knockdown}\index{shRNA}
To silence a gene without touching its DNA: (1) choose a
\qty{21}{nt} sequence unique to its messenger, avoiding seed matches to
other genes; (2) deliver it as a synthetic \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNA} duplex (transient, a
few days) or as a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) expressed from a vector,
which \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{Dicer} processes continuously; in worms, feed the animals bacteria
expressing the double-stranded RNA; (3) measure the messenger by
quantitative PCR and the protein by immunoblot, expecting
\qtyrange{70}{95}{\%} loss rather than the complete absence of a
knockout; (4) control for off-target effects with a second,
non-overlapping \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNA} and a rescue by an RNAi-resistant version of the
gene. A knockdown is a partial, reversible loss of function; the gene
therapies now approved on this principle deliver \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNAs} against liver
messengers (transthyretin, PCSK9) coupled to a sugar that the
hepatocyte takes up.
\end{method}

\section{Small RNAs that guard the genome}

\begin{definition}[piRNAs and transposon silencing]\label{def:b3:rna-regulation:pirna}
\index{piRNA!ping-pong}\index{transposon silencing}\index{hybrid dysgenesis}
\emph{\omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{piRNAs}} are \numrange{26}{31}-nucleotide RNAs, made without \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{Dicer}
from long single-stranded transcripts of genomic \emph{\omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{piRNA} clusters}
--- graveyards of defective transposon copies --- and loaded into the
PIWI subfamily of \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{Argonaute} proteins in the germ line. A \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{piRNA}
antisense to a transposon guides cleavage of the transposon's
transcripts; the cleaved fragment becomes a new sense \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{piRNA}, which in
turn cuts cluster transcripts to make more antisense \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{piRNAs} --- the
\emph{ping-pong} cycle, an amplification that targets whatever
transposon is currently active. Nuclear PIWI proteins also direct
H3K9 methylation and \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{DNA methylation} onto the transposon's genomic
copies, connecting the RNA to the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} marks of
\cref{ch:b3:chromatin-epigenetics}. A mother deposits her \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{piRNAs} in the
egg; a female whose lineage has never met a transposon has no \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{piRNAs}
against it, and her offspring by a male carrying it are sterile ---
\emph{hybrid dysgenesis}, first seen in \emph{Drosophila} crosses
between laboratory and wild strains.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[RNA-directed chromatin silencing]\label{prop:b3:rna-regulation:rddm}
\index{RNA-directed DNA methylation}
In plants, a dedicated pathway --- RNA polymerase IV transcribes a
locus, an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase makes it double-stranded, a
\omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{Dicer} cuts \qty{24}{nt} \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNAs}, \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{Argonaute}~4 carries them back to the
nascent transcripts of polymerase V at the same locus and recruits the
methyltransferase DRM2 --- methylates the DNA of transposons and
repeated sequences; it is the mechanism of paramutation. In fission
yeast, \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNAs} from the centromeric repeats recruit the H3K9
methyltransferase and build the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{heterochromatin} the centromere needs.
In both cases an RNA finds a place in the genome by complementarity and
a \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} mark is written there: sequence-specific, self-reinforcing,
and heritable through divisions.
\end{proposition}

\section{The life of a messenger}

\begin{definition}[Regulated splicing]\label{def:b3:rna-regulation:splicing}
\index{alternative splicing!regulation}\index{SR protein}\index{hnRNP}\index{exon skipping}
Alternative splicing produces different messengers from one gene by
\emph{exon skipping}, the choice between mutually exclusive exons, the
retention of an intron, or the use of alternative 5$'$ or 3$'$ splice
sites. It is regulated by RNA-binding proteins that bind sequence
elements in the exons and introns near a splice site: \emph{SR
proteins} bound to exonic enhancers recruit the spliceosome and promote
inclusion of the exon, \emph{hnRNP} proteins bound to silencers block
it, and the outcome in a given cell is set by the relative amounts of
the two kinds. More than \qty{90}{\%} of human multi-exon genes are
alternatively spliced; the \emph{Dscam} gene of \emph{Drosophila}, with
four blocks of mutually exclusive exons (12, 48, 33 and 2
alternatives), can produce \num{38016} distinct proteins, each neuron
expressing a different set that lets its branches recognise and avoid
one another.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The sex of a fruit fly is decided by a splicing cascade. In females the
\emph{Sex-lethal} (\emph{Sxl}) gene makes a functional protein; SXL is an
RNA-binding protein that blocks a splice site in its own transcript
(so that a stop-codon exon is skipped and the protein keeps being made)
and in the transcript of \emph{transformer}, forcing the female
splice; TRA, with TRA-2, then binds an exonic enhancer in the
\emph{doublesex} transcript and directs the female form of the DSX
transcription factor. In males, with no SXL, every one of these
transcripts takes the default splice, \emph{transformer} contains a
stop codon, and DSX comes out in the male form. Every sexually
dimorphic feature of the fly follows from which DSX is made; a female
in which \emph{tra} is mutated develops as a male.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[mRNA stability and localisation]\label{def:b3:rna-regulation:stability}
\index{AU-rich element}\index{mRNA localisation}\index{iron-responsive element}
A messenger's \emph{half-life} ranges from minutes to days and is set by
elements in its 3$'$ untranslated region. \emph{AU-rich elements}
(AUUUA repeats) in the messengers of cytokines, growth factors and
proto-oncogenes are bound by proteins that recruit deadenylases and
give half-lives of \qtyrange{10}{30}{min}, so that a burst of
transcription produces a burst of protein and no more; mutations that
remove them cause overexpression and, for some proto-oncogenes, cancer.
\emph{Localisation elements}, often structured, are bound by adaptors
that link the messenger to a motor: \emph{bicoid} and \emph{oskar}
messengers are carried to the two poles of the fly egg by dynein and
kinesin on microtubules, $\beta$-actin messenger to the leading edge of
a fibroblast, and the messengers of synaptic proteins into dendrites,
where they are translated on demand. The \emph{iron-responsive element}
(IRE), a stem--loop bound by the iron-regulatory proteins IRP1 and
IRP2 when iron is scarce, does two opposite jobs from two positions: in
the 5$'$ untranslated region of the ferritin messenger, bound IRP
blocks the ribosome; in the 3$'$ untranslated region of the transferrin
receptor messenger, bound IRP protects the transcript from a nuclease.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Iron, read by one hairpin]\label{ex:b3:rna-regulation:iron}
When iron is low, IRP binds both messengers: ferritin, the storage
protein, is not translated, and the transferrin receptor, which imports
iron, is stabilised and abundant --- the cell stores less and imports
more. When iron is high it converts IRP1 into an aconitase (it acquires
an iron--sulfur cluster and loses its RNA affinity) and triggers the
degradation of IRP2: ferritin is translated, the receptor messenger
decays within minutes, and the cell stores more and imports less. No
change of transcription is needed. A point mutation in the ferritin
IRE that prevents IRP binding causes constitutive ferritin synthesis
and the hereditary hyperferritinaemia--cataract syndrome.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Translational control]\label{prop:b3:rna-regulation:translation}
\index{translational control}\index{eIF2}\index{riboswitch}\index{upstream open reading frame}
The initiation step is the usual point of control of translation.
Phosphorylation of the initiation factor \emph{eIF2} by kinases
sensing amino-acid starvation, unfolded proteins, viral double-stranded
RNA or haem deficiency shuts down general initiation within minutes,
sparing a few messengers with special features --- notably those with
\emph{\omterm{prop:b3:rna-regulation:translation}{upstream open reading frames}}, short reading frames in the 5$'$
untranslated region which normally trap ribosomes and which, under
stress, are bypassed so that the stress-response factors (GCN4 in
yeast, ATF4 in mammals) are made precisely when everything else is not.
The cap-binding factor eIF4E is held inactive by 4E-binding proteins
until the growth-signalling kinase mTOR phosphorylates them: a cell
translates at full rate only when nutrients and growth factors say so.
In bacteria, \emph{riboswitches} do it without any protein: a structured
5$'$ region binds a metabolite (thiamine pyrophosphate, S-adenosyl
methionine, a purine) and, on binding, refolds to bury the
ribosome-binding site or to form a transcription terminator, so that a
biosynthetic operon shuts off when its product is abundant.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[What post-transcriptional control buys]\label{rem:b3:rna-regulation:why}
Transcriptional control acts through the nucleus, with a delay of
minutes to hours before a new messenger is made, exported and
translated. Control of the messenger itself acts where the protein is
needed, in seconds to minutes, and can be local: a single dendrite
translating one messenger at one synapse, a fibroblast's leading edge
making actin where it crawls. It also makes one gene into many
proteins, and lets one signal --- iron, an amino acid, a \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA}
--- coordinate hundreds of messengers at once without touching a
single promoter.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:1}
Distinguish miRNA, \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNA} and \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{piRNA} by origin, length, \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{Dicer} dependence,
and what they do to their targets.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:2}
Trace the biogenesis of a \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA} from its gene to a repressed
messenger, naming the enzyme at each cut.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:3}
Why did injecting sense RNA of \emph{unc-22} into worms silence the
gene in the early experiments, and what did Fire and Mello change?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:4}
What happens to ferritin and to the transferrin receptor when a cell
is starved of iron? Which step of expression is controlled in each
case?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:5}
A messenger has a half-life of \qty{2}{h} and is made at $5$
molecules per minute. Compute its steady-state level, then the level
and half-time when a \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA} doubles its decay rate.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:6}
A seed of seven nucleotides matches a random sequence with probability
$4^{-7}$. In a set of \num{20000} messengers with 3$'$ untranslated
regions averaging \qty{1000}{nt}, how many sites does one \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA} seed
match by chance? What, then, distinguishes a real target?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:7}
Predict the sex of a fly (a) with a \emph{tra} loss-of-function
mutation and two X chromosomes; (b) expressing TRA from a constitutive
transgene, with one X; (c) lacking \emph{dsx} altogether.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:8}
A proto-oncogene messenger normally has a half-life of \qty{15}{min}
owing to an \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:stability}{AU-rich element}. A chromosomal translocation removes the
element and the half-life becomes \qty{3}{h}. By what factor does the
protein level rise, if translation and protein degradation are
unchanged? Why is this oncogenic although the protein is normal?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:9}
A laboratory strain of \emph{Drosophila} lacks P elements; a wild
strain carries them. Predict the fertility of the offspring of
(a) laboratory females $\times$ wild males, (b) wild females $\times$
laboratory males, and explain the asymmetry with \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:pirna}{piRNAs}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:10}
Using \cref{thm:b3:rna-regulation:mirna-kinetics}, argue that the
protein made from a messenger fluctuates less, in relative terms, when
the messenger is under \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA} control, at equal mean messenger level.
(The protein averages the messenger over its own lifetime, and the
messenger renews itself faster.) Why might a cell pay for a \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{microRNA}
and a higher transcription rate to get the same mean?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:11}
In worms, an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase makes secondary \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNAs} from
the target messenger; in mammals there is none. Predict, for each,
whether silencing by a single injection of double-stranded RNA lasts
through many cell divisions and whether it spreads to other tissues,
and explain the medical consequence for \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNA} drugs.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:rna-regulation:12}
A newly annotated lncRNA is expressed in the heart. Design the
experiments that would distinguish (a) a functional RNA, (b) a
functional act of transcription whose RNA is irrelevant, and (c) noise,
and say what result each predicts.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A Worm's Timer}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the \emph{lin-4}/\emph{lin-14}
switch of the worm timed with the messenger kinetics, an injected RNA
diluted through the divisions of an embryo and rescued by
amplification, the iron regulon quantified, and a splicing count,
ending on the repression fold, the half-time of the switch and the
number of divisions an unamplified signal survives}]
\label{pb:b3:rna-regulation:1}
Data: the \emph{lin-14} messenger is made at $k = 3$ molecules per
minute per cell and has a half-life of \qty{40}{min}. LIN-14 protein is
made at $\beta = 2$ per messenger per minute and has a half-life of
\qty{3}{h}. From the start of the second larval stage, \emph{lin-4}
RNA accumulates and adds a decay term $\gamma' R = 3\gamma$ to the
messenger and, by blocking initiation, halves $\beta$. A worm embryo
develops from one cell to about \num{550} cells at hatching; an adult
has \num{959} somatic cells.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The messenger.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute $\gamma$ and the steady-state \emph{lin-14} messenger
        level before \emph{lin-4} appears.
  \item Compute the protein degradation rate and the steady-state
        LIN-14 protein level.
  \item Once \emph{lin-4} is present, compute the new messenger level
        and the new time constant of the messenger.
  \item Compute the new protein steady state, and the overall
        repression fold on the protein.
  \item How long after \emph{lin-4} appears does the messenger reach
        half-way to its new level? And the protein --- which step
        limits the speed of the switch?
  \item The original experiments found the protein gone while the mRNA
        was still detectable. Is this consistent with your numbers?
        What fraction of the messenger remains?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Dilution and amplification.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A worm's gonad is injected with $10^{6}$ molecules of
        double-stranded RNA, which are shared equally among \num{250}
        eggs. How many molecules per egg?
  \item If the molecules were simply shared out at each division,
        how many would each cell of the \num{550}-cell hatchling hold?
        After how many further divisions would the average fall below
        one per cell?
  \item Fire and Mello saw silencing in the whole animal and in its
        progeny, at a few molecules per cell. Explain what an
        RNA-dependent RNA polymerase adds to the account, and why
        the effect nevertheless fades after a few generations.
  \item In a mammalian cell with no such polymerase, an \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNA}
        transfection puts \num{5000} duplexes into a cell that divides
        daily; the \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:rnai}{RISC} is stable but is diluted by division. After
        how many days does the count fall below \num{100}, roughly the
        number needed for effective silencing?
  \item A therapeutic \omterm{def:b3:rna-regulation:ncrna}{siRNA} against a liver messenger is given once
        and works for six months. Hepatocytes rarely divide. Explain
        why the mechanism of question 10 does not limit it, and what
        does.
  \item A seed of seven nucleotides matches at random once every
        $4^{7}$ nucleotides. The worm transcriptome has about
        \qty{20}{Mb} of 3$'$ untranslated sequence. How many chance
        seed matches does \emph{lin-4} have, and how did the
        geneticists know which target mattered?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The iron regulon.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item In iron-rich cells the transferrin receptor messenger has a
        half-life of \qty{10}{min}; with IRP bound, \qty{50}{min}.
        With transcription unchanged, by what factor does the messenger
        rise when iron becomes scarce?
  \item Ferritin messenger is unchanged in level but its translation
        falls from $1$ to $0.05$ proteins per messenger per minute when
        IRP binds. By what factor does ferritin synthesis fall?
  \item Combine the two: by what factor does the ratio of import
        capacity to storage capacity change between iron-rich and
        iron-poor states?
  \item The IRP1 protein is either an RNA-binding protein or an
        aconitase, depending on whether it holds an iron--sulfur
        cluster. Explain why this makes it a sensor of iron itself
        and not of a hormone.
  \item A patient carries a ferritin IRE mutation that abolishes IRP
        binding. Predict the ferritin level, the serum iron, and why
        the eye lens is affected.
  \item Explain in one sentence why the same hairpin can activate in
        one position and repress in another.
  \item IRP2 has no iron--sulfur cluster; when iron is high it is
        destroyed by a ubiquitin ligase whose own stability requires
        iron and oxygen. Why might a cell keep two sensors of such
        different chemistry?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Counting isoforms.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \emph{Dscam} has exon blocks with 12, 48, 33 and 2 mutually
        exclusive alternatives. How many isoforms? If each neuron
        expresses about $50$ at random, what is the probability that
        two given neurons share at least one? (Use $1 - (1 -
        50/N)^{50}$.)
  \item Why does a neuron benefit from having isoforms that its
        neighbours almost certainly lack?
  \item A gene with $n$ cassette exons, each independently included
        or skipped, gives how many messengers? How many for $n = 10$?
        Why is the real number in a given cell type usually far
        smaller?
  \item In the fly, why does a female with two X chromosomes but a
        null \emph{Sxl} allele die rather than simply develop as a
        male? (Consider what else the X-counting system controls:
        \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{dosage compensation} of X-linked genes.)
  \item SXL promotes the productive splicing of its own transcript.
        Explain why this makes the sex decision a memory that persists
        after the X-counting signal, present only in the early embryo,
        has gone, and relate it to the self-sustaining switches of
        \cref{ch:b3:chromatin-epigenetics}.
  \item Summarise the timer: the repression fold on LIN-14 protein
        (question~4), the half-time of the messenger switch
        (question~5) and the number of divisions an unamplified
        signal survives before falling below one molecule per cell
        (question~8).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
