\chapter{Functional Organization of a Mammal}\label{ch:b1:mammal-organization}

Open a rabbit on the dissecting board and the first impression is of
order. A muscular sheet, the \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:bodyplan}{diaphragm}, divides the trunk into a chest
holding the heart and two lungs and an abdomen holding everything
else: a liver the size of a fist, a stomach, four metres of intestine
ending in a caecum as large as the stomach, two kidneys against the
back wall. Each \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} has a place, a blood supply, and a job; none of
them works alone. This chapter describes how the mammal is built as a
functioning whole: its \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:bodyplan}{body plan}, the systems into which its \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} are
grouped, the fluid compartments its cells live in, the \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surfaces}
through which it meets the outside, and the thermal balance that keeps
it at \qty{39}{\celsius} in a January field.

\section{The body plan of a mammal}

\begin{definition}[Body plan]\label{def:b1:mammal-organization:bodyplan}
The \emph{body plan}\index{body plan} of an animal is the arrangement
of its axes, cavities and \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} that is shared by all members of its
group. The mammalian body plan is that of a vertebrate: bilateral
symmetry with a head end carrying the brain and the main sense \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs}
(\emph{cephalisation}\index{cephalisation}), a dorsal axis of vertebrae
enclosing the spinal cord, four limbs, and a ventral body cavity ---
the \emph{coelom}\index{coelom} --- containing the viscera. What is
specifically mammalian: the coelom is divided by the muscular
\emph{diaphragm}\index{diaphragm} into a thoracic cavity (heart, lungs)
and an abdominal cavity (digestive \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs}, kidneys, gonads); the body
is covered in hair; the young are fed on milk; the temperature is
regulated.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Organ, organ system]\label{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}
An \emph{organ}\index{organ} is a structure made of several tissues
(\cref{ch:b1:body-plans-tissues}) arranged to perform a definite
function: the heart pumps, the lung exchanges gases, the kidney filters.
An \emph{organ system}\index{organ system} is a set of organs
cooperating in one broad function: the digestive system runs from mouth
to anus with the liver and pancreas attached; the circulatory system is
the heart and the vessels; the excretory system, the kidneys and the
urinary tract.
\end{definition}

\section{Organ systems and the functions they serve}

\begin{proposition}[Three groups of functions]\label{prop:b1:mammal-organization:functions}
The \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ systems} of a mammal serve three groups of functions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{nutrition}\index{nutrition function}: taking in and
        distributing matter and energy and disposing of wastes ---
        the digestive, respiratory, circulatory and excretory systems;
  \item \emph{relation}\index{relation function}: sensing the
        environment and acting on it --- the sense \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs}, the nervous
        system, the endocrine glands, the skeleton and the muscles;
  \item \emph{reproduction}\index{reproduction function}: producing
        offspring --- the gonads, the genital tract and, in mammals,
        the mammary glands.
\end{itemize}
No system works alone: every \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} depends on the blood for supply,
on the nervous and endocrine systems for coordination, and on the
skeleton and skin for protection.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[A mouthful of grass]\label{ex:b1:mammal-organization:mouthful}
The rabbit's grass is cut by the incisors and ground by the molars
(skeleton and muscles), tasted and smelt (senses), swallowed and
digested in the stomach and small intestine; its sugars and amino acids
cross the intestinal wall into the blood and reach the liver, which
stores or releases them under hormonal control (endocrine); its
cellulose is fermented by bacteria in the caecum; the oxygen to burn the
sugars comes through the lungs, the carbon dioxide leaves the same way,
the urea from the amino acids leaves through the kidneys, and the heat
of the whole process leaves through the skin. Seven systems for one
mouthful.
\end{example}

\section{The compartments of the body}

\begin{definition}[Fluid compartments]\label{def:b1:mammal-organization:compartments}
Water makes about \qty{60}{\%} of a mammal's mass. Two thirds of it is
\emph{intracellular fluid}\index{intracellular fluid}, inside the cells;
one third is \emph{extracellular fluid}\index{extracellular fluid},
the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:milieu}{internal environment} of \cref{ch:b1:organism-environment}, itself
divided into the \emph{interstitial fluid}\index{interstitial fluid}
bathing the cells (three quarters of it) and the \emph{blood
plasma}\index{plasma} inside the vessels (one quarter). \emph{Lymph}\index{lymph}
is interstitial fluid collected by the lymphatic vessels and returned
to the blood. The two extracellular fluids have nearly the same
composition --- plasma differs by its proteins, which do not cross the
capillary wall --- and both differ sharply from the intracellular fluid.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Measuring a compartment by dilution]\label{met:b1:mammal-organization:dilution}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Choose a marker that distributes in the compartment and
        nowhere else: a dye bound to plasma proteins (Evans blue) for
        the plasma; inulin, a sugar that leaves the vessels but does
        not enter cells, for the \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:compartments}{extracellular fluid}; heavy water for
        the total body water.
  \item Inject a known amount $m$ and wait for mixing (minutes for
        plasma, hours for total water).
  \item Measure the concentration $c$ in a plasma sample, correcting
        for any loss (urine, metabolism) during the wait.
  \item The volume is $V = m/c$. Intracellular volume is total water
        minus extracellular; interstitial volume is extracellular minus
        plasma. Blood volume is plasma volume divided by the plasma
        fraction of blood ($1 - $ haematocrit).
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[A plasma volume]\label{ex:b1:mammal-organization:evansblue}
\qty{50}{mg} of Evans blue injected into a \qty{70}{kg} man give,
ten minutes later, a plasma concentration of \qty{16.7}{mg/L}:
$V = 50/16.7 = \qty{3.0}{L}$ of plasma. With a haematocrit of
\qty{45}{\%}, the blood volume is $3.0/0.55 = \qty{5.5}{L}$ ---
\qty{8}{\%} of the body mass, a proportion that holds from mouse to
whale.
\end{example}

\section{Exchange surfaces and the circulation}

\begin{proposition}[Every exchange surface is coupled to the blood]\label{prop:b1:mammal-organization:surfaces}
A mammal exchanges with the outside through four surfaces, each folded
inside the body and each perfused by a dense capillary network: the
intestinal mucosa (\qty{30}{m^2} in a human) for nutrients and water,
the alveolar surface of the lungs (\qty{100}{m^2}) for oxygen and
carbon dioxide, the filtering surface of the kidneys (a million
glomeruli, filtering \qty{180}{L} of plasma a day) for wastes and the
regulation of the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:milieu}{internal environment}, and the skin (\qty{2}{m^2}) for
heat and a little water. The circulation links them: blood leaving one
surface reaches the next within a minute, so that what enters through
the gut is oxidised with oxygen from the lung and its waste leaves
through the kidney.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The circulation time]\label{ex:b1:mammal-organization:circtime}
With \qty{5.5}{L} of blood and a cardiac output of \qty{5}{L/min}, a
drop of blood completes the double circuit in about one minute at rest,
and in a quarter of that during hard exercise, when the output reaches
\qty{20}{L/min}. Cardiac output scales as $M^{3/4}$
(\cref{thm:b1:organism-environment:kleiber}): a \qty{2}{kg} rabbit's is
about \qty{0.35}{L/min} for \qty{160}{mL} of blood --- the same
circulation time within a factor of two, at a heart rate of
\qty{200}{} beats per minute.
\end{example}

\section{Endothermy and the thermal balance}

\begin{proposition}[The heat budget]\label{prop:b1:mammal-organization:heatbudget}
\index{heat budget}
A mammal at steady temperature satisfies
\[
M - W = R + C + K + E ,
\]
where $M$ is the metabolic heat production, $W$ the mechanical work done
on the outside, and the right side the losses by radiation $R$,
convection to the air $C$, conduction to the ground $K$ and evaporation
$E$ (from the lungs, and from sweat or panting). The first three losses
are proportional to $T_b - T_a$ and are governed by the insulation of
the fur and the blood flow to the skin; evaporation is the only route
that works when the air is warmer than the body. When the equation is
not satisfied, the body temperature drifts at a rate set by the
imbalance and by the body's heat capacity (about
\qty{3.5}{kJ.kg^{-1}.K^{-1}}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Conservation of energy for the body over a period in which its
temperature does not change: the chemical energy released ($M$) leaves
as work or as heat through the four physical channels. The
proportionality of $R$, $C$, $K$ to the temperature difference is
Newton's law of cooling (\cref{ch:b1:organism-environment}); the
independence of $E$ from that difference follows from evaporation being
driven by the humidity gradient, not the temperature gradient.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The means of thermoregulation]\label{prop:b1:mammal-organization:thermoreg}
Against cold, a mammal reduces loss --- erecting its fur, constricting
the vessels of the skin and extremities, curling up, huddling --- and
raises production: shivering (rhythmic contraction of the muscles) and,
in small and young mammals, \emph{non-shivering
thermogenesis}\index{non-shivering thermogenesis} in \emph{brown adipose
tissue}\index{brown adipose tissue}, whose mitochondria release the
energy of fat directly as heat. Against warmth, it dilates the skin
vessels (a rabbit's ears can shed a third of its heat), and evaporates
water by sweating (horse, human) or panting (dog, rabbit). All of it is
commanded from the hypothalamus, which holds the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:homeostasis}{set point} and reads the
temperature of the blood and of the skin.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Heating the hypothalamus of a dog with an implanted probe makes it
pant and dilate its skin vessels in a cold room; cooling the probe makes
it shiver in a warm one. Oxygen consumption of a rat placed at
\qty{5}{\celsius} triples within an hour, half of the increase from
shivering (recorded as muscle electrical activity) and half from brown
fat, whose temperature, measured with a thermocouple, exceeds that of
the surrounding tissues. Infrared images of a rabbit at
\qty{30}{\celsius} show its ears at nearly body temperature, at
\qty{5}{\celsius} at nearly air temperature.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The rabbit's ears]\label{ex:b1:mammal-organization:ears}
Two ears of about \qty{50}{cm^2} each, thin, nearly bare and richly
vascularised: with their vessels open at \qty{30}{\celsius} in air at
\qty{20}{\celsius}, with a bare-skin coefficient of
\qty{10}{W.m^{-2}.K^{-1}}, they lose $10\times 0.01\times 10 =
\qty{1}{W}$, a sixth of a resting rabbit's production. At
\qty{5}{\celsius} the vessels close, the ears cool to near air
temperature, and the loss through them falls below \qty{0.2}{W}.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Coordination]\label{rem:b1:mammal-organization:coordination}
Every adjustment above --- a vessel narrowing, a muscle shivering, a
gland secreting --- is a command carried by a nerve or a hormone. The
nervous system acts in milliseconds along fixed wires; the endocrine
system acts in minutes to hours through the blood, reaching every cell
that carries the right receptor. Their mechanisms are the subject of
the Year 2 volume; this year takes them as given and studies what they
coordinate.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: A Rabbit's Day}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- twenty-four hours of a
two-kilogram rabbit in January: its energy, its water, its blood and its
heat, ending on its daily energy expenditure and water turnover}]
\label{pb:b1:mammal-organization:1}
A wild rabbit has a mass of \qty{2.0}{kg} and a body temperature of
\qty{39}{\celsius}. Its \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{basal metabolic rate} follows \omterm{thm:b1:organism-environment:kleiber}{Kleiber's law},
$B = \qty{3.4}{W}\,(M/\qty{1}{kg})^{3/4}$. Its day: \qty{12}{h} at rest
in its burrow within its \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:thermo}{thermoneutral zone}, \qty{6}{h} foraging at
three times its basal rate, and \qty{6}{h} at rest outside at
\qty{5}{\celsius}, where it must produce twice its basal rate. It eats
fresh grass containing \qty{25}{\%} dry matter; the dry matter holds
\qty{18}{kJ/g}, of which it digests \qty{65}{\%}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Energy.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the rabbit's \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{basal metabolic rate} in watts.
  \item Compute the energy spent in each of the three periods, in
        kilojoules.
  \item Compute the daily energy expenditure and express it as a
        multiple of the basal rate over \qty{24}{h}.
  \item Compute the digestible energy of one gram of fresh grass.
  \item Compute the mass of fresh grass eaten per day, and the mass of
        dry matter it contains.
  \item Express the fresh intake as a fraction of body mass.
  \item Taking \qty{20}{kJ} per litre of oxygen consumed, compute the
        daily oxygen consumption and the mean rate in millilitres per
        minute.
  \item With a respiratory quotient of $0.9$ (litres of $\mathrm{CO_2}$
        per litre of $\mathrm{O_2}$), compute the daily carbon dioxide
        output in litres and in grams (\qty{22.4}{L/mol},
        \qty{44}{g/mol}).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Water.}
Oxidising the digested dry matter yields \qty{0.6}{g} of metabolic
water per gram. The undigested dry matter leaves in faeces that are
\qty{40}{\%} water. Urine is \qty{130}{mL} per day. The rabbit breathes
\qty{860}{L} of air a day; it inhales air at \qty{10}{\celsius} holding
\qty{5}{g/m^3} of water vapour and exhales air saturated at
\qty{37}{\celsius}, \qty{44}{g/m^3}. Evaporation through the skin is
\qty{20}{g} a day.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the water taken in with the grass.
  \item Compute the metabolic water produced.
  \item Compute the mass of faeces and the water they carry away.
  \item Compute the water lost in the breath.
  \item Draw up the water balance: total inputs, total outputs, and
        the difference.
  \item Must the rabbit drink? What happens to the difference?
  \item In August the grass is \qty{50}{\%} dry matter. Recompute the
        water in the food for the same energy intake, and conclude.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Blood and compartments.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the rabbit's total body water, extracellular volume and
        plasma volume, using the fractions of the chapter.
  \item \qty{1.5}{mg} of Evans blue is injected; the plasma
        concentration after mixing is \qty{15}{mg/L}. Compute the
        plasma volume and compare with the estimate.
  \item With a haematocrit of \qty{40}{\%}, compute the blood volume.
  \item Cardiac output scales as $M^{3/4}$ from a human value of
        \qty{5}{L/min} at \qty{70}{kg}. Compute the rabbit's cardiac
        output and the time a drop of blood takes to complete the
        circuit.
  \item With a heart rate of \qty{200}{} beats per minute, compute the
        stroke volume.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Heat.}
The rabbit's surface is $S = \qty{0.1}{m^2}\,(M/\qty{1}{kg})^{2/3}$;
its fur gives $h = \qty{2.5}{W.m^{-2}.K^{-1}}$; each ear is
\qty{50}{cm^2} of nearly bare skin with $h = \qty{10}{W.m^{-2}.K^{-1}}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the rabbit's surface.
  \item Compute the heat lost through the fur at \qty{5}{\celsius} and
        compare it with the production assumed for the outdoor period.
  \item Compute the loss through the two ears if their vessels are open
        (skin at \qty{39}{\celsius}) and if they are closed (skin at
        \qty{8}{\celsius}). What does the rabbit do in January?
  \item In August, at \qty{30}{\celsius}, compute the loss through the
        fur and through the open ears, and compare their sum with the
        basal production. How does the rabbit stay at
        \qty{39}{\celsius}?
  \item State the result: the rabbit's daily energy expenditure in
        megajoules, as a multiple of its basal rate, and its daily
        water turnover in grams and as a fraction of its mass.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
