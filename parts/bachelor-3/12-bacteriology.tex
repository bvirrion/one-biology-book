\chapter{Bacteriology: Growth, Physiology and Genetics}\label{ch:b3:bacteriology}

A single \emph{Escherichia coli} cell in warm broth divides every
twenty minutes. Left unchecked, its descendants would outweigh the
Earth in two days; in fact they stop within hours, when the sugar runs
out, and sit for weeks in a state that survives starvation. A related
bacterium, given a wound and a few hours, becomes a hundred million
cells and a fever; a hundred years ago it killed a third of the
patients it infected, and since 1941 a mould's poison against it has
saved more lives than any other drug --- while the bacteria, over the
same eighty years, have evolved ways round every \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotic} made.
Bacteria were the organisms in which genes were first shown to be
DNA, in which gene regulation was first understood, and from which
the tools of \cref{ch:b3:genetic-engineering} were taken. This chapter
treats them as organisms: their envelope, the arithmetic of their
growth in a flask and in a continuous culture, the way a population
senses its own density, the traffic of genes between cells, and the
chemistry and evolution of \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics} and resistance.

\section{The bacterial cell}

\begin{definition}[The envelope]\label{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}
\index{peptidoglycan}\index{Gram-positive}\index{Gram-negative}\index{outer membrane}\index{lipopolysaccharide}\index{endospore}
Every bacterium but a few is enclosed in a wall of \emph{peptidoglycan}:
chains of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid,
cross-linked by short peptides into a single molecule that surrounds
the cell and resists the turgor pressure --- several atmospheres ---
of the cytoplasm. \emph{Gram-positive} bacteria have a thick wall of
\numrange{20}{80}{nm}, many layers, threaded with teichoic acids;
\emph{Gram-negative} bacteria have a thin wall of one or two layers
and, outside it, a second lipid bilayer, the \emph{outer membrane},
whose outer leaflet is \emph{lipopolysaccharide} (LPS, endotoxin ---
the molecule that the innate immune system of
\cref{ch:b3:innate-immunity} recognises and that causes septic shock),
enclosing a periplasm. Porins in the outer membrane admit small
molecules and exclude many \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics}. Outside may lie a polysaccharide
capsule against phagocytes, flagella for swimming
(\cref{ch:b3:cytoskeleton-motility}) and pili for attachment and
\omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:hgt}{conjugation}. Inside, the chromosome --- one circular molecule of
\qtyrange{1}{10}{Mb} in most species --- is folded into a nucleoid
without a membrane, alongside \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmids}. Some genera (\emph{Bacillus},
\emph{Clostridium}) can enclose a copy of the chromosome in a thick
coat as an \emph{endospore}, dehydrated and metabolically inert, which
survives boiling, radiation and centuries of drought and germinates
when nutrients return.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[The Gram stain]\label{met:b3:bacteriology:gram}
\index{Gram stain}
The oldest test in the bacteriological laboratory (Gram, 1884) still
decides the first choice of \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotic}. (1) Smear and heat-fix the
sample; (2) stain with crystal violet, then with iodine, which forms a
large insoluble complex with the dye inside the cells; (3) wash with
alcohol: the thick \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{peptidoglycan} of \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-positives}, dehydrated by the
alcohol, traps the complex and the cells stay purple, while the thin
wall of \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negatives} lets it out; (4) counter-stain with safranin,
which colours the now colourless \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negatives} pink. Shape and
arrangement --- cocci in clusters (staphylococci) or chains
(streptococci), rods, spirals --- narrow the identification further,
and a purple coccus in clusters from an abscess is a staphylococcus
before any culture has grown.
\end{method}

\section{Growth}

\begin{theorem}[Exponential growth, the Monod law and the chemostat]\label{thm:b3:bacteriology:monod}
\index{exponential growth!bacteria}\index{Monod equation}\index{chemostat}\index{dilution rate}
A population of $N$ cells dividing with generation time $T$ grows as
$N(t) = N_{0}\,2^{t/T} = N_{0}e^{\mu t}$ with specific growth rate $\mu =
\ln 2/T$. The rate depends on the limiting nutrient $S$ by the
\emph{Monod law}
\[
\mu(S) = \mu_{\max}\,\frac{S}{K_{S} + S},
\]
saturating at $\mu_{\max}$ and half-maximal at $S = K_{S}$. In a
\emph{chemostat} --- a vessel of volume $V$ into which fresh medium of
nutrient concentration $S_{0}$ flows at rate $F$, and out of which
culture leaves at the same rate, with \omterm{thm:b3:bacteriology:monod}{dilution rate} $D = F/V$ --- the
steady state has
\[
\mu(S^{*}) = D,\qquad S^{*} = \frac{K_{S}D}{\mu_{\max} - D},\qquad
X^{*} = Y\,(S_{0} - S^{*}),
\]
where $X$ is the cell density and $Y$ the yield (cells made per unit of
nutrient). The experimenter sets the growth rate by turning a pump;
the culture responds by adjusting the nutrient it leaves behind. If $D$
exceeds $\mu(S_{0})$ the cells cannot keep up and are washed out.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Cells: $\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = \mu(S)X - DX$, growth minus outflow;
at steady state with $X^{*} > 0$, $\mu(S^{*}) = D$, and inverting the
Monod law gives $S^{*}$. Nutrient: $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t = D(S_{0} -
S) - \mu(S)X/Y$, inflow minus outflow minus consumption; at steady
state, with $\mu = D$, $D(S_{0} - S^{*}) = DX^{*}/Y$, whence $X^{*}$.
The steady state exists only if $S^{*} < S_{0}$, that is $D <
\mu(S_{0})$; beyond that the only steady state is $X = 0$, washout.
Stability: if $X$ rises above $X^{*}$ it consumes more, $S$ falls below
$S^{*}$, $\mu$ falls below $D$ and $X$ declines --- a negative feedback
through the nutrient.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A culture in numbers]\label{ex:b3:bacteriology:numbers}
\emph{E.~coli} in rich medium at \qty{37}{\celsius}: $T = \qty{20}{min}$,
$\mu = \qty{2.1}{h^{-1}}$. From one cell, $2^{36}$ after twelve hours
--- $7\times 10^{10}$ cells, about \qty{70}{mg}, which is where a
\qty{100}{mL} flask stops for want of oxygen and sugar; the ``mass of
the Earth in two days'' is $2^{144}\approx 10^{43}$ cells, and the
arithmetic shows only that exponential growth never lasts. In a
chemostat on glucose with $\mu_{\max} = \qty{1.0}{h^{-1}}$, $K_{S} =
\qty{0.01}{g/L}$, $S_{0} = \qty{1}{g/L}$ and $Y = \qty{0.5}{g}$ of cells
per gram of glucose, a \omterm{thm:b3:bacteriology:monod}{dilution rate} of \qty{0.5}{h^{-1}} leaves $S^{*}
= 0.01\times 0.5/0.5 = \qty{0.01}{g/L}$ of glucose and holds $X^{*} =
\qty{0.495}{g/L}$ of cells, dividing every \qty{83}{min}, indefinitely.
The chemostat is how the physiology of a fixed growth rate is studied,
and how thousands of generations of evolution are run in a bottle.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Phases and states]\label{def:b3:bacteriology:phases}
\index{lag phase}\index{stationary phase}\index{sporulation}\index{sigma factor}
In a fresh flask (a \emph{batch culture}) the cells first pause --- the
\emph{lag phase}, while they induce the enzymes the new medium needs
--- then grow exponentially, then stop when a nutrient is exhausted or
a waste accumulates: the \emph{stationary phase}, in which the cells
shrink, thicken their envelope, condense their nucleoid around
protective proteins, switch on stress genes through the alternative
sigma factor RpoS, and can survive for months; a death phase follows
in which most cells lyse and a few live on the remains. Some species
answer starvation with a developmental programme: \emph{Bacillus
subtilis} divides asymmetrically and the larger cell engulfs the
smaller and builds the spore around it, a sequence of eight hours
controlled by a cascade of \emph{sigma factors} --- the subunits that
direct RNA polymerase to one set of promoters --- alternating between
the two compartments, the first bacterial example of differentiation
and of cell-to-cell signalling through a wall.
\end{definition}

\section{Sensing one another}

\begin{definition}[Quorum sensing]\label{def:b3:bacteriology:quorum}
\index{quorum sensing}\index{autoinducer}\index{two-component system}
Bacteria detect their own density by \emph{quorum sensing}: each cell
secretes a small signal molecule, an \emph{autoinducer} --- acylated
homoserine lactones in \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negatives}, short peptides in \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-positives}
--- at a constant rate; the concentration in the medium rises with the
number of cells, and when it crosses a threshold it binds a receptor
that switches on a set of genes, among them the synthase of the
autoinducer itself (hence the name). The genes so controlled are the
ones that pay only in company: light in \emph{Vibrio fischeri},
virulence factors in \emph{Staphylococcus aureus} and
\emph{Pseudomonas aeruginosa}, biofilm matrix, competence for DNA
uptake, and the killing of neighbours. Most bacterial sensing runs
through \emph{two-component systems}: a membrane histidine kinase that
detects a stimulus and phosphorylates a cytoplasmic response regulator,
which binds DNA --- a cell has dozens, for phosphate, osmolarity,
oxygen, and the autoinducers of other species.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Nealson, Platt and Hastings (1970) grew the luminous marine bacterium
\emph{Vibrio fischeri} from a dilute inoculum and found that the cells
made no light until they reached a density of some $10^{7}$ per
millilitre, then all lit up together; medium in which a dense culture
had grown, sterilised and added to a dilute culture, switched the light
on at once. The cells were counting a secreted substance, not time or
nutrient. The substance was identified as an acyl-homoserine lactone
(Eberhard, 1981), and the genes --- \emph{luxI} for the synthase,
\emph{luxR} for the receptor, and the luciferase operon they control ---
were cloned into \emph{E.~coli}, which then glowed when crowded. In
the squid that houses the bacterium at $10^{10}$ per millilitre, the
light organ shines; in the sea, where the bacteria are dilute, they are
dark and save the cost.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A density threshold]\label{prop:b3:bacteriology:threshold}
\index{quorum sensing!threshold}
Let each of $N$ cells in a volume $V$ secrete \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:quorum}{autoinducer} at rate $p$
molecules per second, and let the molecule be lost (degraded or
diluted) with first-order rate $k$. The concentration approaches
$A^{*} = pN/(kV)$, proportional to the cell density $N/V$, with a
time constant $1/k$; the receptor switches on when $A^{*}$ reaches its
threshold $A_{\text{thr}}$, that is at the density
\[
\left(\frac{N}{V}\right)_{\text{thr}} = \frac{k\,A_{\text{thr}}}{p}.
\]
In an enclosed space --- a squid's light organ, an abscess, the mucus
of a lung --- $k$ is small because the signal is not carried away, so
the threshold density is reached by fewer cells; in open water it is
never reached. The cell therefore measures not its numbers but its
numbers per unit of the volume it shares, which is what matters for
any cooperative act, and the positive feedback of the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:quorum}{autoinducer} on
its own synthase makes the switch sharp: once a few cells cross the
threshold, their extra output pushes the rest across.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\mathrm{d}A/\mathrm{d}t = pN/V - kA$ has the steady state $A^{*} =
pN/(kV)$ and relaxes to it as $e^{-kt}$. Setting $A^{*} = A_{\text{thr}}$
and solving for $N/V$ gives the threshold density.
\end{proof}

\section{The traffic of genes}

\begin{definition}[Horizontal gene transfer]\label{def:b3:bacteriology:hgt}
\index{transformation}\index{transduction}\index{conjugation}\index{plasmid!resistance}\index{mobile genetic element}\index{pangenome}
Bacteria reproduce clonally but exchange genes by three routes.
\emph{Transformation}: uptake of naked DNA from the environment by a
competent cell --- the route by which Griffith's pneumococci acquired
their capsule and Avery showed that the transforming principle was DNA
(1944). \emph{Transduction}: a phage that packages a piece of host DNA
and injects it into the next host (\cref{ch:b3:virology}).
\emph{Conjugation}: transfer of a \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid} through a pilus and a
mating bridge from a donor carrying it to a recipient, demonstrated by
Lederberg and Tatum (1946) with auxotrophic strains of \emph{E.~coli}
that produced prototrophic recombinants only when mixed. Conjugative
\omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmids} carry their own transfer genes; many also carry resistance
genes, often clustered in \emph{integrons} and flanked by transposons,
so that a single mating can transfer resistance to five \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics}
at once, across species. The consequence is that a bacterial species
has a core genome shared by all strains and an accessory genome that
varies --- a \emph{pangenome}: two \emph{E.~coli} strains may differ by
a fifth of their genes, and pathogenic strains differ from harmless
ones mainly by acquired islands of virulence genes. The genes of the
Year~2 volume's mutation chapter arise by mutation; the genes of this
one mostly arrive.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Robert Koch (1876--1884) established that a specific bacterium causes a
specific disease: he isolated \emph{Bacillus anthracis} from sick
animals, grew it in pure culture on solid medium (the agar plate and
the single colony are his laboratory's inventions), showed that the
culture reproduced anthrax in healthy animals and could be re-isolated
from them --- the postulates that still define a pathogen --- and went
on to the tubercle bacillus and the cholera vibrio. Alexander Fleming
(1928) noticed that a mould contaminating a plate of staphylococci had
cleared a zone around itself; the substance, penicillin, was purified
and shown to cure infections by Florey and Chain (1940--1941), and
within a decade resistant staphylococci carrying a penicillin-destroying
enzyme were common in hospitals --- a warning Fleming himself gave in
his Nobel lecture.
\end{proof}

\section{Antibiotics and resistance}

\begin{definition}[Antibiotics]\label{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}
\index{antibiotic}\index{beta-lactam}\index{selective toxicity}\index{minimum inhibitory concentration}
An \emph{antibiotic} is a molecule that kills bacteria or stops their
growth at concentrations harmless to the host, by acting on a target
the host lacks or has in a different form --- \emph{selective
toxicity}. The targets are few. The cell wall: \emph{$\beta$-lactams}
(penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems) mimic the terminal
D-Ala-D-Ala of the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{peptidoglycan} peptide and acylate the enzymes that
cross-link it, so that the growing wall is weak and the cell bursts
under its own turgor; vancomycin binds the D-Ala-D-Ala itself. The
ribosome, whose bacterial form differs from the eukaryotic: aminoglycosides
(the small subunit, causing misreading), tetracyclines (blocking tRNA
entry), macrolides and chloramphenicol (the large subunit's exit
tunnel and peptidyl transferase). DNA gyrase and topoisomerase~IV:
the quinolones. RNA polymerase: rifampicin. Folate synthesis, which
animals do not do: sulfonamides and trimethoprim. The membrane: the
polymyxins, a last resort. The \emph{minimum inhibitory concentration}
(MIC) is the lowest concentration of a drug that prevents visible
growth of a strain; a strain is called resistant when its MIC exceeds
the concentration the drug reaches in the patient.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Measuring susceptibility]\label{met:b3:bacteriology:mic}
\index{disk diffusion}\index{broth dilution}
Two tests. \emph{\omterm{met:b3:bacteriology:mic}{Broth dilution}}: inoculate a fixed number of cells
(about $5\times 10^{5}$ per millilitre) into a row of tubes or wells
containing the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotic} in twofold steps, incubate overnight, and
read the first clear well: that concentration is the MIC. \emph{\omterm{met:b3:bacteriology:mic}{Disk
diffusion}}: spread the strain on an agar plate, place paper discs
loaded with known amounts of several \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics}, incubate; the drug
diffuses outward, its concentration falling with distance, and growth
is inhibited out to the radius where the concentration equals the MIC,
so that the diameter of the clear zone --- read against calibrated
tables --- gives the susceptibility for each disc on one plate. Both
tests take a day; sequencing the genome for known resistance genes and
mutations takes the same day and is beginning to replace them.
\end{method}

\begin{definition}[Resistance]\label{def:b3:bacteriology:resistance}
\index{antibiotic resistance}\index{beta-lactamase}\index{efflux pump}\index{MRSA}
A bacterium resists an \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotic} by one of five means. It destroys the
drug: \emph{$\beta$-lactamases} hydrolyse the $\beta$-lactam ring, and
the extended-spectrum and carbapenemase variants now destroy the
newest ones. It alters the target: a mutation in the ribosomal protein
or gyrase that the drug binds, or, in MRSA (methicillin-resistant
\emph{Staphylococcus aureus}), an acquired gene for a cross-linking
enzyme that $\beta$-lactams do not bind; vancomycin resistance replaces
the terminal D-Ala with D-lactate, which the drug does not recognise.
It pumps the drug out with \emph{efflux pumps}, often broad in
specificity. It stops the drug getting in, by losing a porin. Or it
bypasses the blocked step with an alternative enzyme. Target mutations
arise by chance in the patient at rates of $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-6}$ per
division and are selected during treatment; the enzymes and pumps are
mostly acquired on \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmids} from the vast reservoir of soil and gut
bacteria, in which they evolved over ages against the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics} that
fungi and other bacteria make. Persisters --- dormant cells that
survive a drug without being genetically resistant --- explain relapses
after treatment that should have worked.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Why resistance is inevitable and combination is the answer]\label{prop:b3:bacteriology:selection}
\index{resistance!selection}\index{combination therapy!antibiotics}
An infection of $10^{9}$ cells treated with a drug against which
resistance mutations arise at $10^{-8}$ per division already contains
about ten resistant cells; the drug kills the rest and the ten take
over --- the arithmetic of \cref{ch:b3:cancer-biology} exactly, since a
bacterial population and a \omterm{def:b3:cancer-biology:hallmarks}{tumour} are both evolving clones under
selection. Two drugs with independent targets face a doubly resistant
cell at $10^{-16}$ per division, which a billion cells do not contain.
This is why tuberculosis, whose lesions hold $10^{8}$--$10^{9}$ bacilli,
has been treated with three or four drugs at once since the 1950s and
why monotherapy of it produced resistance within months; it is also
why every use of an \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotic}, on a patient or in a feedlot, selects
resistance somewhere, and why the rate of new \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics} --- none of a
new class against \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negatives} since the 1960s --- is the measure
against which the losses are counted. The remedies are those of any
evolutionary contest: use less, combine, finish the course so that
partially resistant cells do not survive to breed, and find new
targets.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The mutant selection window]\label{ex:b3:bacteriology:window}
A strain has MIC \qty{1}{mg/L}; its first-step resistant mutants, present
at one in $10^{7}$, have MIC \qty{8}{mg/L}. A dose that holds the drug
at \qty{4}{mg/L} kills the wild type and leaves the mutants to grow
without competition: the concentration range between the MIC of the
wild type and that of the mutants is the \emph{mutant selection
window}, and a treatment that sits in it breeds resistance. A dose
above \qty{8}{mg/L} kills both; too low a dose, or a course stopped when
the patient feels better and the drug level is falling through the
window, is how a resistant population is made. The same logic sets the
regimens of tuberculosis treatment: six months, four drugs, at doses
above every single-step mutant's MIC.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Most bacteria are not pathogens]\label{rem:b3:bacteriology:most}
The bacteria of this chapter's clinic are a tiny minority. Of the
estimated million species, a few hundred cause human disease; the rest
run the cycles of nitrogen, sulfur and carbon (the Year~2 volume), fix
the nitrogen of every legume, digest the cellulose in every ruminant,
and make up the microbiomes of \cref{ch:b3:microbiomes}, whose loss
under \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotic} treatment is a cost of every course. The \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics}
themselves are their molecules --- weapons and signals evolved among
soil bacteria and fungi long before there were hospitals --- and so
are most of the resistance genes.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:1}
Describe the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-positive} and \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negative} envelopes and explain why
the \omterm{met:b3:bacteriology:gram}{Gram stain} distinguishes them.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:2}
A culture of $10^{3}$ cells per millilitre reaches $10^{9}$ in
\qty{8}{h} of exponential growth. Find the number of generations, the
generation time and $\mu$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:3}
Name the three routes of horizontal gene transfer and give the classic
experiment that demonstrated each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:4}
Give the target of penicillin, of streptomycin, of ciprofloxacin and of
rifampicin, and say why each is selectively toxic.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:5}
A chemostat has $\mu_{\max} = \qty{0.8}{h^{-1}}$, $K_{S} =
\qty{0.02}{g/L}$, $S_{0} = \qty{2}{g/L}$, $Y = 0.4$. Compute $S^{*}$ and
$X^{*}$ at $D = 0.2$, $0.6$ and $0.79$ h$^{-1}$, and the \omterm{thm:b3:bacteriology:monod}{dilution rate}
at which washout begins.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:6}
Using \cref{prop:b3:bacteriology:threshold}, compare the threshold
density for \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:quorum}{quorum sensing} in a squid light organ ($k =
\qty{0.01}{h^{-1}}$) and in flowing sea water ($k = \qty{10}{h^{-1}}$),
with $p = 100$ molecules per cell per hour and $A_{\text{thr}} =
\qty{10}{nmol/L}$ ($6\times 10^{12}$ molecules per litre).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:7}
A disc-diffusion plate shows zones of $25$, $12$ and \qty{0}{mm} for
three drugs. Interpret each, and explain why a zone diameter can be
converted into an MIC.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:8}
Explain how a single \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:hgt}{conjugation} event can make a sensitive
\emph{Klebsiella} resistant to five \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotics}, and why resistance
genes are so often found together on \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmids}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:9}
A patient has $10^{9}$ bacteria and takes a drug against which
resistance arises at $10^{-7}$ per division. Compute the expected
resistant cells at the start; then with two independent drugs. Why are
persisters a different problem?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:10}
Show that the chemostat steady state is stable: perturb $X$ slightly
above $X^{*}$ and follow the signs of $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t$ and
$\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t$. Then explain why two species competing for
one nutrient in a chemostat cannot coexist at steady state, and which
one wins at a given $D$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:11}
The \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:quorum}{autoinducer} induces its own synthase. Modify the model of
\cref{prop:b3:bacteriology:threshold} so that $p$ jumps from $p_{0}$ to
$p_{1} \gg p_{0}$ when $A > A_{\text{thr}}$, and show that the system
can have two stable states over a range of densities (hysteresis):
a population that has switched on stays on at a density where a naive
population would be off. What is the biological use of that?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:bacteriology:12}
Explain the mutant selection window and use it to argue for or against
each of: (a) high doses for short courses; (b) low doses for long
courses; (c) stopping when symptoms resolve; (d) \omterm{thm:b3:cancer-biology:goldie-coldman}{combination therapy}.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A Chemostat and a Clinic}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a culture grown in a flask and held
in a chemostat, a luminous bacterium counted to its quorum, and an
infection treated against the arithmetic of resistance, ending on the
cells in the chemostat, the density at which the light comes on and the
odds that a two-drug course meets a resistant cell}]
\label{pb:b3:bacteriology:1}
Data: \emph{E.~coli} at \qty{37}{\celsius}, $T = \qty{20}{min}$; a cell
weighs \qty{1}{pg}. Chemostat: $V = \qty{1}{L}$, $F = \qty{0.5}{L/h}$,
$\mu_{\max} = \qty{1.0}{h^{-1}}$, $K_{S} = \qty{0.01}{g/L}$, $S_{0} =
\qty{2}{g/L}$ glucose, $Y = 0.5$. \emph{Vibrio}: $p = 500$ \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:quorum}{autoinducer}
molecules per cell per hour, $A_{\text{thr}} = 6\times 10^{12}$
molecules per litre (\qty{10}{nmol/L}), $k = \qty{0.05}{h^{-1}}$ in the
light organ. Infection: $10^{9}$ cells; resistance to drug A at
$10^{-8}$ per division, to drug B at $10^{-7}$; wild-type MIC of A
\qty{1}{mg/L}, first-step mutant \qty{8}{mg/L}; a dose gives
\qty{16}{mg/L} falling with half-life \qty{4}{h}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The flask.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item One cell in \qty{100}{mL} of broth. How many cells after
        \qty{6}{h} of exponential growth, and what density per mL?
  \item The broth supports at most $2\times 10^{9}$ cells per mL. When
        does growth stop, and what is the culture's mass of cells?
  \item How long would it take the descendants of one cell to reach
        the mass of the Earth ($6\times 10^{27}$ g)? Comment.
  \item The \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:phases}{lag phase} lasts \qty{1}{h} when cells from \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:phases}{stationary phase}
        are inoculated into fresh medium, and almost nothing when
        exponential cells are used. Explain.
  \item Compute $\mu$ from $T$. If the medium is switched to a sugar on
        which $T = \qty{60}{min}$, what is $\mu$, and by what factor
        does the number of cells after \qty{6}{h} fall?
  \item A sample of the stationary culture is spread on a plate and
        gives $200$ colonies from \qty{0.1}{mL} of a $10^{-6}$ dilution.
        What is the viable count, and why might it differ from a
        microscope count?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The chemostat.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the \omterm{thm:b3:bacteriology:monod}{dilution rate} $D$ and the generation time the
        culture is forced to adopt.
  \item Compute $S^{*}$ and $X^{*}$ (in g/L and cells per mL).
  \item How many cells leave the vessel per hour? How many generations
        does the culture go through in a month?
  \item The pump is turned up to $F = \qty{0.95}{L/h}$. New $S^{*}$ and
        $X^{*}$? What happens at $F = \qty{1.1}{L/h}$?
  \item A mutant appears with $\mu_{\max} = \qty{1.2}{h^{-1}}$ and the
        same $K_{S}$. At $D = 0.5$, what $S^{*}$ does it need, and what
        happens to the original strain? (Use
        \cref{exo:b3:bacteriology:10}.)
  \item A month's run is $700$ generations. If a beneficial mutation
        arises at $10^{-9}$ per division and the vessel holds $10^{12}$
        cells, how many beneficial mutations arise per generation?
        What does this say about evolution in a chemostat?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The quorum.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the threshold cell density for light in the light
        organ, in cells per mL.
  \item The light organ holds \qty{1}{\micro L} of bacteria at $10^{10}$
        per mL. By what factor is the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:quorum}{autoinducer} above threshold?
  \item In sea water the signal is swept away with $k = \qty{20}{h^{-1}}$.
        Threshold density? Compare with the $10^{2}$ cells per mL of
        the open sea.
  \item After the squid expels \qty{90}{\%} of the bacteria at dawn,
        how long until the remaining $10^{9}$ per mL, dividing every
        \qty{2}{h}, restore $10^{10}$ per mL? Does the light go out
        meanwhile?
  \item Each glowing cell spends about \qty{20}{\%} of its energy on
        light. Why is a dark cell in the sea favoured, and why not in
        the organ?
  \item A pathogen uses the same system to delay its toxins until it
        is numerous. Propose a drug strategy that exploits this and say
        why it might select resistance more slowly than an \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:antibiotics}{antibiotic}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The clinic.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Expected number of cells resistant to A at the start of
        treatment; to B; to both.
  \item Probability that no cell resistant to A exists; to both.
  \item After the dose, when does the drug level fall to \qty{8}{mg/L},
        and to \qty{1}{mg/L}? How long per dose does it spend in the
        mutant selection window?
  \item If the drug is given every \qty{12}{h}, what fraction of the
        time is the level in the window, and what does that predict
        over a ten-day course with A alone?
  \item Suggest a dosing change that keeps the level above
        \qty{8}{mg/L} throughout (compute the interval or the dose
        needed), and state its cost.
  \item One cell in $10^{4}$ is a persister, dormant and untouched by
        any concentration of either drug. How many survive the course,
        what happens when the drug is withdrawn, and what does this
        imply for the length of treatment?
  \item Summarise: the cells per mL in the chemostat (question~8), the
        light threshold density (question~13), and the probability that
        the two-drug course meets no resistant cell (question~20).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
