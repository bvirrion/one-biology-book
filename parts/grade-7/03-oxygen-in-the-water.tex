\chapter{Oxygen in the Water}\label{ch:g7:oxygen-in-the-water}

The mountain stream and the summer pond are both ``water''
--- yet the trout that darts in one would gasp in the other,
and the carp that thrives in the pond would find the stream
bare of its food. The difference between the two waters is
largely one invisible number: how much \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen} is \omterm{def:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:dissolved}{dissolved} in
them. This chapter learns to read, measure and predict that
number --- and with it, the map of water life.

\section{Dissolved oxygen, a scarce stock}

\begin{definition}[Dissolved oxygen]\label{def:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:dissolved}
Water holds \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen} the way sweet tea holds sugar ---
invisibly, \emph{dissolved}\index{dissolved oxygen}. The
quantity is tiny: a litre of well-aerated water carries
about \qty{10}{mg} of \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen}, some thirty times less than a
litre of air. Every \omterm{def:g7:how-animals-breathe:four}{gill} and every underwater respirer of
\cref{ch:g7:how-animals-breathe} draws on this one thin
stock (\cref{prop:g7:what-is-respiration:habitats}).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[What fills the stock]\label{prop:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:fills}
Two suppliers replenish a water's \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the \emph{air above}: \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen} dissolves in at the
        surface --- faster where the water is stirred,
        splashed and foamed (rapids, waterfalls, waves,
        rain);
  \item the \emph{\omterm{def:g5:food-webs:roles}{producers} below}: by day, water \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:plant}{plants}
        and green single \omterm{def:g5:first-look-at-cells:cell}{cells} release \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen} into the
        water as they manufacture
        (\cref{rem:g6:plants-are-producers:gas}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{proposition}[What drains it, and what it can hold]\label{prop:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:drains}
The stock is drained by \emph{every respirer} --- \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animals},
\omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:plant}{plants} at night, and above all the \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposers} when there
is much dead matter to eat
(\cref{pb:g7:what-is-respiration:1}'s eighth jar). And the
ceiling moves: \emph{cold} water can hold notably more
\omterm{def:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:dissolved}{dissolved oxygen} than warm --- a stream at
\qty{5}{\degreeCelsius} carries half as much again as a
pond at \qty{25}{\degreeCelsius}. Warmth is a double
squeeze: less \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen} held, while every engine note
(\cref{ex:g7:what-is-respiration:demand}) runs faster.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Stream against pond]\label{ex:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:streampond}
Now the two waters read like ledgers. The mountain stream:
cold (high ceiling), churned white over every boulder
(fast refilling) --- \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen} near the ceiling all year. The
summer pond: warm (low ceiling), still (slow refilling),
rich in mud and dead matter (hungry \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposers}) ---
\omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen} scraping along the floor, worst in the small hours
before dawn, when the \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:plant}{plants} have been respiring all night
with no daylight manufacture to offset them.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Reading the pond's curve]\label{ex:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:curve}
The pond's daily wave is
\cref{rem:g6:plants-are-producers:gas} drawn as data: sunrise
starts the \omterm{def:g5:food-webs:roles}{producers}' manufacture and the curve climbs;
late afternoon peaks it; night \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaves} every respirer
drawing on the stock with no supplier but the still surface
--- the curve slides to its dawn minimum. \omterm{prop:g3:sorting-living-things:groups}{Fish} kills in
overgrown ponds happen at dawn, and now you know why.
\end{example}

\section{The map of inhabitants}

\begin{proposition}[Oxygen draws the map]\label{prop:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:map}
Because \omterm{def:g5:biodiversity:species}{species} differ in their \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen} needs, the \omterm{def:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:dissolved}{dissolved}
number maps the inhabitants
(\cref{prop:g6:exploring-our-environment:decide}, under
water):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{demanding} \omterm{def:g5:biodiversity:species}{species} --- trout, mayfly larvae
        with their waving \omterm{def:g7:how-animals-breathe:four}{gill} tufts
        (\cref{pb:g7:how-animals-breathe:1}) --- live only
        in cold, churned, oxygen-rich water;
  \item \emph{tolerant} \omterm{def:g5:biodiversity:species}{species} --- carp, many pond snails,
        rat-tailed larvae with snorkels to the surface ---
        manage in warm, still, poorer water;
  \item so the census reads backward: find mayfly larvae
        under the stones and the water is good; find only
        snorkelers and air-gulpers, and it is poor.
\end{itemize}
River wardens census the small life for exactly this
reason: the inhabitants are a record of the water's worst
recent days, which a single probe reading can miss.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[The organic-waste story]\label{ex:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:waste}
A village's untreated waste --- rich \omterm{def:g6:plants-are-producers:matter}{organic matter} ---
reaches a clear stream. Downstream of the pipe, the
\omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposers} feast and multiply
(\cref{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}), and their
massed \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{respiration} drains the \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen}: the trout and mayfly
larvae vanish, the tolerant snorkelers take over, and
further downstream still --- the waste consumed, the water
re-aerated over riffles --- the demanding \omterm{def:g5:biodiversity:species}{species} return.
The pollution was \omterm{def:g6:plants-are-producers:matter}{organic matter}; the killer was \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen}
hunger; the map recorded it all.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Judging a water]\label{met:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:judge}
To judge a stream or pond:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item measure or estimate the suppliers: \omterm{def:g6:exploring-our-environment:conditions}{temperature},
        stirring, green growth;
  \item weigh the drains: mud and dead matter, crowding,
        any organic waste inflow;
  \item census the small life under stones and among weeds
        --- demanding \omterm{def:g5:biodiversity:species}{species} present, or tolerant only?
  \item if sampling at one hour, remember the pond curve:
        a fine afternoon reading can hide a lethal dawn.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Problem: The Warden's Yearbook}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- one river, four
stations, a year of readings}]\label{pb:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:1}
A river warden keeps a yearbook for four stations: K1, a
cold rapid below a weir; K2, a slow warm reach through
pastures; K3, just below a creamery's waste pipe; K4, two
kilometres further down, past a long stony riffle. Each
station gets probe readings and a small-life census.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Reading the stations.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item K1 reads \qty{11}{mg/L} at
        \qty{8}{\degreeCelsius}; K2 reads \qty{7}{mg/L}
        at \qty{22}{\degreeCelsius}. \omterm{def:g6:classification-kinship:attribute}{Attribute} the
        difference to the right suppliers and ceilings.
  \item K1's census: trout fry, mayfly larvae. K2's: carp,
        pond snails, rat-tailed larvae. Interpret both
        with \cref{prop:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:map}.
  \item K3 reads \qty{3}{mg/L} though the water is no
        warmer than K2. Name the drain, and the workers
        behind it.
  \item K4 reads \qty{9}{mg/L} and its census again shows
        mayfly larvae. Explain the recovery with both
        suppliers and the waste's fate.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The year's rhythms.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item K2's dawn readings in August dip to
        \qty{4}{mg/L} while its afternoon readings reach
        \qty{9}{mg/L}. Draw or describe its daily curve,
        and name the two processes trading places.
  \item January's readings at K2 are steadier and higher
        than August's, though the pasture reach is as
        still as ever. Give the two winter reasons.
  \item A hot, windless fortnight is forecast. Rank the
        four stations by fish-kill risk, with one reason
        each.
  \item The warden times her August probe visits for just
        before sunrise. Defend the schedule.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Verdicts and
remedies.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The creamery installs treatment that removes most
        \omterm{def:g6:plants-are-producers:matter}{organic matter} from its outflow. Predict K3's
        probe and census a year later, in order of
        recovery.
  \item A landowner proposes felling K2's bankside trees
        ``to let more \omterm{def:g6:exploring-our-environment:conditions}{light} make more \omterm{def:g7:what-is-respiration:respiration}{oxygen}''. Weigh the
        proposal: what does the shade also do, by
        \cref{prop:g7:oxygen-in-the-water:drains}, and
        what would you answer?
  \item The census finds mayfly larvae at a station whose
        single afternoon probe reading looks mediocre.
        Which witness do you believe about the season as
        a whole, and why?
  \item Close the yearbook with the warden's one-sentence
        rule for the whole river, using \emph{cold},
        \emph{stirred} and \emph{unloaded} (of organic
        waste).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
