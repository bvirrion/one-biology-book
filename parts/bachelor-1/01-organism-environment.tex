\chapter{The Organism: A System in Interaction with Its Environment}\label{ch:b1:organism-environment}

A rabbit sits at the edge of a meadow on a January morning. In the next
hour it will eat a hundred grams of grass, breathe some \qty{400}{L} of
air, lose heat through its fur to air at \qty{2}{\celsius}, drink from a
puddle, and bolt when a buzzard's shadow crosses the field. Nothing it
does is possible without something crossing its boundary: matter in and
out, energy in and out, information in. Two hundred metres away an oak
does the same things without moving --- takes carbon dioxide and light,
draws water through a root system larger than its crown, and loses that
water to the same cold air. This chapter states what an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} is as a
system, why its size dictates how it is built, how it holds its inside
steady while everything outside changes, and how its energy needs scale
with its mass. The two \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} of this opening, a mammal and a
flowering plant, are the models the whole year returns to.

\section{An open system}

\begin{definition}[Organism, open system]\label{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}
An \emph{organism}\index{organism} is a living individual: a bounded,
self-maintaining, self-reproducing system, made of one cell or of many
cells that share a common origin and a common functioning. It is an
\emph{open system}\index{open system}: matter and energy cross its
boundary continuously, and it stays alive only as long as they do. The
\emph{environment}\index{environment} of an organism is everything
outside that boundary that exchanges with it --- the physical medium
(air, water, soil, light, temperature) and the other organisms.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The three exchanges]\label{prop:b1:organism-environment:exchanges}
Every \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} exchanges three things with its environment:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{matter}\index{matter exchange}: it takes in nutrients,
        water and gases and releases wastes, gases and water;
  \item \emph{energy}\index{energy exchange}: it takes in energy as
        light (autotrophs) or as the chemical energy of food
        (heterotrophs), and releases it almost entirely as heat;
  \item \emph{information}\index{information exchange}: it detects
        signals from the environment (light, chemicals, temperature,
        touch, sound) and responds to them.
\end{itemize}
Over a period in which the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} neither grows nor shrinks, its
balance sheet is closed: matter in equals matter out, and energy in
equals heat out plus the work done on the outside.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first two follow from conservation of mass and energy applied to the
volume inside the boundary: whatever accumulates is the difference
between inflow and outflow, and a steady \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} accumulates nothing.
Chemical energy that is not stored as new biomass ends as heat, because
every reaction of the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} dissipates its free energy as heat, and
the mechanical work done on the outside (moving, lifting) is the only
other exit. The third is the observation that no \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} is inert to
its surroundings.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Autotroph, heterotroph]\label{def:b1:organism-environment:trophy}
An \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} is \emph{autotrophic}\index{autotrophy} when it builds its
own organic matter from mineral carbon (carbon dioxide) using an external
energy source --- light for the \emph{photoautotrophs}\index{photoautotroph}
(plants, algae, cyanobacteria), the oxidation of mineral compounds for the
\emph{chemoautotrophs}\index{chemoautotroph}. It is
\emph{heterotrophic}\index{heterotrophy} when it must take in organic
matter made by other \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms}, which supplies both its carbon and its
energy (animals, fungi, most bacteria).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The rabbit and the oak]\label{ex:b1:organism-environment:rabbitoak}
The rabbit's hundred grams of grass supply, once digested, about
\qty{500}{kJ} of chemical energy and the carbon of every molecule it
will build; the oxygen it breathes lets it oxidise that food, and the
carbon dioxide it exhales is the same carbon leaving. The oak's intake is
the reverse: carbon dioxide in, oxygen out, the energy coming as light
--- some \qty{5}{kW} falling on its crown at midday, of which it stores
under one percent as sugar. Both release almost all the energy they
process as heat: the rabbit's fur is warm, and the oak's leaves are
cooled by the evaporation that its own light absorption drives.
\end{example}

\section{Scales of organisation and the problem of size}

\begin{definition}[Levels of organisation]\label{def:b1:organism-environment:levels}
Living matter is organised in nested \emph{levels of
organisation}\index{levels of organisation}, each built from the units
of the one below and each with properties the lower level does not have:
molecules ($\qty{1}{nm}$), cells ($\qtyrange{1}{100}{\micro m}$),
tissues, organs ($\qtyrange{1}{100}{mm}$), organ systems, the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism}
($\qty{1}{\micro m}$ to $\qty{100}{m}$), then above it the population,
the community, the ecosystem and the biosphere. A cell respires; a
molecule does not. A population evolves; an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} does not.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Surface and volume]\label{prop:b1:organism-environment:sv}
For bodies of the same shape and of characteristic size $L$, the surface
grows as $L^2$ and the volume as $L^3$, so the surface-to-volume ratio
falls as $1/L$:
\[
\frac{S}{V} \propto \frac{1}{L}, \qquad
\frac{S}{V}\Big|_{\text{sphere}} = \frac{4\pi r^2}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3} = \frac{3}{r}.
\]
Everything an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} exchanges crosses a surface, and everything it
consumes is proportional to its volume: a large \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} cannot feed
its volume through its outer surface.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Scaling a shape by a factor $k$ multiplies every area by $k^2$ and every
volume by $k^3$; the ratio is multiplied by $1/k$. For a sphere the
formula is exact.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A bacterium and a human]\label{ex:b1:organism-environment:svnumbers}
A bacterium of radius $\qty{0.5}{\micro m}$ has
$S/V = 3/r = \qty{6e6}{m^{-1}}$: every cubic micrometre of its cytoplasm
is within half a micrometre of the outside, and diffusion through its
membrane feeds it entirely. A human modelled as a sphere of \qty{70}{L}
has $r = \qty{0.26}{m}$ and $S/V = \qty{12}{m^{-1}}$, half a million
times less. The skin, about \qty{2}{m^2}, could not supply the oxygen
that \qty{70}{kg} of tissue consumes; the lung, folded inside the chest,
offers \qty{100}{m^2}, and the intestine, folded three times over,
\qty{30}{m^2}.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Exchange surfaces are folded, thin and ventilated]\label{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}
Above a size of about a millimetre, an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} cannot be fed by
diffusion through its outer surface. Multicellular \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} of any size
therefore carry \emph{specialised exchange surfaces}\index{exchange surface}, whose common design solves the same problem four ways: a large
area (folding: villi, alveoli, gill lamellae, root hairs, leaf
blades), a small thickness (one cell layer or less between the two
media), a renewed medium on the outside (ventilation, water current,
transpiration stream), and a transport system on the inside (blood,
sap) that carries what crosses the surface to the rest of the volume.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fick's law (\cref{ch:b1:membranes-transport}) gives the flux across a
surface as proportional to area, to the concentration difference and to
the inverse of thickness; renewing the outer medium keeps the
concentration difference large; a transport system removes what has
crossed and keeps the inner concentration low. Each of the four features
increases the flux; a large \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} needs all four.
\end{proof}

\section{The internal environment and homeostasis}

\begin{definition}[Internal environment]\label{def:b1:organism-environment:milieu}
In a multicellular animal most cells are not in contact with the outside
world but bathed in an \emph{internal environment}\index{internal environment}: the extracellular fluid (blood plasma, lymph, the
interstitial fluid between cells) whose composition, temperature and
volume the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} regulates. The cells exchange with this fluid; the
fluid exchanges with the outside through the specialised surfaces. The
idea is Claude Bernard's (1865): the constancy of the internal
environment is the condition of a free and independent life.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Homeostasis]\label{def:b1:organism-environment:homeostasis}
\emph{Homeostasis}\index{homeostasis} is the maintenance of a regulated
variable of the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:milieu}{internal environment} (temperature, glucose
concentration, pH, osmolarity, blood pressure) close to a
\emph{set point}\index{set point} despite external changes. It is
achieved by \emph{negative feedback}\index{negative feedback}: a
\emph{sensor} measures the variable, an integrating centre compares it
with the set point, and an \emph{effector} acts in the direction that
cancels the deviation. The variable is not fixed but oscillates within a
narrow band around the set point.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Endotherms and ectotherms]\label{prop:b1:organism-environment:thermo}
An \emph{ectotherm}\index{ectotherm} (most invertebrates, fishes,
amphibians, reptiles) has a body temperature that follows the
environment's; its \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{metabolic rate} rises with temperature, roughly
doubling for every \qty{10}{\celsius}. An \emph{endotherm}\index{endotherm}
(mammals, birds) holds its body temperature at a \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:homeostasis}{set point} by
producing heat metabolically. Within a \emph{thermoneutral
zone}\index{thermoneutral zone} of ambient temperatures it does so at
its basal rate, adjusting only insulation and blood flow to the skin;
below that zone its \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{metabolic rate} rises linearly as the air cools,
because heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference and must
be matched by heat production; above it, cooling by evaporation costs
water and energy.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Partial proof]
For a body at $T_b$ in air at $T_a$, the heat lost per unit time through
its surface is $P = hS\,(T_b - T_a)$ (Newton's law of cooling), with $h$
a coefficient lowered by fur, feathers or fat. Steady temperature demands
a heat production equal to $P$, hence linear in $T_a$ below the
\omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:thermo}{thermoneutral zone}, with slope $-hS$. The existence and width of the
zone, where $h$ itself is adjusted (fluffing the fur, constricting skin
vessels), are physiological facts, not consequences of the equation.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A night at two degrees]\label{ex:b1:organism-environment:night}
The rabbit of the opening, at rest in its burrow, produces about
\qty{6}{W} of heat in its \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:thermo}{thermoneutral zone}. Outside at
\qty{2}{\celsius}, twenty degrees below the lower edge of that zone,
its production must rise to about \qty{12}{W}: shivering and the
oxidation of its fat reserves supply the difference, and its feeding
time lengthens. The lizard on the same bank does not fight the cold: its
body drops to \qty{3}{\celsius}, its metabolism falls to a twentieth of
its summer value, and it does not move until the sun returns.
\end{example}

\section{Metabolic rate and body mass}

\begin{definition}[Metabolic rate, allometry]\label{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}
The \emph{metabolic rate}\index{metabolic rate} $B$ of an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} is
the rate at which it converts chemical energy, measured in watts as heat
production or as oxygen consumption ($\qty{1}{L}$ of $\mathrm{O_2}$
consumed $\approx \qty{20}{kJ}$). The \emph{basal metabolic
rate}\index{basal metabolic rate} is that of a resting, fasting
\omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:thermo}{endotherm} in its \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:thermo}{thermoneutral zone}. A quantity $Y$ is said to scale
\emph{allometrically}\index{allometry} with body mass $M$ when
$Y = a\,M^{b}$ with an exponent $b \neq 1$; on a log--log plot the
relation is a straight line of slope $b$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Kleiber's law]\label{thm:b1:organism-environment:kleiber}
\index{Kleiber's law}
Across mammals and birds, from a shrew to a whale, the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{basal metabolic
rate} scales as the three-quarter power of body mass:
\[
B \approx \qty{3.4}{W}\times\left(\frac{M}{\qty{1}{kg}}\right)^{3/4}.
\]
The \emph{mass-specific} rate $B/M$ therefore falls as $M^{-1/4}$: each
gram of a mouse burns energy about twenty times faster than each gram of
an elephant.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Kleiber (1932) plotted the measured heat production of animals from
\qty{20}{g} to \qty{600}{kg} against their mass on logarithmic axes and
found a straight line of slope $0.74$, not the $0.67$ that a
surface-proportional heat loss would predict. The line has since been
extended over eight orders of magnitude in mass with the same slope
within a few hundredths. Why the exponent is $3/4$ rather than $2/3$
remains debated: the leading explanation ties it to the geometry of the
branching networks (vessels, airways) that deliver oxygen to every cell.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Reading an allometric plot]\label{met:b1:organism-environment:loglog}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Plot $\log Y$ against $\log M$ (or use logarithmic axes). A
        power law $Y = aM^b$ becomes the line $\log Y = \log a + b\log M$.
  \item The exponent $b$ is the slope: take two points a decade apart
        in $M$; $b$ is the number of decades $Y$ has climbed. An
        exponent of $1$ means proportionality (isometry); $b < 1$, the
        quantity grows more slowly than mass; $b > 1$, faster.
  \item The prefactor $a$ is the value of $Y$ at $M = 1$ (where
        $\log M = 0$).
  \item To compare two \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms}, use the ratio: $Y_2/Y_1 =
        (M_2/M_1)^b$. Per unit mass, the exponent becomes $b-1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Mouse and elephant]\label{ex:b1:organism-environment:mouseelephant}
A \qty{20}{g} mouse: $B = 3.4\times 0.02^{0.75} = \qty{0.18}{W}$, or
\qty{9}{W/kg}. A \qty{4}{t} elephant: $B = 3.4\times 4000^{0.75} =
\qty{1700}{W}$, or \qty{0.43}{W/kg}. The elephant burns ten thousand
times more than the mouse, but each of its grams burns twenty-one times
less: $(4000/0.02)^{-1/4} = 1/21$. The mouse must eat a tenth of its
mass every day; the elephant, a fiftieth. The mouse's heart beats
\qty{600}{} times a minute, the elephant's \qty{30}{}: heart rate, like
$B/M$, scales as $M^{-1/4}$, and both animals' hearts beat about a
billion times in a lifetime.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Two scaling exponents, and what follows from them]\label{rem:b1:organism-environment:consequences}
Many rates of an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} scale as $M^{-1/4}$ (heart rate, breathing
rate, mass-specific metabolism) and many times as $M^{+1/4}$ (lifespan,
gestation, time to maturity); their products, such as heartbeats per
lifetime, are then independent of mass. Sizes of organs scale close to
$M^{1}$ (heart, lung, blood volume are a fixed fraction of the body),
while the surfaces through which they exchange scale as $M^{3/4}$ ---
which is why a large animal's lung is more finely folded than a small
one's, not merely bigger.
\end{remark}

\section{Two model organisms}

\begin{proposition}[A mammal and a flowering plant]\label{prop:b1:organism-environment:models}
The two multicellular \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} that this year uses as models solve the
problems above in opposite ways. A mammal is a mobile heterotroph with
a regulated \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:milieu}{internal environment}: it obtains matter and energy by
finding and eating food, exchanges through internal surfaces (gut, lung,
kidney) supplied by a circulation, holds its temperature and its blood
composition constant, and reaches a fixed adult form. A flowering plant
is a fixed \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:trophy}{photoautotroph} with an open, modular body: it obtains energy
and carbon from the light and air at its leaves and water and minerals
from the soil at its roots, exchanges through external surfaces that it
keeps enlarging by growth throughout its life, does not regulate its
temperature, and adjusts its form to its site. The next two chapters
describe each in turn.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The same hour, two budgets]\label{ex:b1:organism-environment:budgets}
In the hour of the opening, the rabbit turned \qty{500}{kJ} of grass
into heat, motion and a little growth, keeping its blood at
\qty{39}{\celsius} and its glucose near \qty{6}{mmol/L}; it did so by
moving to the food. The oak turned a few hundred kilojoules of the
sunlight on its crown into sugar, lost \qty{5}{L} of water through its
leaves to do so, and grew a few micrometres of new wood; it did so by
being where the light and water were. Both are \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{open systems}; both keep
themselves going by exchanging; the shapes of their exchanges are the
shapes of their lives.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:1}
Name the three kinds of exchange between an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} and its
environment, with one example of each for a mammal and one for a plant.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:2}
State what happens to the surface-to-volume ratio when a spherical cell
doubles its radius, and why this limits cell size.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:3}
Define \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:homeostasis}{homeostasis} and name the three components of a negative-feedback
loop, with their identity for the regulation of body temperature.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:4}
From the metabolic-rate figure, by what factor does the lizard's
\omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{metabolic rate} fall between \qty{30}{\celsius} and \qty{10}{\celsius},
and by what factor does the mouse's rise between \qty{20}{\celsius} and
\qty{0}{\celsius}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:5}
A cube-shaped \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} of side \qty{1}{mm} needs \qty{1}{nmol} of
oxygen per second per cubic millimetre, and its surface can take up at
most \qty{2}{nmol/s} per square millimetre. Can it live by diffusion
through its surface? Repeat for a side of \qty{10}{mm}. Find the
largest side that can.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:6}
Using \omterm{thm:b1:organism-environment:kleiber}{Kleiber's law}, compute the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{basal metabolic rate} of a
\qty{500}{kg} horse and of a \qty{5}{g} shrew, and the mass-specific
rate of each. How many times its own mass in food, at \qty{5}{kJ/g},
must the shrew eat per day if its daily expenditure is three times its
basal rate?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:7}
A resting human produces \qty{80}{W}. Convert this to litres of oxygen
consumed per minute and to kilojoules per day. A \qty{2000}{kcal} diet
corresponds to how many watts? (\qty{1}{kcal} = \qty{4.18}{kJ}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:8}
The lung of a \qty{70}{kg} human has an \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface} of about
\qty{100}{m^2}. If lung surface scaled with body mass to the power
$3/4$, what surface would a \qty{20}{g} mouse's lung have, and a
\qty{4000}{kg} elephant's? Compare with the surface of a sphere of the
animal's volume (density \qty{1000}{kg/m^3}).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:9}
A negative-feedback loop cannot hold a variable exactly at its \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:homeostasis}{set
point}. Explain why, using the temperature loop, and say what determines
the width of the band within which the variable oscillates.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:10}
A body loses heat at the rate $P = hS(T_b - T_a)$ with
$h = \qty{5}{W.m^{-2}.K^{-1}}$ for a furred mammal. For a \qty{20}{g}
mouse modelled as a sphere of density \qty{1000}{kg/m^3}, compute the
surface, the heat loss at $T_a = \qty{0}{\celsius}$ with $T_b =
\qty{37}{\celsius}$, and compare with its basal rate from \omterm{thm:b1:organism-environment:kleiber}{Kleiber's law}.
What must the mouse do?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:11}
If heat loss scaled with surface ($M^{2/3}$) and heat production with
\omterm{thm:b1:organism-environment:kleiber}{Kleiber's law} ($M^{3/4}$), show that the smallest \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:thermo}{endotherms} are the
ones that struggle most in the cold, and that above some mass an animal
would have trouble losing heat. Estimate, with $h = \qty{5}{W.m^{-2}.K^{-1}}$
and $T_b - T_a = \qty{20}{K}$, the mass at which basal production
equals loss for a spherical body.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:organism-environment:12}
``Because a plant does not regulate its temperature or its internal
fluids, it is a simpler system than an animal.'' Discuss in a paragraph,
using the two exchange strategies of this chapter, the surfaces each
uses, and the meaning of \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:homeostasis}{homeostasis}.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Mouse and the Elephant}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- two mammals five orders of magnitude
apart: their surfaces, their heat, their food, their hearts and their
years, ending on the ratio that Kleiber's law predicts}]
\label{pb:b1:organism-environment:1}
A house mouse has a mass of \qty{20}{g}; an African elephant,
\qty{4000}{kg}. Both keep a body temperature near \qty{37}{\celsius},
and both are modelled, when a shape is needed, as spheres of density
\qty{1000}{kg/m^3}. \omterm{thm:b1:organism-environment:kleiber}{Kleiber's law} is $B = \qty{3.4}{W}\,(M/\qty{1}{kg})^{3/4}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Geometry.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the volume of each animal.
  \item Compute the radius of the equivalent sphere for each.
  \item Compute the surface of each sphere.
  \item Compute the surface-to-volume ratio of each, and their ratio.
  \item Show that this ratio equals the cube root of the mass ratio,
        and check numerically.
  \item Real mice and elephants are not spheres. Say, with a reason,
        whether the real surface-to-volume ratio of each is larger or
        smaller than the sphere's, and which animal departs more.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Heat.}
Heat is lost through the surface at the rate $P = hS\,(T_b - T_a)$.
Take $h = \qty{10}{W.m^{-2}.K^{-1}}$ for bare skin and $T_a =
\qty{17}{\celsius}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the heat loss of each bare sphere.
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{basal metabolic rate} of each from \omterm{thm:b1:organism-environment:kleiber}{Kleiber's law}.
  \item Compare loss and production for the elephant. Is bare skin a
        problem for it?
  \item Compare loss and production for the mouse. What value of $h$
        would its fur have to provide for basal production to cover
        the loss?
  \item An elephant's ears add \qty{4}{m^2} of thin, richly perfused
        surface and it flaps them in the heat. Explain what they are for,
        using Part I.
  \item Explain why no \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:thermo}{endotherm} smaller than a few grams exists, and
        why the smallest ones (shrews, hummingbirds) let their
        temperature fall at night.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Food.}
A free-living animal spends about twice its basal rate over a day.
The mouse's seeds supply \qty{18}{kJ} per gram, the elephant's fresh
grass \qty{5}{kJ} of digestible energy per gram.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the daily energy expenditure of each in kilojoules.
  \item Compute the daily food intake of each in grams.
  \item Express each intake as a fraction of body mass, and compare.
  \item The elephant's gut is about \qty{35}{m} long and the mouse's
        \qty{40}{cm}. Compute the ratio of gut lengths and compare it
        with the ratio of body lengths (radii of Part I). What does
        the comparison suggest about the scaling of the gut?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Hearts and years.}
Heart rate scales as $f = f_0\,(M/\qty{1}{kg})^{-1/4}$, lifespan as
$\tau = \tau_0\,(M/\qty{1}{kg})^{1/4}$. The mouse's heart beats
\qty{600}{} times a minute and it lives two years.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute $f_0$ and $\tau_0$ from the mouse.
  \item Predict the elephant's heart rate and lifespan.
  \item Compute the number of heartbeats in the life of each animal.
  \item Explain, from the two exponents, why this number is the same.
  \item Predict the heart rate and lifespan of a \qty{70}{kg} human
        from the same formulas, and compare with the real values
        (\qty{70}{} beats per minute, some \qty{80}{} years). Which
        prediction fails, and what might the reason be?
  \item Breathing rate scales like heart rate. The mouse breathes
        \qty{160}{} times a minute; predict the elephant's breathing
        rate, and compute the number of heartbeats per breath for each.
  \item Write the mass-specific \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{metabolic rate} $B/M$ as a power of $M$
        and give its exponent.
  \item Compute $B/M$ for the mouse and for the elephant.
  \item State the result: the ratio of the mouse's mass-specific
        \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:allometry}{metabolic rate} to the elephant's, and the power of the mass
        ratio that it equals.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
