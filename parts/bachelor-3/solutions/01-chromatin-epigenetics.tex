\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:chromatin-epigenetics} --- Chromatin and Epigenetics}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:1}
Two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 (an octamer) with \qty{147}{bp}
of DNA in $1.65$ turns; linkers of \qtyrange{20}{60}{bp}, giving a
repeat near \qty{200}{bp}. The nuclease cuts only the exposed linker
DNA, never inside a core, so every fragment is a whole number of
repeats: a ladder of \num{200}, \num{400}, \qty{600}{bp}, not a smear.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:2}
Diploid: $6.0\times 10^{9}$ bp; $6.0\times 10^{9}/190 \approx
3.2\times 10^{7}$ \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes}; length $6.0\times 10^{9}\times
\qty{0.34}{nm} = \qty{2.0}{m}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:3}
H3K27me3: \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histone} H3, lysine 27, trimethylated --- silent \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} of
developmentally repressed genes, written by PRC2 and read by the
chromodomain proteins of PRC1. H4K16ac: H4, lysine 16, acetylated ---
active, open \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} (it breaks a nucleosome--nucleosome contact).
H3S10ph: H3, serine 10, phosphorylated --- a mitotic mark associated
with chromosome condensation (and, transiently, with gene activation
by some signals); not a stable silencing or activating mark.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:4}
The orange gene is X-linked with an orange and a black allele; a
patched coat needs one of each, on two different X chromosomes, one of
which is silenced per cell. A male has one X and is either orange or
black all over. A calico male carries two X chromosomes and a Y (XXY),
and is sterile.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:5}
$\qty{200}{bp}\times \qty{0.34}{nm} = \qty{68}{nm}$ per bead of
\qty{11}{nm}: ratio $6.2$. Required: $\qty{4}{cm}/\qty{4}{\micro m} =
10^{4}$. Further folding by $10^{4}/6.2 \approx 1600$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:6}
$\mu = 0.98$, $\delta = 0.01$: $p^{*} = 0.01/0.03 = 0.33$; ratio $\mu -
\delta = 0.97$; half-life $\ln 2/(-\ln 0.97) = 22.8 \approx 23$
divisions. $\mu = 0.999$, $\delta = 0.001$: $p^{*} = 0.5$; ratio
$0.998$; half-life $\ln 2 / 0.002 \approx 350$ divisions.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:7}
$A$: active (promoter H3K4me3 with \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{acetylation}). $B$: silent,
Polycomb-repressed --- facultative \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{heterochromatin}, the state of a
developmental gene switched off in this lineage but used in others, so
$B$ is the one likely to be active in another cell type. $C$: silent,
constitutive \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{heterochromatin} (HP1 pathway), typically repeats or genes
silenced in every cell type.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:8}
\emph{UBE3A} is expressed only from the maternal allele in neurons. Her
deletion came from her father, on the allele that is silent anyway, so
she is healthy; when she transmits it, it becomes a maternal deletion:
each child has probability $1/2$ of inheriting it and then has Angelman
syndrome. Had the deletion come from her mother, she would herself have
Angelman syndrome; a healthy carrier can only have inherited it
paternally. (A paternal deletion of the whole region would give
Prader--Willi; \emph{UBE3A} alone is not enough for that.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:9}
One X lacking \emph{\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{Xist}} cannot be inactivated, so the other X is
silenced in every cell: inactivation is complete but no longer random
(skewed), and the embryo is viable. Both X lacking \emph{\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{Xist}}: no
inactivation, two active X chromosomes, twofold X-linked expression, and
the female embryo dies early. A \emph{\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{Xist}} transgene on an autosome
coats that autosome in cis and silences its genes, causing loss of
function of one autosomal copy --- the experiment that showed
\emph{\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{Xist}} is sufficient for silencing in cis.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:10}
Methylated cytosines deaminate to thymine, which repair does not
recognise, so a methylated \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpG} mutates to TpG or CpA at a high rate.
Only sequences unmethylated in the \emph{germ line}, generation after
generation, keep their \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpGs}; islands have kept theirs, so they have
been unmethylated in the germ line for most of vertebrate history.
A gene methylated only in liver is unmethylated in sperm and egg;
mutations that arise in liver cells are not inherited, so somatic
methylation leaves no footprint in the sequence.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:11}
At replication the old, marked \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{histones} are shared between the two
daughter duplexes and interleaved with new, unmarked ones, so each
daughter domain is about half marked. HP1 bound to a retained marked
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosome} recruits SUV39H, which methylates the unmarked neighbours;
as long as marked \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes} are dense enough to bring a \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{writer} to
every gap, the domain is refilled before the next division --- the
same feedback as the theorem's high $\mu$. Spreading stops at
boundaries: insulator proteins (CTCF), highly transcribed genes and
tRNA genes, \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes} carrying incompatible active marks (H3K4me3,
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{acetylation}) that the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{writer} cannot use, and the point where \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{erasers}
(demethylases, HDAC turnover) outpace the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{writer}. Test: recruit HP1
transiently to a reporter (a fusion with a DNA-binding domain whose
binding is drug-controlled), release it, and follow the reporter's
silence and H3K9me3 over divisions; predict persistence that depends
on the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{writer}, and loss if the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{writer} or the \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{reader}'s dimerisation is
mutated, or if an insulator is placed within the domain.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:12}
Exclude: a shared environment (the grandchildren's own diet and
poverty); a uterine effect --- the famine-exposed mother's daughter was
a fetus, with her germ cells already present, so the grandchild's
germline was exposed directly and the effect is not transgenerational;
genetic differences between families that survived and those that did
not; reverse causation (body mass itself alters methylation in blood);
differences in blood cell composition; and multiple testing across many
\omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpGs}. Mouse experiment: expose inbred F$_0$ \emph{males} (no uterine
route), mate them to unexposed females or use in vitro fertilisation
and embryo transfer, and follow F$_2$ and F$_3$ through the paternal
line, measuring methylation in the sperm of each generation; a mark
that persists in F$_3$ sperm and phenotype, in a strain with no genetic
variation, is germline transmission.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:1}
\textbf{1.} $6.4\times 10^{9}\times \qty{0.34}{nm} = \qty{2.2}{m}$;
$6.4\times 10^{9}/200 = 3.2\times 10^{7}$ \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes}.
\textbf{2.} \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{Histone}: $3.2\times 10^{7}\times 1.08\times 10^{5} =
3.5\times 10^{12}$ Da $= \qty{5.7}{pg}$. DNA: $6.4\times 10^{9}\times
650 = 4.2\times 10^{12}$ Da $= \qty{6.9}{pg}$. Comparable masses.
\textbf{3.} Nucleus: $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi\,(\qty{5}{\micro m})^{3} =
\qty{520}{\micro m^3}$. Core: $\pi\,(5.5)^{2}\times 5.5 =
\qty{520}{nm^3} = 5.2\times 10^{-7}\,\unit{\micro m^3}$; total
$3.2\times 10^{7}\times 5.2\times 10^{-7} = \qty{17}{\micro m^3}$:
about \qty{3}{\%} of the nucleus.
\textbf{4.} $3.2\times 10^{7}\times \qty{11}{nm} = \qty{0.35}{m}$;
\omterm{prop:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:compaction}{packing ratio} $2.2/0.35 \approx 6$.
\textbf{5.} $6.4\times 10^{9}/2\times 10^{5} = \num{32000}$ loops of
\num{1000} \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{nucleosomes} each.
\textbf{6.} $0.21^{2}\times 6.4\times 10^{9} = 2.8\times 10^{8}$;
observed $5.6\times 10^{7}$, so \qty{80}{\%} lost, by deamination of
5-methylcytosine to thymine (unrepaired C$\to$T transitions at
methylated \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:methylation}{CpGs}).
\textbf{7.} $p_{n+1} = 0.99\,p_{n} + 0.02\,(1 - p_{n}) = 0.97\,p_{n} +
0.02$; $p^{*} = 0.02/0.03 = 0.67$.
\textbf{8.} $p_{n} = 0.67 + 0.33\times 0.97^{n}$: $n = 10$: $0.91$;
$n = 50$: $0.74$; $n = 200$: $0.67$ (the excess is $7\times 10^{-4}$).
\textbf{9.} $\ln 2/(-\ln 0.97) = 22.8 \approx 23$ divisions; at one a
week, about \num{23} weeks --- five months.
\textbf{10.} Each replication pairs one old strand, still methylated, with
one new strand that no enzyme methylates; the old strands are neither
gained nor lost, the number of strands doubles, so the methylated
fraction halves. $0.80/2^{n} < 0.05$ requires $2^{n} > 16$: five
divisions ($0.025$; four give exactly \qty{5}{\%}).
\textbf{11.} Ratio $0.9995 - 0.0005 = 0.999$; half-life $\ln
2/(-\ln 0.999) \approx 690$ divisions, about \num{13} years at one a
week.
\textbf{12.} A gene silent for a lifetime of some \num{4000} weekly
divisions needs a half-life of hundreds of divisions, which only the
feedback of question~11 gives: \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{readers} of the mark (MeCP2, HP1)
recruit its \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:histone-code}{writers} (H3K9 methylase, DNMT3), so a dense domain of marks
copies itself with an efficiency no single enzyme has.
\textbf{13.} One X carries the orange allele, the other the black one;
every skin cell has silenced one X and expresses only the other; the
descendants of a cell keep its choice, so a patch is a clone (or a group
of adjacent clones) that made the same choice.
\textbf{14.} All $N$ choose the orange-bearing X, or all choose the
other: $2\times (1/2)^{N} = 2^{1-N}$.
\textbf{15.} $2^{1-N} = 10^{-9}$: $N - 1 = \log_{2} 10^{9} = 29.9$,
$N \approx 30$ cells.
\textbf{16.} $\qty{3000}{cm^2}/\qty{15}{cm^2} = 200$ patches, far more
than $30$ founders: during growth the descendants of a founder are
dispersed by cell mixing and migration, so one clone forms several
islands (and neighbours with the same choice merge, which works the
other way but less).
\textbf{17.} One \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:x-inactivation}{Barr body} per X beyond the first: XXY one, XXX two, XY
none.
\textbf{18.} Tolerated when the gene has a functional Y homologue, so
males also have two copies (the pseudoautosomal genes and pairs such as
\emph{KDM5C}/\emph{KDM5D}); it matters in X aneuploidies --- in Turner
syndrome (XO) the escapee genes are present in a single copy, which is
why an XO individual is not simply a normal female (\emph{SHOX}
haploinsufficiency and short stature), and in XXY the escapees are
overdosed.
\textbf{19.} $1/\num{15000} = 6.7\times 10^{-5}$; deletions $0.70\times
6.7\times 10^{-5} = 4.7\times 10^{-5}$ (one in \num{21000}); maternal
disomy $1.7\times 10^{-5}$ (one in \num{60000}).
\textbf{20.} Angelman is the loss of one gene, \emph{UBE3A}, so a single
point mutation in the maternal allele suffices. Prader--Willi is the
loss of several paternally expressed genes at once (\emph{SNRPN}, the
small-RNA cluster); a point mutation inactivates one of them and gives
at most a partial phenotype, never the whole syndrome.
\textbf{21.} \qty{20}{\%} of the children receive a paternal deletion and
have Prader--Willi; none has Angelman, which needs a maternal deletion.
\textbf{22.} Three chromosomes, two maternal and one paternal, one lost
at random: the paternal is lost with probability $1/3$, leaving
maternal disomy and Prader--Willi; with probability $2/3$ a maternal
copy is lost and the child is normal.
\textbf{23.} Paternal genes should promote demand on the mother; their
loss (Prader--Willi) should reduce demand --- which fits the poor
suckling and failure to thrive of the newborn --- while the later
insatiable appetite is the opposite and is harder to fit (a proposed
reading is that the paternal genes normally switch the child toward
weaning). Angelman, the loss of a maternal gene, should increase
demand; the prolonged sucking and frequent night waking of Angelman
children fit, the seizures and speech loss are not about resources at
all.
\textbf{24.} Target the paternal antisense transcript
(\emph{UBE3A-ATS}) with an antisense oligonucleotide that degrades it
or blocks its elongation: the paternal \emph{UBE3A} is intact and is
silenced only in cis by that RNA, so removing the RNA restores its
expression. The \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:imprinting}{imprinting control region}'s methylation is untouched,
so \emph{SNRPN} and the other imprinted genes keep their normal
parental pattern.
\textbf{25.} Half-life of a mark: about $23$ divisions without feedback,
about $690$ with it; the calico coat was decided by a founder population
of about $30$ cells.
\end{solution}
