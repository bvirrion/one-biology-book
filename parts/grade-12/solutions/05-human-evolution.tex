\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:5}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:6}
Two ancestral \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} fused in the \omterm{def:g12:human-evolution:lineage}{human lineage} into one, so 24
pairs became 23. The fused \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} carries, in the middle, the

a record of the fusion; the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} content matches the two chimpanzee
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} for \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}. Descent from a common set of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes},
modified once, is what kinship predicts.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:8}

\emph{Homo habilis} and early \emph{H. erectus}. 100\,000 years ago:
\emph{H. erectus} (last \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}), \emph{H. heidelbergensis} (last),

\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:9}
Interbreeding between modern humans leaving Africa and Neanderthals
met in western Asia and Europe some 50\,000 years ago. \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{Populations}
that stayed in sub-Saharan Africa never met Neanderthals, so they
carry none.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:10}
Upright (forward opening), small brain, large jaws, 2.8 million years:
an australopithecine, placed on a side branch near the base of the
lineage --- a relative of the ancestors of \emph{Homo}, not a
demonstrated ancestor.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:11}
The great apes form a \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade}, and humans are one branch inside it;
since a \omterm{prop:g12:phylogenetic-trees:rules}{clade} includes all descendants of its node, humans are apes,
as they are primates and mammals. "Descend from apes" wrongly puts
apes at a node; the living apes are sister tips.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:12}
Hypothesis 1: competition --- modern humans, more numerous or better
organised, took the resources; evidence would be a decline of
Neanderthal sites where the two overlapped. Hypothesis 2: absorption
--- small Neanderthal \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} were partly absorbed by interbreeding
and partly went extinct by drift and chance in a changing climate;
evidence would be the Neanderthal \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} in us and signs of small,
declining Neanderthal \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} before contact. Both may be true.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:13}
Each step of the dispersal was made by a small group carrying a sample
of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} it left; each new region was founded
from the last. Diversity fell at every founding, so the farthest
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} have the least. The dispersal proceeded by successive
small migrations rather than by mass movement.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:14}
Dairying made milk a food for adults: carriers of the persistence
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} fed better and left more descendants. Migration to weak-sun
latitudes made vitamin D scarce: lighter skin, making more of it, was
favoured. Farming created dense \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} and standing water where
malaria spread: \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} protecting against it rose. In each case a
change in the way of life changed which \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} reproduced.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:human-evolution:15}
Selection is any difference in reproduction between carriers of
different \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}; medicine changes which \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} matter, not whether
any do --- resistance to infections, fertility, the age of
reproduction still vary and are inherited. The three traits show
selection acting within the last ten thousand years, driven by culture
itself. And drift continues in every \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} regardless. Human
evolution has changed direction, not stopped.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g12:human-evolution:1}
\textbf{1.} A: $21.5/0.15 \approx \qty{1.43}{m}$; B: $18.5/0.15
\approx \qty{1.23}{m}$.

\textbf{2.} $0.8 \times 1.43 = \qty{1.14}{m}$ and $0.8 \times 1.23 =
\qty{0.98}{m}$: both strides are shorter than a slow walk's; they were
walking, unhurried.

\textbf{3.} A deep heel: the heel strikes first, as in human walking;
an arch: a foot that acts as a lever and spring; a parallel big toe:
a foot for pushing off, not for grasping branches.

\textbf{4.} A striding, heel-first gait with a human-like foot,
sustained over \qty{27}{m}: habitual bipedalism. Unknown from prints:
the pelvis, the femur angle, the spinal opening, the spine's curves.

\textbf{5.} That upright walking preceded the enlargement of the
brain by more than a million years.

\textbf{6.} 1: australopithecine. 2: \emph{Homo habilis}. 3: \emph{Homo
erectus}. 4: Neanderthal. 5: \emph{Homo sapiens}.

\textbf{7.} From 450 at 3.0 million to 650 at 1.9, 950 at 1.0, then
1450 and 1350: the fastest growth is between 1.9 and 1.0 million
years (\qty{300}{cm^3} per million years), then between 1.0 and 0.06.

\textbf{8.} No: Neanderthal brains were as large or larger. Modern
humans are identified by the high forehead, the small face under the
braincase, the chin and the rounded skull.

\textbf{9.} Two \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} of the bush living side by side, distinct
in anatomy, that interbred: the Neanderthal \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} in living people
outside Africa is the evidence.

\textbf{10.} A fossil is a tip on its own side branch; its combination
of characters places it near the node from which later \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
descend, but no character proves it was the very \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} that
gave rise to them. It is a relative close to the ancestor, which is
all a fossil can be shown to be.

\textbf{11.} Segments making up 1.6\% of her genome are closer to the
Neanderthal sequence than to any modern one: they were inherited from
Neanderthal ancestors who interbred with modern humans.

\textbf{12.} The chart gives about 1.8\% Neanderthal in Europe and
about 3.5\% of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} of the Asian \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} in Melanesia: her European ancestry
brought the Neanderthal share, her Melanesian ancestry the other
(diluted by her mixed descent).

\textbf{13.} $50\,000/25 = 2000$ generations. The share is not diluted
because the whole \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} carries it: mixing with other carriers
keeps the average constant; dilution happens only when carriers mix
with non-carriers, and outside Africa there were none.

\textbf{14.} That the boundary was not sealed: after some 500\,000
years of separation the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} could still produce fertile
offspring --- \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} in the making, isolated by geography more than
by biology.

\textbf{15.} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{Mitochondria} pass only through mothers: no Neanderthal
maternal line has survived among living humans, whether because
Neanderthal mothers' descendants died out or because the interbreeding
ran mostly one way. The nuclear traces survived; that lineage did not.

\textbf{16.} $1.2/0.27 \approx 4.4$ million years --- a lower estimate,
since the clock was fitted on more distant nodes.

\textbf{17.} The fossils are older than the clock's estimate; the true
node is at least as old as the oldest fossil of the lineage, so 6 to 7
million years, and the clock's rate for this recent interval must be
somewhat slower than 0.27\%.

\textbf{18.} \emph{H. erectus} and, in the timeline, the last
\emph{Paranthropus} (and, from about 0.7, \emph{H. heidelbergensis});
of these, only the lineage leading through \emph{H. heidelbergensis}
to \emph{H. sapiens} has living descendants, and \emph{H. erectus}
only through \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} that fed into it --- one line out of several.

\textbf{19.} The skeleton and brain have changed little in that time,
while tools, art, language, farming and cities have transformed how
humans live; the changes are transmitted by learning, accumulate
within generations, and now shape the environment in which the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
evolves.

\textbf{20.} Walker A stood about \qty{1.4}{m} and walked slowly on
human-like feet; feet came first, the brain a million years later, the
chin last; the student carries \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} of two extinct \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}.
\end{solution}
