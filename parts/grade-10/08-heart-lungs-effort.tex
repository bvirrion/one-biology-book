\chapter{Heart and Lungs During Effort}\label{ch:g10:heart-lungs-effort}

Run up four flights of stairs and stop at the top. Your chest is heaving
--- forty breaths a minute instead of twelve, each one three times
deeper --- and your heart is hammering at a hundred and seventy. Nothing
about the stairs required you to decide this; the body arranged it, in
seconds, because the muscles of \cref{ch:g10:exercise-and-energy} were
demanding ten times more oxygen than a minute earlier. This chapter is
about the delivery system --- lungs, heart, blood vessels --- and about
how its output is multiplied when effort begins.

\section{Ventilation}

\begin{definition}[Ventilation]\label{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}
\emph{Pulmonary ventilation}\index{ventilation} is the volume of air
moved into and out of the lungs per minute: the volume of one breath
(the \emph{tidal volume}) multiplied by the number of breaths per
minute (the \emph{respiratory rate}). At rest, about \qty{0.5}{L}
twelve to fifteen times a minute: \qtyrange{6}{8}{L/min}. In the
lungs the air reaches some 300 million \emph{alveoli}\index{alveolus},
thin-walled sacs wrapped in capillaries, where oxygen passes into the
blood and carbon dioxide out of it.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Ventilation during exercise]\label{prop:g10:heart-lungs-effort:vent-exercise}
During exercise both the tidal volume and the rate rise: a fit adult
can breathe \qty{2.5}{L} forty times a minute, \qty{100}{L/min} or
more, fifteen times the resting value. The rise begins within the
first seconds of effort, before any change in the blood, and settles
at a level proportional to the oxygen being consumed.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{The heart and the two circulations}

\begin{definition}[Cardiac output]\label{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}
The heart is a double pump of four chambers. The right side receives
the blood returning from the organs and sends it to the lungs
(\emph{pulmonary circulation}); the left side receives it back from
the lungs and sends it to the organs (\emph{systemic circulation}).
Valves between the chambers and at the exits make the flow one-way.
The \emph{cardiac output}\index{cardiac output} $Q$ is the volume of
blood each side pumps per minute: the volume ejected per beat, the
\emph{stroke volume} $V_s$, multiplied by the \emph{heart rate} $f$,
\[
Q = f \times V_s .
\]
At rest, $70$ beats per minute of \qty{70}{mL}: about \qty{5}{L/min}
--- the whole blood volume once a minute.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Cardiac output during exercise]\label{prop:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output-exercise}
During exercise the heart rate rises with the power delivered, up to a
maximum of roughly $220$ minus the age in years, and the stroke volume
rises too, by about half in an untrained person and more in a trained
one. The \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}{cardiac output} of a young adult goes from \qty{5}{L/min} at
rest to \qtyrange{20}{25}{L/min} at maximal effort, and to
\qty{35}{L/min} or more in an endurance champion, whose stroke volume
can exceed \qty{180}{mL}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Getting the blood where it is needed}

\begin{proposition}[Redistribution of the blood flow]\label{prop:g10:heart-lungs-effort:redistribution}
At rest the muscles receive about a fifth of the \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}{cardiac output}; the
gut, liver and kidneys take half. During heavy exercise the small
arteries of the working muscles widen and those of the gut and kidneys
narrow: the muscles then receive more than four fifths of an output
that has itself quadrupled --- some \qty{20}{L/min} instead of
\qty{1}{L/min}, twenty times more. The brain's share is kept constant
in absolute terms, and the skin's rises to carry the heat away.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{proposition}[Oxygen delivery and extraction]\label{prop:g10:heart-lungs-effort:extraction}
Each litre of arterial blood carries about \qty{200}{mL} of oxygen,
almost all of it bound to the haemoglobin of the red blood \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. At
rest the organs remove about \qty{50}{mL} from each litre; during
exercise the working muscles remove up to \qty{150}{mL}, three times
more. The oxygen consumed per minute is the \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}{cardiac output} multiplied
by the amount removed per litre --- so the fivefold rise in output and
the threefold rise in extraction together give the fifteenfold rise in
oxygen consumption of \cref{ch:g10:exercise-and-energy}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[The oxygen budget checked]\label{ex:g10:heart-lungs-effort:fick}
Rest: \qty{5}{L/min} $\times$ \qty{50}{mL/L} $= \qty{250}{mL/min}$, the
resting consumption. Maximal effort of a trained student:
\qty{23}{L/min} $\times$ \qty{150}{mL/L} $= \qty{3.45}{L/min}$ --- a
$\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max of \qty{50}{mL/min} per kilogram for
\qty{69}{kg}, exactly the figure of the previous chapter.
\end{example}

\section{How the response is controlled}

\begin{proposition}[Nervous and hormonal control]\label{prop:g10:heart-lungs-effort:control}
Heart and breathing muscles are driven by nerve centres in the base of
the brain. Two sets of nerves reach the heart: one slows it, active at
rest; one accelerates it and strengthens each beat. At the start of
exercise the brain's command to the muscles is copied to these centres
--- the heart speeds up within one or two beats --- and sensors in the
muscles and joints report the movement. Then, as the exercise
continues, sensors in the arteries measure the carbon dioxide and
acidity of the blood and adjust \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{ventilation} and heart rate to keep
them near their resting values. The adrenal glands add the hormone
\emph{adrenaline}\index{adrenaline}, which reinforces the acceleration
and widens the muscles' arteries.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Cutting the slowing nerve of an animal raises its resting heart rate
from 70 to about 100; stimulating it stops the heart. Stimulating the
accelerating nerve doubles the rate. A person told to expect an effort
shows a rising heart rate before moving; a subject breathing air
enriched in carbon dioxide increases \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{ventilation} within a minute, and
one whose blood carbon dioxide is kept constant artificially during
exercise still increases it at the start of movement --- the two
mechanisms, anticipation and correction, are both real.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Computing the delivery of oxygen]\label{met:g10:heart-lungs-effort:compute}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{Ventilation}: tidal volume $\times$ rate. Only about 70\% of
        each breath reaches the \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{alveoli}; the rest fills the airways.
  \item \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}{Cardiac output}: $Q = f \times V_s$, in litres per minute if
        $V_s$ is in litres.
  \item Oxygen consumed per minute: $Q \times$ (oxygen removed per
        litre of blood).
  \item Flow to an organ: its share of $Q$. Compare absolute flows,
        not shares --- a smaller share of a larger output can be more
        blood.
  \item Check: consumption cannot exceed the $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max,
        and $f$ cannot exceed about $220 - $ age.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Where the limit lies]\label{rem:g10:heart-lungs-effort:limit}
\omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{Ventilation} can exceed \qty{150}{L/min} and the blood leaving the lungs
is still nearly saturated at maximal effort: the lungs are not the
bottleneck. The stroke volume is: it sets how much blood, hence how
much oxygen, each beat delivers. Endurance training enlarges the heart
and its stroke volume --- which is why a trained resting heart beats
at 45 instead of 70 for the same \qty{5}{L/min}, and why
$\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max rises with training.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Stress Test}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a cardiologist's exercise test:
heart rate, stroke volume, ventilation and oxygen extraction measured
at rising power, and the multiplication they achieve together}]
\label{pb:g10:heart-lungs-effort:1}
Tom, 17, mass \qty{68}{kg}, pedals on a laboratory bicycle while his
heart rate, \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{ventilation} and expired air are recorded. Results:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
power (W) & 0 & 75 & 150 & 225 & 300 \\ \hline
heart rate (per min) & 62 & 105 & 140 & 175 & 198 \\
stroke volume (mL) & 72 & 100 & 116 & 120 & 120 \\
tidal volume (L) & 0.5 & 1.2 & 1.8 & 2.3 & 2.6 \\
breaths per min & 13 & 18 & 24 & 32 & 44 \\
$\mathrm{O_2}$ removed per litre of blood (mL) & 52 & 90 & 120 & 145 & 155 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The heart.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute Tom's \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}{cardiac output} at each power.
  \item By what factor has the output risen at \qty{300}{W}? What
        part of that factor comes from the rate and what part from the
        stroke volume?
  \item Above what power does the stroke volume stop rising? What
        alone increases the output beyond that?
  \item Compare Tom's maximal heart rate with the rule of thumb.
  \item His resting output is about \qty{4.5}{L/min}. If his blood
        volume is \qty{5}{L}, how long does one full circuit take at
        rest, and at \qty{300}{W}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The lungs.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{ventilation} at each power.
  \item By what factor has it risen at \qty{300}{W}? Which of the two
        factors, volume or rate, contributes more?
  \item Tom's vital capacity is \qty{5.0}{L}. What fraction of it does
        each breath use at \qty{300}{W}?
  \item \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{Ventilation} rose by a larger factor than \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}{cardiac output}. Does
        this mean the lungs limit performance? Use
        \cref{rem:g10:heart-lungs-effort:limit}.
  \item Only 70\% of each breath reaches the \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{alveoli}. Compute the
        alveolar \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:ventilation}{ventilation} at rest and at \qty{300}{W}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The oxygen.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute Tom's oxygen consumption at each power, from the
        \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}{cardiac output} and the oxygen removed per litre.
  \item Express the value at \qty{300}{W} in millilitres per minute
        per kilogram. Is it a $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max, and of what
        kind of subject?
  \item By what factor has consumption risen from rest to
        \qty{300}{W}? Split it into the factor from the heart and the
        factor from extraction.
