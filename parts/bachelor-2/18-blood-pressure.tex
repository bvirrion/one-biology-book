\chapter{Regulation of Blood Pressure and Exercise}\label{ch:b2:blood-pressure}

Stand up quickly after lying down and, for a second, half a litre of
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} drains into the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} of your legs; the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}, receiving less,
pumps less, the pressure at the head falls, and the brain --- which
is a metre above the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} and cannot store oxygen --- is a second or
two from fainting. It almost never does, because within one beat a
reflex has tightened the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} and quickened the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}. Run for
a bus and the muscles need ten times the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} they had at rest;
they get it within a minute, and the pressure that drives it is
held steady while the resistance of the whole body falls by two
thirds. This chapter is about that regulation: what sets the
pressure, how it is sensed, the fast reflex and the slow hormones
that correct it, and how the system is reorganised, rather than
overridden, by exercise.

\section{What the pressure is}

\begin{theorem}[The equation of the circulation]\label{thm:b2:blood-pressure:equation}
\index{mean arterial pressure}\index{total peripheral resistance}
The \omterm{thm:b2:blood-pressure:equation}{mean arterial pressure} $\bar P$ is the product of what the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}
puts in and what the vessels let out:
\[
\bar P - P_{\text{ven}} = Q\,R, \qquad Q = f\times V_s,
\]
where $Q$ is the \omterm{thm:b2:heart:starling}{cardiac output} (\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} rate $f$ times \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume}
$V_s$), $R$ the \omterm{thm:b2:blood-pressure:equation}{total peripheral resistance} --- the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} of all
the organs in parallel --- and $P_{\text{ven}}$ the venous pressure,
near zero. At rest, $\qty{5}{L/min}\times\qty{1.08}{mmHg.s/mL}$ gives
\qty{90}{mmHg}. Every regulation of the pressure acts on one of three
quantities: the rate, the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} (through the filling and the
contractility of \cref{ch:b2:heart}) or the resistance (through the
arteriolar radius, to the fourth power). Because the organs are in
parallel, the flow to each is its share of the pressure divided by
its own resistance: dilating the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} of one organ raises its
flow without changing the others', provided the pressure is held ---
and holding the pressure is what the regulation is for.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Ohm's law for a fluid: the flow through a resistance is the pressure
drop across it divided by the resistance (Poiseuille,
\cref{ch:b2:blood-circulation}); for resistances in parallel the
flows add and $1/R = \sum 1/R_i$. The mean pressure over a beat is
approximately the diastolic plus a third of the \omterm{thm:b2:blood-circulation:windkessel}{pulse pressure}:
$80 + 40/3 = \qty{93}{mmHg}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Why the pressure must be regulated]\label{prop:b2:blood-pressure:why}
Too low, and the brain --- a metre above the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} in a standing
adult, which costs \qty{75}{mmHg} of hydrostatic head --- and the
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} muscle itself are underperfused: the brain fails within
seconds. Too high, and the vessels are damaged: the arterial wall
thickens and stiffens, the kidney's glomeruli scar, the retina
bleeds, and the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} labours against the pressure until it fails.
The pressure must also stay steady while the demands of the organs
change by an order of magnitude --- the gut after a meal, the muscles
in exercise, the skin in heat --- and while the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} volume itself
is lost in haemorrhage or sweat. Two systems do this: a nervous
reflex that acts within a beat on the rate, the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} and
the resistance, and hormonal systems that act over hours and days
on the resistance and, above all, on the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} volume through the
kidney.
\end{proposition}

\section{The fast loop: the baroreflex}

\begin{definition}[The baroreflex]\label{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}
\index{baroreceptor}\index{baroreflex}\index{vasomotor centre}\index{autonomic nervous system}
Stretch receptors in the walls of the carotid sinuses (at the fork
of each carotid \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{artery}, below the jaw) and of the aortic arch --- the
\emph{baroreceptors} --- fire at a rate that rises with the pressure
that distends them, faster for a rising pressure. Their nerves
reach the \emph{cardiovascular centres} of the medulla, which
control the two branches of the autonomic outflow: the vagus, whose
acetylcholine slows the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:conduction}{sinoatrial node}, and the sympathetic
nerves, whose noradrenaline speeds the node, strengthens the
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle}, constricts the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} (raising $R$) and constricts
the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} (squeezing stored \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} toward the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} and raising the
filling). A rise in pressure increases baroreceptor firing, which
excites the vagus and inhibits the sympathetic outflow: the rate
falls, the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} relax, the pressure comes down. A fall does
the reverse. The loop is a \emph{negative feedback} with a set
point near \qty{95}{mmHg}, a delay of about a second, and a gain
such that a disturbance is corrected to within a fifth of its size:
it is the reason you do not faint on standing.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Hering (1923) found that pressing on the carotid sinus slowed the
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} and lowered the pressure, and that cutting the sinus nerve
abolished the response. Perfusing an isolated carotid sinus at set
pressures while recording the animal's arterial pressure gave the
sigmoid curve of the reflex: the animal's pressure falls as the
sinus pressure is raised, steeply around \qty{100}{mmHg} and little
outside \qtyrange{60}{160}{mmHg}. Dogs with both sinus and aortic
nerves cut keep a normal mean pressure over days but a wildly
variable one from minute to minute --- the reflex is a buffer, not
the setter of the long-term level.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Gain of a negative feedback loop]\label{thm:b2:blood-pressure:gain}
\index{feedback gain}
If a disturbance would, without regulation, change the pressure by
$\Delta P_0$, and the loop responds to any deviation $\Delta P$ by a
correction of $-G\,\Delta P$ (the \emph{open-loop gain} $G$), the
deviation that remains once the loop has acted is
\[
\Delta P = \frac{\Delta P_0}{1 + G}.
\]
The \omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreflex} has $G \approx 4$: standing up, which would drop the
pressure at the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} by some \qty{40}{mmHg}, drops it by
\qty{8}{mmHg}; a haemorrhage that would halve the pressure lowers it
by a tenth --- until the loss exceeds what tighter vessels and a
faster \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} can hide. The reflex does not abolish a disturbance;
it divides it by five. And it \emph{resets}: held at a new pressure
for hours, the \omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreceptors} adapt and defend the new level, which
is why the reflex cannot cure hypertension and why the long-term
level is set elsewhere.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The final deviation is the disturbance plus the correction:
$\Delta P = \Delta P_0 - G\,\Delta P$, hence $\Delta P(1 + G) =
\Delta P_0$. With $G = 4$, $\Delta P = \Delta P_0/5$.
\end{proof}

\section{The slow loops: hormones and the kidney}

\begin{proposition}[Renin, angiotensin, aldosterone, and the volume]\label{prop:b2:blood-pressure:raas}
\index{renin--angiotensin system}\index{aldosterone}\index{antidiuretic hormone}\index{natriuretic peptide}
Over hours and days the pressure is set by the \emph{\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} volume},
and the volume by the kidney. When the pressure or the sodium
delivered to the kidney falls, or the sympathetic nerves fire, cells
of the kidney's \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} release the enzyme \emph{renin} into the
\omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}; renin cuts a \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma} protein into \emph{angiotensin I}, which
an enzyme of the lung capillaries converts to \emph{angiotensin II}
--- the most powerful vasoconstrictor in the body, raising $R$ within
minutes, and a hormone that makes the adrenal cortex secrete
\emph{aldosterone}, which makes the kidney retain sodium, and with
it water, over the following days. The pituitary's
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:blood-pressure:raas}{antidiuretic hormone}} (vasopressin), released when the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}
becomes concentrated or its volume falls, makes the kidney retain
water directly and also constricts vessels. And when the atria are
overstretched by too much volume they secrete \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:blood-pressure:raas}{natriuretic
peptide}}, which makes the kidney excrete sodium and water. The
kidney is thus the final arbiter: it can excrete or retain litres,
and a pressure that persists above the kidney's set point is
corrected, over days, by the loss of volume --- unless the kidney's
set point is itself raised, which is what most hypertension is.
(The kidney's own machinery is the subject of the Year 3 volume.)
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Goldblatt (1934) narrowed the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{artery} to one kidney of a dog: the
animal became hypertensive within days and stayed so; the
underperfused kidney was found to release renin, and removing it
cured the animal. Drugs that block the conversion of angiotensin
(ACE inhibitors) or its receptor lower the pressure of most
hypertensives, and a kidney transplanted from a hypertensive rat
strain makes a normal recipient hypertensive.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A haemorrhage]\label{ex:b2:blood-pressure:haemorrhage}
A donor gives \qty{500}{mL}, a tenth of the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}. Within seconds the
\omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreflex} constricts the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} and \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} and quickens the
\omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}: the pressure barely moves, the skin goes pale and cool
(its \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} closed), the pulse is faster. Within minutes the
lowered \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{capillary} pressure lets interstitial fluid move into the
vessels (the Starling balance of \cref{ch:b2:blood-circulation}),
restoring half the volume by the next hour; renin, angiotensin and
\omterm{prop:b2:blood-pressure:raas}{antidiuretic hormone} reduce the urine to a trickle, and thirst
follows; over days the kidney retains salt and water and the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma}
volume is back, and over weeks the marrow replaces the red cells. A
loss of \qty{2}{L} outruns all of this: the reflex is saturated,
the pressure collapses, the underperfused tissues release lactate
and the capillaries leak --- shock, from which only transfusion
returns the patient.
\end{example}

\section{Exercise: the system reorganised}

\begin{proposition}[Exercise]\label{prop:b2:blood-pressure:exercise}
\index{exercise physiology}\index{muscle blood flow}\index{cardiac output!exercise}
In hard exercise the skeletal muscles' consumption of oxygen rises
twentyfold, and their \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} flow from \qty{1}{L/min} to \qty{20}{L/min}.
Three things happen at once. Locally, the metabolites of the working
muscle --- $\mathrm{CO_2}$, lactate, adenosine, potassium, heat,
low oxygen --- relax its \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} (\emph{metabolic vasodilation})
and open the capillaries, cutting the muscle's resistance to a
tenth. Centrally, a command from the motor cortex to the medulla,
reinforced by signals from the muscles' own receptors, resets the
\omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreflex} to a higher set point and drives the sympathetic outflow:
rate to 180, contractility up, the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} of the gut, kidneys and
resting muscle constricted, the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} squeezed; the muscle pump and
deep breathing return the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} faster. The result is an output of
\qty{25}{L/min} with a total resistance a third of the resting value,
a systolic pressure of \qty{150}{mmHg} and a diastolic no higher
than at rest; four fifths of the flow goes to muscle, the brain's
share is unchanged in absolute terms, the gut's is halved, the
skin's rises to shed the heat. The reflex is not overridden but
re-tuned: it defends a higher pressure while the vessels reopen the
body.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Oxygen delivery and the Fick principle]\label{thm:b2:blood-pressure:fick}
\index{Fick principle}\index{VO2max@$\dot V_{\mathrm{O_2}\max}$}
The oxygen a body consumes per minute equals the \omterm{thm:b2:heart:starling}{cardiac output}
times the difference in oxygen content between arterial and mixed
venous \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}:
\[
\dot V_{\mathrm{O_2}} = Q\,(C_a - C_v).
\]
At rest, $\qty{5}{L/min}\times(200 - 150)\,\unit{mL/L} = \qty{250}{mL/min}$;
in maximal exercise, $\qty{25}{L/min}\times(200 - 40) =
\qty{4000}{mL/min}$, a sixteenfold rise from a fivefold rise in flow
and a threefold rise in extraction. This maximal rate,
$\dot V_{\mathrm{O_2}\max}$, is the standard measure of endurance
fitness, and its ceiling is the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}: the muscles could extract
still more and the lungs could load still more, but the output can
rise no further. Training raises the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} (a bigger, more
compliant \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{ventricle}, more \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} volume) and so the output --- the
athlete's resting rate of 45 is the sign of a \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} of
\qty{110}{mL} --- and adds capillaries and mitochondria to the muscle
so that it extracts more. Conversely, the Fick principle is how the
output itself is measured in a patient, from the oxygen consumed and
the two \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} samples.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each litre of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} passing through the tissues leaves $C_a - C_v$
millilitres of oxygen behind, and $Q$ litres pass per minute; at
steady state that is what the lungs take up and the body consumes.
Solving for $Q$ gives the measurement.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Standing up, fainting, and the astronaut]\label{ex:b2:blood-pressure:standing}
On standing, \qty{500}{mL} of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} shifts into the leg \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins}, the
venous return and the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} fall by a third, and the
pressure at the carotid sinus drops --- the \omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreflex} answers within
a beat with a faster \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} and tighter vessels, and the pressure at
the head is back within ten seconds. A soldier standing motionless
on a hot parade ground loses the muscle pump, pools \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} in
dilated skin \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins}, and faints: lying flat cures him at once. An
astronaut after months without gravity has lost a litre of \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}
volume (the kidney excreted what it read as excess when the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood}
pooled in the chest), and on landing the reflex, unpractised, cannot
keep him upright. And the giraffe, whose head is two metres above
its \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}, runs a mean pressure of \qty{200}{mmHg} and wears
compression stockings of skin --- the hydrostatics of
\cref{ch:b2:blood-circulation} set the requirements, and the reflexes
of this chapter meet them.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\section{Problem: Standing, Bleeding, Running}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- one circulation taken through
three challenges, with its pressure, flows, reflex corrections and
oxygen delivery computed, ending on the residual drop on standing,
the pressure after a haemorrhage, and the output and oxygen uptake
in exercise}]
\label{pb:b2:blood-pressure:1}
Data at rest: $Q = \qty{5}{L/min}$, rate 70, $\bar P = \qty{95}{mmHg}$,
$P_{\text{ven}} = 0$, \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{blood} \qty{5}{L}, \omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreflex} gain $G = 4$,
arterial oxygen \qty{200}{mL/L}, venous \qty{150}{mL/L}. Organ
resistances at rest (\unit{mmHg.s/mL}): brain 7.6, \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} 22.8, gut
and liver 4.1, kidneys 5.2, muscle 5.7, skin 19, other 28.5. \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{Blood}
density \qty{1060}{kg/m^3}; $\qty{1}{mmHg} = \qty{133}{Pa}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- At rest.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the \omterm{thm:b2:blood-pressure:equation}{total peripheral resistance} from the organ
        resistances in parallel, and check it against $\bar P/Q$.
  \item Compute the flow to each organ and the fraction of the output
        it receives.
  \item Compute the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} and the oxygen consumption.
  \item The brain weighs \qty{1.4}{kg} and the kidneys \qty{0.3}{kg}.
        Compute their flows per kilogram and comment.
  \item Why must the pressure, and not the flow, be the regulated
        variable?
  \item The \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} muscle receives \qty{250}{mL/min} and extracts
        \qty{70}{\%} of the oxygen; muscle at rest extracts
        \qty{25}{\%}. Compute the oxygen used by each per minute and
        explain why the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}'s only reserve is more flow.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Standing up.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item On standing, \qty{500}{mL} shift into the leg \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{veins} and the
        \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume} falls by \qty{30}{\%}. Compute the unregulated
        fall of $\bar P$ (resistance unchanged).
  \item Compute the residual fall after the \omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreflex}.
  \item The reflex achieves this by raising the rate to 85 and the
        resistance. Compute the new resistance needed.
  \item Compute the hydrostatic pressure difference between the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart}
        and a brain \qty{40}{cm} higher, and the brain's arterial
        pressure before and after the reflex.
  \item A patient on a drug that blocks the sympathetic nerves has
        $G = 1$. Compute her residual fall and the brain pressure,
        and say what she experiences.
  \item Explain why lying the fainted patient flat restores
        consciousness in seconds.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Bleeding.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A loss of \qty{1}{L} would, unregulated, lower $\bar P$ by
        \qty{45}{mmHg}. Compute the pressure after the reflex.
  \item The reflex constricts the skin and gut \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:vessels}{arterioles} so that
        their resistances double. Recompute the total resistance and
        the flows to skin, gut and brain at the pressure of
        question 13.
  \item Over the next hour, \qty{500}{mL} of interstitial fluid
        enter the capillaries. Explain the mechanism with the
        \omterm{prop:b2:blood-circulation:exchange}{Starling forces}, and what happens to the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{haematocrit}.
  \item Over three days the kidney restores the \omterm{def:b2:blood-circulation:blood}{plasma} volume.
        Name the two hormones responsible and their triggers.
  \item How long does the marrow take to replace the red cells at
        \num{2.5} million a second (\num{5e12} per litre)?
  \item A loss of \qty{2.5}{L} saturates the reflex. Explain, with the
        gain and the reflex curve, why the pressure then collapses.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Running.}
In hard exercise the muscle resistance falls to \qty{0.33}{mmHg.s/mL},
skin to 5, gut and kidneys double, brain and \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} resistances fall
so that the brain keeps its flow and the \omterm{def:b2:heart:anatomy}{heart} muscle quadruples its
flow at the new pressure; $\bar P$ rises to \qty{110}{mmHg}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the flow to muscle and to skin.
  \item Compute the total resistance and the \omterm{thm:b2:heart:starling}{cardiac output}.
  \item At a rate of 180, compute the \omterm{prop:b2:heart:cycle}{stroke volume}.
  \item The venous oxygen falls to \qty{40}{mL/L}. Compute the oxygen
        uptake and the factor by which it exceeds rest.
  \item Attribute that factor to flow and to extraction.
  \item Explain how the \omterm{def:b2:blood-pressure:baroreflex}{baroreflex} can permit a pressure of
        \qty{110}{mmHg} instead of correcting it.
  \item State the result: the residual drop on standing, the
        pressure after the \qty{1}{L} haemorrhage, and the output
        and oxygen uptake in exercise.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
