\chapter{Plant Gas Exchange and Sap Transport}\label{ch:b1:plant-transport}

At the top of a sequoia a hundred metres above the ground, water is
being pulled out of the soil by the evaporation from \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}, up a
column of dead \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} no wider than a hair, under a tension that would
snap a steel wire of the same cross-section. There is no pump; the
water is hauled by the same \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds} that make it cling to
itself. Meanwhile, in a second set of tubes beside the first, the
sugar those \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} made is flowing the other way, pushed by pressure
the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} themselves generate. A plant's two plumbing systems, the
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} and the \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem}, are driven by physics that the plant sets up and
then \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} to run. This chapter describes the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} through which
a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} trades water for carbon dioxide, the cohesion--tension
mechanism that lifts sap, the vessels that carry it, the \omterm{thm:b1:plant-transport:pressureflow}{pressure-flow
mechanism} that moves sugar, and the compromise between drinking and
eating that governs the whole.

\section{Stomata: the gate}

\begin{definition}[Stoma, guard cells]\label{def:b1:plant-transport:stoma}
\index{stoma}\index{guard cell}
A \emph{stoma} is a pore in the epidermis of a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} (or a green stem)
bounded by two \emph{guard \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}}, kidney-shaped \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} whose walls are
thickened on the side facing the pore and whose \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{cellulose} microfibrils
run around them like hoops. When the guard \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} take up water and
swell, the hoops prevent them from widening and force them to bow
apart: the pore opens. When they lose water, it closes. A \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} carries
a few hundred \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} per square millimetre, mostly on its lower
surface; open, their pores are one or two percent of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s area
and nearly all of its gas exchange passes through them.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[How a stoma opens, and when]\label{prop:b1:plant-transport:opening}
\index{abscisic acid}
The \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:stoma}{guard cells} open the pore by pumping protons out (\cref{ch:b1:plant-water-minerals}),
which drives potassium and chloride in and makes malate inside; the
solutes lower their \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential}, water follows from the
neighbouring \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, and their \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor} rises by a megapascal. Reversing
the ion fluxes closes the pore in minutes. The signals: \emph{light}
(blue light directly on the \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:stoma}{guard cells}, and the fall of internal
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ as photosynthesis starts) opens; \emph{high internal
$\mathrm{CO_2}$} closes; \emph{water stress} closes, through the
hormone \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:plant-transport:opening}{abscisic acid}}, made in wilting \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}, which
triggers ion loss from the \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:stoma}{guard cells} within minutes; dry air
closes. The \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} opens its gate when carbon is worth the water and
shuts it when water is scarce --- and most plants do so in a daily
rhythm, opening at dawn and closing at dusk, that persists for days in
constant light.
\end{proposition}

\section{Lifting water: cohesion and tension}

\begin{theorem}[The cohesion--tension mechanism]\label{thm:b1:plant-transport:cohesiontension}
\index{cohesion--tension theory}\index{transpiration}
Water rises in the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} because it is \emph{pulled} from above, not
pushed from below. Evaporation from the wet walls of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s
mesophyll \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} into the air spaces (\emph{transpiration}) draws the
water in the wall's fine pores into curved menisci, whose surface
tension puts the water behind them under tension; the tension is
transmitted, through the continuous columns of water in the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}
held together by cohesion (the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds} of
\cref{ch:b1:water-small-molecules}), down the stem and into the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots},
and lifts water from the soil. To hold a column of height $h$ against
gravity requires a tension $\rho g h$ --- \qty{1}{MPa} per hundred
metres --- plus what is needed to overcome the friction of flow, about
as much again; the columns of a tall tree are at \qtyrange{-2}{-3}{MPa},
well within the tensile strength of water in a fine tube.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Dixon and Joly (1894) showed that water in a sealed glass tube
withstands tensions of many megapascals without breaking, and that a
transpiring leafy branch, sealed to a tube of water dipping into
mercury, lifts the mercury higher than atmospheric pressure could.
Scholander's \emph{pressure chamber} (1965) measures the tension
directly: a cut \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} is sealed into a chamber with its stalk
protruding, and the gas pressure that just forces sap back to the cut
surface equals the tension the sap was under --- \qtyrange{0.5}{1}{MPa}
in a well-watered crop at noon, \qtyrange{2}{4}{MPa} in a desert shrub
or the crown of a tall tree. A dendrometer shows the trunk of a tree
shrinking by day, as the tension squeezes the vessels, and swelling by
night; a thermometer in the wood shows sap flowing fastest when
transpiration is highest, and stopping at night. No living \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} is
required: a stem killed with poison or heat still conducts.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Xylem conduits, cavitation]\label{def:b1:plant-transport:xylem}
\index{xylem vessel}\index{cavitation}
Water travels in the \emph{\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{tracheids}} and \emph{vessels} of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}
(\cref{ch:b1:flowering-plant-organization}): dead \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} emptied of
their contents, their lignified walls thickened in rings and spirals
that keep them from collapsing under the tension inside, joined end to
end through perforations (vessels) or through pits in their side
walls (\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{tracheids}). A vessel may be \qtyrange{20}{300}{\micro m} wide
and centimetres to metres long: wide tubes carry far more (flow
through a tube rises as the fourth power of its radius) but are more
vulnerable to \emph{cavitation} --- the sudden formation of a gas
bubble in water under tension, which empties the conduit and puts it
out of service. Pits between conduits stop bubbles spreading; freezing
and drought both cause cavitation, and a tree's vessels are partly
refilled by \omterm{prop:b1:plant-water-minerals:rootpressure}{root pressure} in spring or replaced by new wood each year.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The top of the tree]\label{ex:b1:plant-transport:top}
At \qty{100}{m} the column's weight alone demands \qty{1}{MPa} of
tension; friction in the vessels adds as much again by day, so the
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} at the crown is near \qty{-2}{MPa} and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}, to draw
water from it, lower than that: their \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} must close earlier in
the day and their photosynthesis is slower than a low branch's, and
the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} at the top are small and thick. The height of the tallest
trees, some \qty{120}{m}, is probably set by this: above it, the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}
cannot keep the tension without cavitating on a dry afternoon.
\end{example}

\section{Moving sugar: pressure flow}

\begin{definition}[Phloem, sieve tubes, source and sink]\label{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}
\index{phloem}\index{sieve tube}\index{source}\index{sink}
Sugar travels in the \emph{phloem}, in \emph{\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{sieve tubes}}: files of
living \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} that have lost their nuclei and most \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:prokeuk}{organelles} but keep
their membranes, joined end to end through perforated \emph{sieve
plates}, each \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} nursed by a \emph{companion \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}} that supplies its
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} and \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP}. The sap is \qtyrange{10}{30}{\%} sucrose, with \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino
acids}, ions and signalling molecules, and it flows from
\emph{sources} --- photosynthesising \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}, or storage \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} being
emptied --- to \emph{sinks} --- growing tips, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}, fruits, seeds,
storage \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} being filled --- at \qtyrange{0.5}{1}{m/h}, in any
direction, and in different directions in different tubes.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Pressure flow]\label{thm:b1:plant-transport:pressureflow}
\index{pressure-flow mechanism}
\omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{Phloem} sap moves by bulk flow driven by a difference of hydrostatic
pressure between source and sink (M\"unch, 1930). At the source,
companion \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} \emph{load} sucrose into the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{sieve tubes} against its
gradient (by proton symport, from the \omterm{def:b1:plant-water-minerals:pathways}{apoplast}, or through
\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{plasmodesmata}); the tube's \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} falls, water enters from
the neighbouring \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}, and the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor} rises to \qty{1}{MPa} or more.
At the sink, sucrose is \emph{unloaded} and consumed or stored, the
\omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} rises, water \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}, and the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor} is low. Between
them the sap flows down the pressure gradient through the sieve
plates, carrying whatever is dissolved in it. The plant spends energy
only at the two ends; the flow itself is passive.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
An aphid feeding on a stem inserts its stylet into a single \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{sieve
tube}; cut the aphid away and the stylet, left in place, exudes sap for
hours under the tube's own pressure --- pure \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem} sap, whose sucrose
concentration and rate of flow can be measured, and whose pressure,
measured with a manometer on the stylet, is about \qty{1}{MPa} near a
source and lower toward the sink. Labelled $\mathrm{^{14}CO_2}$ fed to
a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} appears in the stem's \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem} within minutes and moves at the
speed the stylet flow predicts; chilling a length of stem, which
slows the living \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} but barely affects a pressure-driven flow,
slows transport only slightly; poisoning the source \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s loading
stops it.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Reading a translocation experiment]\label{met:b1:plant-transport:translocation}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Feed a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} labelled $\mathrm{CO_2}$; sample the stem above
        and below at intervals: the label's position against time
        gives the direction and speed of flow.
  \item Girdle the stem (remove a ring of bark, which carries the
        \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem}, leaving the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}): sugar accumulates above the ring
        and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots} starve below --- the \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem}, not the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem},
        carries it; the tree dies in a season.
  \item Collect sap from severed aphid stylets at two heights: the
        drop in sucrose and in pressure between them is the gradient
        that drives the flow.
  \item Change the sinks: remove the fruits, and the label goes to the
        \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{roots}; shade the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}, and it becomes a sink itself. The
        pattern of flow is the pattern of demand.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[A potato in two seasons]\label{ex:b1:plant-transport:potato}
In summer the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} are sources and the tubers underground the
sinks: sucrose flows down, is unloaded, converted to \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch}, and the
tubers swell. In spring the tuber sprouts: its \omterm{def:b1:carbohydrates:polysaccharide}{starch} is turned back
to sucrose, loaded into the \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem}, and flows up to the growing shoot
--- the same tube, the opposite direction, because the source and the
sink have changed places.
\end{example}

\section{The compromise}

\begin{proposition}[Water-use efficiency]\label{prop:b1:plant-transport:wue}
\index{water-use efficiency}
Every \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:stoma}{stoma} that admits carbon dioxide loses water, and the exchange
is unequal: the inside of the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} is saturated with vapour and the
air outside is dry, while $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is at \qty{0.04}{\%} outside
and lower inside, so the outward gradient of water is hundreds of
times the inward gradient of carbon dioxide. A C3 \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} loses
\qtyrange{200}{500}{} molecules of water per $\mathrm{CO_2}$ fixed; a
C4 \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}, concentrating $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and keeping its \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata}
narrower, \qtyrange{100}{200}{}; a \omterm{def:b1:photosynthesis:c4cam}{CAM plant}, opening only at night,
\qtyrange{20}{50}{} (\cref{ch:b1:photosynthesis}). The
\emph{\omterm{prop:b1:plant-transport:wue}{water-use efficiency}} is the carbon gained per water lost, and
its improvement --- by closing at midday, by sunken \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} and thick
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{cuticles}, by the C4 and CAM pathways --- is the plant's side of the
bargain with a dry atmosphere.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The bargain in numbers]\label{ex:b1:plant-transport:bargain}
A maize plant transpiring a litre and a half a day fixes some
\qty{10}{g} of carbon dioxide: \qty{80}{mol} of water for
\qty{0.23}{mol} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$, three hundred and fifty to one. A
wheat field in a season transpires five hundred tonnes of water per
hectare to make eight tonnes of grain; the water is the price of the
carbon, and in most of the world's fields it is the price that limits
the harvest.
\end{example}

does the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}'s \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} reach its minimum, and why do the

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:plant-transport:4}
Define source and sink, and say what happens at each in the
\omterm{thm:b1:plant-transport:pressureflow}{pressure-flow mechanism}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:plant-transport:5}
Compute the tension needed to hold a column of water \qty{30}{m} high

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:plant-transport:6}
Flow through a tube scales as $r^4$. Compare the flow through one
vessel of \qty{100}{\micro m} radius with that through the number of
\qty{10}{\micro m} \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{tracheids} of the same total cross-section. Why do

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:plant-transport:7}
\omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{Phloem} sap at \qty{20}{\%} sucrose (\qty{0.6}{mol/L}) has a \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{solute

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:plant-transport:8}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:plant-transport:9}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:plant-transport:10}

\section{Problem: The Sequoia}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a hundred-metre tree on a summer
day: the tension at its crown, the sap's speed, the water it moves,
the sugar it sends down and the carbon it buys, ending on the xylem
tension at the crown}]
\label{pb:b1:plant-transport:1}
A sequoia is \qty{100}{m} tall with a crown of \qty{2000}{m^2} of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf}.
On a summer day it transpires \qty{600}{L} of water in \qty{12}{h}
through sapwood of cross-section \qty{0.5}{m^2}, of which \qty{20}{\%}
is conducting lumen. Water: $\rho = \qty{1000}{kg/m^3}$, $g =
\qty{9.8}{m/s^2}$, \qty{18}{g/mol}. Friction along the trunk costs as
much tension as gravity. Soil \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} \qty{-0.1}{MPa}. The
crown fixes \qty{4}{\micro mol} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ per square metre of
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} per second for \qty{12}{h}; sucrose is \qty{342}{g/mol} and
carries twelve carbons; \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem} sap is \qty{15}{\%} sucrose by mass
(density \qty{1.06}{g/mL}) and flows at \qty{0.6}{m/h}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Tension.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the tension needed to support the column against
        gravity.
  \item Add the friction: what is the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} tension at the crown?
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} at the crown, taking
        $\Psi_s = 0$ for the sap.
  \item What must the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}' \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} be at the crown for
        water to enter them? If their $\Psi_s = \qty{-2.5}{MPa}$, what
        \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor} do they have?
  \item Compare with a \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} at \qty{10}{m} on the same tree ($\Psi_s =
        \qty{-1.5}{MPa}$).
  \item Water in fine tubes withstands about \qty{-30}{MPa}; why does
        the tree not simply grow to \qty{1000}{m}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Flow.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the transpiration rate in litres per hour and in
        cubic metres per second.
  \item Compute the conducting cross-section of the sapwood.
  \item Compute the mean speed of the sap.
  \item Compute the mass of water held in the conducting lumen of the
        trunk (\qty{100}{m} of it).
  \item How long does a water molecule take to travel from \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} to
        crown at that speed?
  \item Compute the transpiration per square metre of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaf} per hour,
        in grams, and per second in millimoles.
  \item The sap flows down a potential gradient from \qty{-0.1}{} at
        the soil to the crown's value of question 3 over \qty{100}{m}.
        What is the gradient in megapascals per metre? How does it
        compare with the gravitational part alone?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Sugar.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ fixed by the crown per day, in
        moles.
  \item Compute the sucrose it corresponds to, in moles and in
        kilograms.
  \item Half is respired in the crown; the rest is sent down the
        \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem}. Compute the mass of sucrose translocated per day and
        the mass of sap that carries it.
  \item Compute the volume of sap and, at \qty{0.6}{m/h}, the
        cross-section of \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{sieve tubes} needed to carry it in \qty{24}{h}.
  \item Compute the time for sucrose to travel from crown to \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root}.
  \item Compute the water-use ratio: moles of water transpired per
        mole of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ fixed.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Compromises.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem} sap's \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:osmosis}{water potential} at the crown, with
        $\Psi_s = \qty{-1.2}{MPa}$ and \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor} \qty{1.0}{MPa}: compute it
        and compare with the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}'s there. Which way does water move
        between the two, and is that consistent with loading?
  \item At the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} the \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem} sap is \qty{5}{\%} sucrose ($\Psi_s =
        \qty{-0.4}{MPa}$) and the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} at \qty{-0.3}{MPa}. What \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor}
        makes the \omterm{def:b1:plant-transport:phloem}{phloem}'s $\Psi$ equal to the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem}'s? What pressure
        difference between crown and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{root} drives the flow?
  \item The \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{stomata} close at midday and transpiration halves.
        Recompute the friction tension and the crown \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} potential.
        What does the tree gain and lose?
  \item In a dry year the soil falls to \qty{-1.0}{MPa}. Recompute the
        crown potential with full transpiration. Can the crown \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}
        ($\Psi_s = \qty{-2.5}{MPa}$) keep any \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor}? What must the
        tree do?
  \item Explain why the tallest \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} are the smallest and thickest.
  \item State the result: the \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{xylem} tension at the crown of a
        \qty{100}{m} tree on a summer day, and the sap speed that goes
        with it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
