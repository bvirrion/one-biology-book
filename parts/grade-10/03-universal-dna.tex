\chapter{DNA: A Universal Genetic Molecule}\label{ch:g10:universal-dna}

In a darkened laboratory, under a blue lamp, one of two white mice glows
green from nose to tail. It carries, in every one of its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, a single
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} taken from a jellyfish that lights up in the Pacific --- and the
mouse's \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} read that jellyfish \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} exactly as the jellyfish's \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
do. The experiment works because every living thing writes its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} in
the same molecule, with the same four letters, and reads them by the
same rules. This chapter is about that molecule, \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}: how it is built,
what its structure explains, and what the glowing mouse proves.

\section{The genetic material}

\begin{definition}[Genetic information]\label{def:g10:universal-dna:information}
The \emph{genetic information}\index{genetic information} of a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} is
the set of instructions, transmitted from a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} to its daughter \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
and from parents to offspring, that determines the traits of the
organism and the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} can make. In every living thing it
is carried by molecules of \emph{DNA}\index{DNA} (deoxyribonucleic
acid), packed with \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} into the \emph{chromosomes}\index{chromosome}
--- in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus} of a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:prokaryote}{eukaryotic cell}, free in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cytoplasm} of a
bacterium.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[DNA is the molecule of heredity]\label{prop:g10:universal-dna:material}
The hereditary material of a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} is its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, not its \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} or any
other constituent: transferring purified \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} from one strain of
bacteria to another transfers the first strain's traits.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Griffith (1928) found that harmless pneumonia bacteria, mixed with
killed \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} of a deadly strain, became deadly and stayed so through
generations: something from the dead \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} had "transformed" them.
Avery, MacLeod and McCarty (1944) separated the extract of the dead
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} into its families of molecules and tested each one: only the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}
fraction transformed; treating the extract with an enzyme that
destroys \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} abolished the effect, while enzymes destroying \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} or
RNA did not. The transforming substance, and hence the carrier of the
heritable trait, was \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Where the DNA is]\label{ex:g10:universal-dna:where}
A dye specific for \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} stains the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus} of a cheek \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} and nothing
else in the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cytoplasm}; in a dividing \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} it stains the compact
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}. A bacterium takes up the dye in a central region without
an envelope. In both, the amount of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} doubles before each division
and is halved between the two daughter \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} --- the behaviour
expected of information that must be copied and shared.
\end{example}

\section{The structure of DNA}

\begin{definition}[Nucleotide]\label{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is a chain of \emph{nucleotides}\index{nucleotide}. Each
nucleotide is made of three parts: a phosphate group, a sugar
(deoxyribose) and one of four nitrogen-containing
\emph{bases}\index{base (DNA)}: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G)
and cytosine (C). Sugars and phosphates alternate to form the backbone
of the chain; the bases stick out from it, one per nucleotide. The
four nucleotides differ only by their base, so a strand of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is
described by the sequence of its bases, written as a string of
letters: \texttt{ATGGCTTAC\dots}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The double helix]\label{prop:g10:universal-dna:helix}
A \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecule is made of two strands wound around each other in a
right-handed \emph{double helix}\index{double helix} about \qty{2}{nm}
wide, with one turn every ten base pairs (\qty{3.4}{nm}). The strands
run in opposite directions and face each other by their bases, which
pair according to a strict rule of
\emph{complementarity}\index{complementarity}: A pairs only with T, G
only with C. The sequence of one strand therefore fixes the sequence
of the other.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Chargaff (1950) measured the base composition of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} from many species
and found, in every one, as much A as T and as much G as C, while the
proportion of A+T to G+C varied from species to species --- a pairing
rule, not a chemical accident. Franklin's X-ray photographs of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}
fibres (1952) showed the signature of a helix of \qty{2}{nm} diameter
and \qty{3.4}{nm} pitch. Watson and Crick (1953) built the only model
that fits both: two backbones outside, paired bases inside, A--T and
G--C pairs having the same width so that the helix is regular.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Chargaff's rule in numbers]\label{ex:g10:universal-dna:chargaff}
Human \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}: A 30.9\%, T 29.4\%, G 19.9\%, C 19.8\% --- A$\,\approx\,$T
and G$\,\approx\,$C to within the precision of the measurement, and
A+T $= 60\%$. \emph{E.~coli}: A 24.7\%, T 23.6\%, G 26.0\%, C 25.7\%,
A+T $= 48\%$. Yeast: A+T $= 64\%$. If a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} sample contains 32\% of A,
\omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:helix}{complementarity} predicts 32\% of T and $(100 - 64)/2 = 18\%$ each of G
and C.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Writing the complementary strand]\label{met:g10:universal-dna:complement}
Given one strand, write under each base its partner (A$\leftrightarrow$T,
G$\leftrightarrow$C), then read the result in the opposite direction
if the convention of the exercise requires it. For
\texttt{5'-ATGGCTTAC-3'} the partner strand, read in its own
direction, is \texttt{5'-GTAAGCCAT-3'}. Counting: a strand with
$n_\mathrm{A}$ adenines faces a strand with $n_\mathrm{A}$ thymines,
so in the whole molecule $n_\mathrm{A} = n_\mathrm{T}$ and
$n_\mathrm{G} = n_\mathrm{C}$.
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[What the structure explains]\label{prop:g10:universal-dna:explains}
Two properties of the genetic material follow from the structure.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Information}: the order of the bases along a strand is
        not constrained by the chemistry --- any sequence is possible
        --- so the sequence can carry a message, as the order of
        letters carries a text. A human \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} holds $\num{3.2e9}$ base
        pairs in each set of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}.
  \item \emph{Copying}: since each strand determines the other, the
        molecule can be duplicated by separating the strands and
        building a new partner on each. The mechanism is the subject of
        \cref{ch:g11:dna-replication}.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Genes, alleles, chromosomes}

\begin{definition}[Gene and allele]\label{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}
A \emph{gene}\index{gene} is a segment of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecule --- typically
some thousands of base pairs --- whose sequence carries the
instructions for making one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} (or, sometimes, one working
molecule of RNA). It occupies a fixed position on a given \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome}.
The versions of a gene that differ by a few bases are its
\emph{alleles}\index{allele}: same position, same function, slightly
different sequence, hence often a slightly different \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Numbers]\label{ex:g10:universal-dna:numbers}
The human genome --- one complete set of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} --- carries
$\num{3.2e9}$ base pairs and about $\num{20000}$ \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}; \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} occupy
only a few per cent of the sequence. A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} of \emph{E.~coli} carries
one circular \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecule of $\num{4.6e6}$ base pairs and about 4300
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}. Two unrelated humans differ at roughly one base in a thousand:
some three million positions, most of them outside \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} and of no
consequence, a few of them \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} that change a trait.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Chromosomes]\label{prop:g10:universal-dna:chromosome}
A \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome} is one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecule, wound around packing \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} and
coiled on itself. Stretched out, the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} of one human \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosome} would
be from \qty{1.5}{cm} to \qty{8.5}{cm} long; the 46 \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes} of a
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} together hold about \qty{2}{m} of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} in a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus}
\qty{6}{\micro m} across. \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{Chromosomes} are visible as separate bodies
only during \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} division, when they are coiled most tightly; between
divisions they are unwound and fill the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus} as a tangle.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{One molecule for all living things}

\begin{proposition}[Universality of DNA]\label{prop:g10:universal-dna:universal}
All living things --- bacteria, fungi, plants, animals --- carry their
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{genetic information} as \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} built from the same four \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides}, and
read a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}'s sequence by the same rules. Consequently a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} taken
from one species and inserted into the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} of another is read by the
recipient's \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, which make the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} it codes for:
\emph{transgenesis}\index{transgenesis} is possible, and the
transgenic organism passes the foreign \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} to its descendants like
any other.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} for the green fluorescent \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} (GFP) of the jellyfish
\emph{Aequorea victoria} has been inserted into bacteria, yeast, plants,
fish, mice and rabbits: every one of them makes the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} and glows
green under blue light, and the trait is inherited. The human \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} for
insulin, inserted into \emph{E.~coli} in 1978, makes the bacterium
produce human insulin, which has been the source of the hormone for
diabetics ever since. No case is known of a species whose \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} are
written in another chemistry.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Uses of transgenesis]\label{ex:g10:universal-dna:uses}
Human insulin and growth hormone from bacteria; a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} for resistance
to an insect pest inserted into maize or cotton; rice carrying \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}
for making a precursor of vitamin A; the GFP \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} attached to another
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} so that the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} where that \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is active light up under the
microscope --- the last use, a research tool, is the commonest of all.
Each use raises its own questions of safety and of consequences for
the environment; each rests on the same fact, the universality of the
genetic molecule.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Unity and diversity, at the molecular level]\label{rem:g10:universal-dna:unity}
The universality of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is the molecular face of the unity of life met
in \cref{ch:g10:chemistry-of-life}: one chemistry, one information
molecule, one reading system for every organism on Earth, which is
what a common origin predicts (\cref{ch:g10:common-ancestry}). The
diversity of life is written in the same molecule too, as differences
of sequence: between two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} a few bases, between two species a
few per cent of the genome, between a bacterium and a human the whole
organisation of the text.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Jellyfish Gene}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- one gene from a jellyfish followed
into a mouse: its length, its letters, why the mouse can read it, and
the two metres of DNA that carry it in every cell}]
\label{pb:g10:universal-dna:1}
The green fluorescent \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} of the jellyfish \emph{Aequorea victoria}
is a chain of 238 \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acids}. Its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, as used in the laboratory, is
a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} segment of 720 base pairs. Take \qty{0.34}{nm} per base pair, ten
base pairs per turn, and a mouse genome of $\num{2.7e9}$ base pairs per
set of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{chromosomes}, two sets per \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The molecule.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the length of the GFP \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} in nanometres, and the
        number of turns of helix it makes.
  \item One strand of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} begins \texttt{ATGAGTAAAGGAGAAGAAC}.
        Write the complementary strand.
  \item In the whole \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, adenine represents 27\% of the bases. Give
        the percentages of the other three bases.
  \item The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} has 720 base pairs and codes for 238 \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acids}.
        Compute the ratio, and propose what it suggests about how many
        bases specify one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid} (a hypothesis that
        \cref{ch:g11:gene-expression} will confirm).
  \item How many different sequences of 720 bases are possible in
        principle? Write the answer as a power of 4, then explain in
        one sentence why the sequence can be called information.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Why the mouse can read it.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item State the property of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} that makes it possible for a mouse
        \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} to use a jellyfish \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}.
  \item The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is inserted into a fertilised egg rather than into
        the skin of an adult mouse. Using the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} theory, explain what
        difference this makes to where the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} ends up.
  \item The adult mouse glows in every tissue. What does this show
        about the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} content of its different \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} types?
  \item The glowing mouse is mated with an ordinary mouse. Predict,
        with a reason, whether some of the offspring glow.
  \item A control mouse is injected with purified GFP \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} instead
        of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}: it glows faintly for a few days, then stops.
        Explain the difference between the two experiments.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The evidence behind the story.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item In Avery's experiment, name the fraction that transformed the
        bacteria and the treatment that abolished transformation.
  \item Explain why the transformation experiment is a natural version
        of \omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:universal}{transgenesis}.
  \item Chargaff found A$\,=\,$T and G$\,=\,$C in every species but
        A+T varying from 25\% to 75\%. Which of the two observations
        supports base pairing, and which shows that the sequence is
        free?
  \item Franklin's photographs gave the diameter of the helix as
        \qty{2}{nm}. Explain why pairing a large base with a small one
        (A with T, G with C) rather than two large ones makes the
        helix regular.
  \item Why did the discovery of the structure immediately suggest how
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is copied?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Two metres in a \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the total length of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} in one mouse \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}.
  \item The \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{nucleus} is \qty{6}{\micro m} across. By what factor must
        the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} be shortened by coiling to fit?
  \item What fraction of the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} is the GFP \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}?
  \item A mouse has about $\num{2e4}$ \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} averaging $\num{3e4}$ base
        pairs including their non-coding parts. What fraction of the
        genome do \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} occupy?
  \item State the result in one sentence: how much \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} does a mouse
        \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} carry, and what single fact about that \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} allows one
        jellyfish \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} among its thousands to be read?
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
