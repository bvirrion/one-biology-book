\chapter{Decomposers and the Soil}\label{ch:g6:decomposers-and-soil}

Every autumn, the wood drops a thick carpet of \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaves} ---
tonnes of them per hectare. Yet walk the same wood each
spring and you wade through no rising sea of \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf} litter: the
carpet is thin again, year after year. Somebody is eating the
forest's dead. This chapter goes down into the \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil} to meet
the eaters --- and to close, at last, the circle of matter.

\section{The soil is alive}

\begin{definition}[Soil]\label{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}
\emph{Soil}\index{soil} is the earth's living \omterm{def:g1:the-five-senses:sense}{skin}: a mixture
of \emph{mineral} grains (ground rock, from sand to clay),
\emph{organic} remains in every stage of decay, water, air
--- and an enormous population of \omterm{def:g6:exploring-our-environment:components}{living things}. Its dark
upper layer, richest in decayed matter, is the
\emph{humus}\index{humus} layer that gardeners prize.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[A handful, censused]\label{ex:g6:decomposers-and-soil:handful}
One handful of woodland \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil}, sorted on a white tray:
mineral grains; \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{root} threads; \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{half-leaves}, \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf} \omterm{def:g3:bones-and-muscles:skeleton}{skeletons}
and black crumbs --- \omterm{def:g6:plants-are-producers:matter}{organic matter} on its way down; and the
census --- an earthworm, two woodlice, a millipede, tiny
white springtails, thread-fine \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{fungus} felts. And the tray
shows only the visible: a single gram of \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil} holds more
\omterm{def:g6:exploring-our-environment:components}{living things} too small to see than there are people in a
great city.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Decomposers]\label{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}
The \emph{decomposers}\index{decomposer} are the \omterm{def:g6:exploring-our-environment:components}{living
things} that feed on \emph{dead \omterm{def:g6:plants-are-producers:matter}{organic matter}} --- fallen
\omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaves}, dead wood, droppings, corpses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the visible crew: earthworms, woodlice, millipedes,
        springtails, fly larvae, dung beetles;
  \item the \emph{fungi}\index{fungus}: molds and mushrooms,
        whose feeding body is a felt of white threads spread
        through \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil} and dead wood --- the mushroom of
        \cref{exo:g1:living-or-not:10} is only its \omterm{prop:g3:flowers-fruits-seeds:transform}{fruit};
  \item and, finishing every job, \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil} life too small to
        see --- the final dismantlers.
\end{itemize}
They are \cref{def:g5:food-webs:roles}'s shadow-workers,
promised their own chapter: this is it.
\end{definition}

\section{Decomposition: organic back to mineral}

\begin{proposition}[What decomposition does]\label{prop:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposition}
\emph{Decomposition}\index{decomposition} is the feeding of
the \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposers}, and its result is the reverse of the
\omterm{def:g5:food-webs:roles}{producers}' trade: dead \emph{organic} matter is dismantled,
stage by stage and eater by eater, until its substance is
returned to the \emph{mineral} world --- crumbly \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{humus}, then
dissolved \omterm{prop:g4:what-plants-need:needs}{mineral salts} in the \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil}'s water, and gas
returned to the air. The autumn carpet becomes, by spring,
exactly what \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{roots} drink
(\cref{prop:g4:what-plants-need:needs}).
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[The leaf's last year]\label{ex:g6:decomposers-and-soil:leaf}
Follow one oak \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf} down. November: fallen, whole. Winter:
softened, mined by \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{fungi}'s threads, nibbled to a lace
\omterm{def:g3:bones-and-muscles:skeleton}{skeleton} by springtails and woodlice. Spring: dragged
underground by an earthworm, eaten, and cast out as
worm-worked crumbs. Summer: its last fragments are black
\omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{humus}, and its minerals ride the \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil}'s water --- into a
\omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{root}, perhaps the oak's own. Eaten four times, the \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf} has
become food for its own tree.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The circle closes]\label{rem:g6:decomposers-and-soil:circle}
With the \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposers} in place, the matter story of this year
closes into a wheel: \omterm{def:g5:food-webs:roles}{producers} build organic from mineral
(\cref{ch:g6:plants-are-producers}); \omterm{def:g5:food-webs:roles}{consumers} pass it along
(\cref{ch:g6:matter-from-food}); \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposers} return it to
mineral, ready for the \omterm{def:g5:food-webs:roles}{producers} again. Matter is not used
up --- it goes round. The atoms of this page's paper have
been \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf}, \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{humus}, \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{root} and \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf} again more times than can be
counted.
\end{remark}

\section{Working with the decomposers}

\begin{example}[Compost, explained at last]\label{ex:g6:decomposers-and-soil:compost}
The compost bin of \cref{ex:g2:caring-for-nature:compost}
now reads as an appliance: a \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposer} workshop stocked
with peelings. Worms, woodlice and the unseen crews
dismantle the kitchen's organic waste to dark \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{humus}; the
gardener spreads it, and its minerals feed the beds
(\cref{rem:g4:what-plants-need:soil}). The heap even runs
warm --- the \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposers}' own burning
(\cref{prop:g6:matter-from-food:losses}) heats their
workshop.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[What speeds and slows the work]\label{prop:g6:decomposers-and-soil:speed}
\omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{Decomposers} are \omterm{def:g6:exploring-our-environment:components}{living things} with \omterm{def:g6:exploring-our-environment:conditions}{conditions}
(\cref{prop:g6:exploring-our-environment:decide}): they work
fastest \emph{warm}, \emph{damp} and \emph{aired}, and
almost stop when cold, dry, or drowned airless. Hence
autumn's slow winter carpet and spring's quick clearing;
hence too the gardener's compost recipe --- moist, turned
for air, never a sodden sealed mass.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Reading soil health]\label{met:g6:decomposers-and-soil:read}
To judge a \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil} the naturalist's way:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item dig a hand's depth and look: dark crumbly \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{humus}, or
        pale lifeless dust?
  \item census a handful on a white tray --- worms,
        woodlice, springtails: a rich crew means the cycle
        is turning;
  \item check the surface: does dead matter disappear over
        the seasons, or pile up unrotted?
  \item treat the crew as livestock: feed it (dug-in
        remains, compost, mulch), keep it damp, spare it
        poisons.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[The earthworm, gardener of gardeners]\label{ex:g6:decomposers-and-soil:worm}
The earthworm does double duty: \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{decomposer} --- dragging
\omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaves} down, eating decayed matter --- and \emph{plougher},
its burrows airing the \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil} and its casts kneading mineral
and organic together. A single meadow can carry more worm
weight below the grass than cow weight above it; old
naturalists called the worm the true maker of farmland, and
they were right.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Leaf-Bag Experiments}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- mesh bags, buried
leaves, and a winter of soil science}]\label{pb:g6:decomposers-and-soil:1}
The class buries \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf} bags in the school hedge bank in
November: equal weights of fresh oak \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaves} in mesh bags,
to be dug up and reweighed through the year. Three bag
types: coarse mesh (lets in worms, woodlice, everything),
fine mesh (excludes the visible crew, admits only the
unseen), and sealed plastic (excludes everything and the
air besides).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Setting up.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Why must all bags hold the \emph{same} weight of
        the \emph{same} \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaves}, buried the \emph{same} day
        in the \emph{same} bank? Name the method.
  \item What question does the fine-mesh bag ask, that the
        coarse one cannot ask alone?
  \item One team wants a fourth bag: coarse mesh, but
        buried in the dry gravel of the schoolyard. What
        question would it ask, by
        \cref{prop:g6:decomposers-and-soil:speed}?
  \item Predict, before digging: rank the three hedge-bank
        bags by how much \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf} weight they will lose by
        June.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The results.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item June's weighings: coarse mesh --- most weight gone,
        \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaves} reduced to \omterm{def:g3:bones-and-muscles:skeleton}{skeletons} and crumbs; fine mesh
        --- clearly lightened, \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaves} softened and
        blackened but slower; sealed plastic --- weight
        almost unchanged, contents sour sludge. Interpret
        each bag.
  \item In the coarse bag the class finds woodlice, a
        worm, springtails and white \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposers}{fungus} threads. Assign
        each its role in the \omterm{def:g1:plants-around-us:parts}{leaf}'s dismantling
        (\cref{ex:g6:decomposers-and-soil:leaf} is the
        model).
  \item The fine bag lost real weight although every
        visible \omterm{def:g1:animals-around-us:animal}{animal} was excluded. What does that prove
        about the unseen \omterm{def:g6:decomposers-and-soil:soil}{soil} life?
  \item The gravel-yard bag (question 3's) lost far less
        than its hedge-bank twin. Which working \omterm{def:g6:exploring-our-environment:conditions}{conditions}
        does the gravel deny?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The larger lesson.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Where did the coarse bag's lost weight \emph{go}?
        Answer with
        \cref{prop:g6:decomposers-and-soil:decomposition}
        --- two destinations at least.
  \item The hedge's spring nettles grow greenest exactly
        along the bank where the class buried its bags. A
        coincidence? Argue from the cycle of matter.
  \item Use the sealed bag to explain why landfilled
        organic waste, wrapped airtight in plastic,
        persists for decades while the same waste composts
        in months.
  \item Conclude in two sentences: what did the winter
        prove about who clears the forest's carpet, and
        what does the clearing feed?
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
