\chapter{Selection, Drift and Speciation}\label{ch:g12:selection-drift-speciation}

In 1848 a black form of the peppered moth was caught near an industrial
city where every tree trunk was coated with soot; by 1895 nearly every
moth in the region was black. A century later, with the soot gone and
the trunks pale again, the black form had almost vanished. Nobody bred
the moths; the birds that eat them did the sorting. The last two
chapters described how variation arises; this one is about what happens
to it in a \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} --- how some \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} become common and others
rare, by selection and by chance --- and how, in the end, one \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}
becomes two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}.

\section{Populations and their alleles}

\begin{definition}[Population, allele frequency]\label{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}
A \emph{population}\index{population} is the set of individuals of one
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} living in the same place and breeding among themselves. Its
genetic makeup is described by the \emph{frequencies}\index{allele frequency}
of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of each \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}: for a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} with two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}
$A$ and $a$, the fraction $p$ of all copies that are $A$ and the
fraction $q = 1 - p$ that are $a$. Evolution, at this scale, is a
change of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} frequencies from one generation to the next.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Frequencies without any force]\label{prop:g12:selection-drift-speciation:hw}
In a large \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} where mating is at random, where no \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}
gives an advantage, and where no \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} or migration occurs, the
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{frequencies} stay the same from generation to generation, and
the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotypes} appear in the proportions
\[
AA : Aa : aa = p^2 : 2pq : q^2 .
\]
This is the reference state: any departure from it, observed over
the generations, is the sign that one of the conditions has failed
--- that selection, chance, migration or \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} is at work.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each gamete carries $A$ with probability $p$ and $a$ with probability
$q$; two gametes drawn at random give $AA$ with probability $p^2$,
$aa$ with $q^2$, and $Aa$ with $pq + qp = 2pq$. Counting the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}
of the new generation: $A$ copies make up $p^2 + \tfrac12 (2pq) =
p(p + q) = p$ of the total. The frequency is unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Counting alleles]\label{ex:g12:selection-drift-speciation:count}
Among 1000 people, 490 are $AA$, 420 $Aa$ and 90 $aa$. Copies of $a$:
$420 + 2 \times 90 = 600$ out of 2000, so $q = 0.3$ and $p = 0.7$ ---
and the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} counts are exactly $p^2$, $2pq$, $q^2$ of 1000: the
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} is at the reference state for this \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}. A \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive} disease
affecting one newborn in 2500 ($q^2 = 1/2500$) implies $q = 1/50$ and
carriers $2pq \approx 1/25$: the figures of
\cref{ch:g11:genes-and-disease}, derived.
\end{example}

\section{Selection}

\begin{definition}[Natural selection]\label{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:selection}
\emph{Natural selection}\index{natural selection} is the difference in
reproduction between individuals that carry different \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, in a
given environment: the carriers of one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} leave, on average, more
descendants than the carriers of another, and the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}'s frequency
rises from generation to generation. What is selected is the whole
individual --- its survival, its mating, its fertility --- and the
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} ride along.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Selection changes frequencies in a direction]\label{prop:g12:selection-drift-speciation:direction}
An \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} whose carriers have even a slightly higher reproductive
success spreads; one whose carriers have less becomes rare. The
direction is set by the environment, and reverses if the environment
does; the speed depends on the size of the advantage and on whether
the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is expressed in one copy (\omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{dominant}, fast) or only in two
(\omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive}, slow at first, and never quite eliminated, since its rare
copies hide in heterozygotes).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The peppered moth: birds take the moths they see; on sooty trunks the
pale form is seen, on clean ones the dark form; the frequency of the
dark \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} rose from near zero to 98\% in fifty years of pollution and
fell back below 10\% in forty years of clean air, tracking the trunks.
\omterm{prop:g11:antibiotic-resistance:mechanisms}{Antibiotic resistance} (\cref{ch:g11:antibiotic-resistance}): the
resistant \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}'s carriers alone reproduce in the presence of the
drug. Lactase persistence (\cref{ch:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype}): the
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is common exactly where milk has been a food for adults for
millennia. In each case the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}'s fortune follows the environment.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Selection by the other sex]\label{ex:g12:selection-drift-speciation:sexual}
A peacock's train makes it slower and more visible to predators, yet it
is kept because peahens choose the males with the largest and most
regular trains: an \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} that improves the train is passed on more
often, whatever it costs in survival. \emph{Sexual selection} --- the
choice of mates --- produces the ornaments, songs and contests of
animals, and can push a trait in a direction that survival alone would
never favour.
\end{example}

\section{Chance: genetic drift}

\begin{proposition}[Genetic drift]\label{prop:g12:selection-drift-speciation:drift}
In any \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} of finite size, the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} of one generation are a
random sample of the previous one: which individuals happen to
reproduce, and which \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} their gametes happen to carry, fluctuate
by chance. \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{Allele} \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{frequencies} therefore wander from generation to
generation without any advantage being involved --- \emph{genetic
drift}\index{genetic drift}. The smaller the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}, the larger
the wandering; in a small \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} an \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} can be lost, or reach
100\%, by chance alone within a few generations, whatever its value.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{Populations} founded by a few individuals --- an island colonised by a
handful of birds, a human community descended from a few dozen
settlers --- carry a distorted sample of the source \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}'s
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}: a rare disease \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} can be common in them, and much of the
source's diversity missing. \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{Species} that passed through a
\emph{bottleneck} of very few survivors (the cheetah of
\cref{ch:g10:biodiversity-scales}, the northern elephant seal reduced
to twenty animals in 1890) show almost no \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic diversity} today,
even after recovering to thousands. And in the laboratory, many small
replicate \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} started at the same frequency scatter in every
direction within a few generations, while large ones stay put.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A founder effect]\label{ex:g12:selection-drift-speciation:founder}
An island community was founded by a few dozen settlers, one of whom
carried an \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} for a rare disorder of the eye. Ten generations
later the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}'s frequency in the community is one in ten --- a
hundred times the mainland value --- because one carrier among thirty
founders is already 1.7\% of the copies, and drift in a small,
isolated \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} moved it further. The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} confers no advantage;
its abundance is an accident of who boarded the boat.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Selection or drift?]\label{met:g12:selection-drift-speciation:which}
Faced with a change of \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{allele frequency}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Estimate the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}'s size: drift is strong below a few
        hundred, negligible in millions.
  \item Look for a direction that tracks the environment (selection),
        or for a random walk (drift). Replicate \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} moving the
        same way indicate selection; moving in different directions,
        drift.
  \item Look for a mechanism linking the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} to survival or
        reproduction; without one, suspect drift.
  \item Remember that both act at once: selection sets a tendency,
        drift adds noise, and in small \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} the noise can drown
        the tendency.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{From population to species}

\begin{proposition}[Speciation]\label{prop:g12:selection-drift-speciation:speciation}
Two \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} of one \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} that stop exchanging \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} --- separated
by a barrier of geography, of behaviour or of timing --- evolve apart:
each accumulates its own \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}, is selected by its own environment
and drifts its own way. When the divergence has gone far enough that
individuals of the two \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} no longer interbreed, or give sterile
offspring, even when they meet again, two \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} exist where there was
one. \emph{Speciation}\index{speciation} is this process; it usually
takes thousands to millions of generations, though polyploidy
(\cref{ch:g12:diversification-of-life}) achieves it in one.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Islands: each of the Galápagos islands carries its own finches,
descended from a mainland \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} and closest to those of the
neighbouring islands. Lakes: a single ancestral cichlid fish has given
hundreds of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} in one African lake within a few hundred thousand
years, separated by habitat and by the females' choice of male colour.
Ring \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}: \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} of a gull spread around the Arctic,
interbreeding with their neighbours all the way round, until the two
ends meet in Europe as forms that do not interbreed --- a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}
boundary caught in the act of forming. And \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} separated recently,
such as polar and brown bears, still produce fertile hybrids
occasionally: the boundary is a matter of degree.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The species, reconsidered]\label{prop:g12:selection-drift-speciation:concept}
A \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} is a \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}, or a set of \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}, whose members
interbreed among themselves and are genetically isolated from other
such sets --- a definition that works for most animals and plants at a
given moment, but has edges: \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations} in the process of separating,
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} that still hybridise, organisms that reproduce without sex.
A \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} is not a fixed type but a lineage in time: it begins when a
\omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} becomes isolated, exists while its members keep
interbreeding, and ends by extinction or by splitting into new
\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}. The \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} alive today are the tips of a tree whose branches
are the subject of \cref{ch:g12:phylogenetic-trees}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{remark}[Evolution, assembled]\label{rem:g12:selection-drift-speciation:assembled}
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{Mutation} and the shuffle supply variation; duplication, transfer,
polyploidy, regulation and \omterm{def:g12:diversification-of-life:symbiosis}{symbiosis} supply more; selection sorts it
by the environment, drift by chance; isolation lets \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{populations}
diverge until they are \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}; extinction removes them. None of
these steps has a goal, and none looks ahead; together, over the
four billion years of the fossil record, they have produced the
diversity of \cref{ch:g10:biodiversity-scales}. The theory that names
these steps and their interplay is the theory of evolution, and every
chapter of this year has been a piece of its evidence.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Mice of the Island}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a population of mice on an island:
its allele frequencies computed, a new predator's selection followed,
the drift of a tiny colony simulated, and the birth of a species
foreseen}]
\label{pb:g12:selection-drift-speciation:1}
On a rocky island, mice carry a coat-colour \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} with two \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}: $D$
(dark, \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{dominant}) and $d$ (pale). A census of 2000 mice finds 720 dark
mice of \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} $DD$, 960 dark $Dd$, and 320 pale $dd$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} now.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{frequencies} $p$ of $D$ and $q$ of $d$ by counting
        copies.
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} numbers expected at the reference state
        and compare with the census. Is the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} at the reference
        state?
  \item What fraction of the dark mice are carriers of $d$?
  \item Explain, from the previous answer, why removing all the pale
        mice from the island for one generation would lower $q$ only a
        little. Compute the new $q$ after such a removal, if the
        remaining mice breed at random.
  \item Which of the four conditions of the reference state is most
        likely to fail on a small island, and with what effect?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- A predator arrives.}
Owls colonise the island. On the pale rock, dark mice are seen and
eaten twice as often: each generation, half the dark mice die before
breeding while all the pale mice survive.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Starting from the census numbers, compute the number of mice of
        each \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} that survive to breed in the first generation.
  \item Compute $p$ and $q$ among the survivors.
  \item If the survivors mate at random, compute the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype}
        proportions of the next generation, then apply the owls again
        and compute $q$ among its survivors.
  \item Repeat once more (two significant figures). Describe the trend
        of $q$ over the three generations.
  \item Explain why $q$ rises steadily, and why $D$, unlike a
        \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive} \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} under the same selection, can be eliminated
        completely.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- A colony of six.}
A storm carries six mice to a neighbouring islet: 2 $DD$, 3 $Dd$, 1 $dd$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute $q$ in the colony and compare with the island.
  \item In the first litter, by chance, only the two $DD$ and one $Dd$
        breed. What are the possible values of $q$ in the next
        generation? What has happened to the \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}'s variation?
  \item Explain why, on the islet, an \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} can vanish in a few
        generations without owls or any advantage.
  \item The islet has no owls. Predict the fate of $d$ there, and say
        why two neighbouring islands can end with different coat
        colours for no reason of adaptation.
  \item Give the name of the process, and the feature of the colony
        that makes it \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{dominant} there.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Ten thousand years later.}
The islet's mice, isolated, have diverged: smaller, paler, breeding in a
different season. Brought together with island mice in the laboratory,
they rarely mate, and the few hybrids are sterile.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Are the islet's mice a new \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}? Justify with the
        definition.
  \item Name the barrier that started the process and the two
        mechanisms that drove the divergence.
  \item The season of breeding differs between the two. Explain how
        such a difference, once present, reinforces the isolation.
  \item If the islet joined the island again tomorrow, would the two
        merge back into one \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}? Distinguish the case of
        question 17 from an earlier stage, say after 100 years.
  \item State the result: the frequency of $d$ on the island before and
        after three generations of owls, the fate of $d$ on the islet
        and the process responsible, and the number of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} at the
        end.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
