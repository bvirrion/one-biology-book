\chapter{Conservation Biology}\label{ch:b3:conservation-biology}

In 1813 Audubon watched a flock of passenger pigeons pass over
Kentucky for three days, darkening the sky; there were perhaps five
billion of them, a quarter of all the birds in North America. On
1~September 1914 the last one, a female named Martha, died in the
Cincinnati Zoo. The species was not rare when its decline began; it
was harvested by the trainload, and its forests were cut, and a bird
that bred only in immense colonies could not breed once the colonies
had thinned below some size no one measured. In 1995 the kakapo, a
flightless nocturnal parrot of New Zealand, was down to fifty-one
birds; every one has since been named, weighed, fitted with a
transmitter and, at the right moment, offered a supplementary meal,
and there are two hundred and fifty. In the same year thirty-one grey
wolves were released into Yellowstone, seventy years after the last
native pack had been shot; within two decades the elk had moved, the
willows had come back along the rivers, and the beavers with them.
Conservation biology is the application of this book's population
genetics, ecology and behaviour to a single practical question --- how
to keep species from disappearing --- and its arithmetic is the
arithmetic of small numbers.

\section{Why small populations die}

\begin{definition}[The risks of smallness]\label{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}
\index{demographic stochasticity}\index{environmental stochasticity}\index{Allee effect}\index{extinction vortex}\index{minimum viable population}
A large population declines when its death rate exceeds its birth
rate; a small one can vanish without any change in either.
\emph{Demographic stochasticity} is the chance that, among a few
individuals, more happen to die than are born, or all the survivors are
male: it matters below a few dozen. \emph{Environmental stochasticity}
--- a bad winter, a drought, a disease --- strikes every individual at
once, and a population of a few hundred that a large one would ride out
can be wiped out; \emph{catastrophes} are its tail. The \emph{Allee
effect} makes the per-capita growth rate fall at low density --- mates
cannot be found, colonial breeders cannot nest, herds cannot defend
their young, the passenger pigeon's flocks could not saturate the
predators that took their squabs --- so that below a threshold density
the population declines toward zero on its own. And below a few
hundred, the genetic processes of the next section reduce fitness
generation by generation. These feed one another: a smaller population
loses variation, becomes less fertile, grows smaller, is likelier to
be hit by chance, and loses more --- the \emph{extinction vortex}
(Gilpin and Soul\'e, 1986). A \emph{minimum viable population} is the
size above which the probability of extinction over a stated horizon
(say \qty{5}{\%} in a century) is acceptable; it depends on the species'
biology and on how variable its environment is, and is usually in the
thousands.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Effective population size]\label{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}
\index{effective population size}\index{sex ratio}\index{harmonic mean}\index{inbreeding coefficient}\index{heterozygosity}
The rate at which a population loses variation and accumulates
inbreeding is governed not by its census size $N$ but by its
\emph{effective size} $N_{e}$: the size of an ideal population ---
constant, randomly mating, equal \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{sex ratio}, Poisson family sizes --- that
would lose heterozygosity at the same rate. Three departures from the
ideal lower it. Unequal \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{sex ratio}, with $N_{m}$ breeding males and
$N_{f}$ females:
\[
N_{e} = \frac{4N_{m}N_{f}}{N_{m} + N_{f}} ;
\]
one male and a hundred females give $N_{e} \approx 4$. Fluctuating size
over $t$ generations: $N_{e}$ is the \emph{\omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{harmonic mean}} of the
generation sizes, $1/N_{e} = (1/t)\sum 1/N_{i}$, dominated by the
smallest --- a population of a thousand that once passed through ten
has $N_{e}$ near a hundred over that span. And variance in family size
above Poisson lowers it further. In a population of effective size
$N_{e}$ the \emph{\omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{inbreeding coefficient}} $F$ --- the probability that
the two alleles at a locus in an individual are copies of one ancestral
allele --- rises by $1/2N_{e}$ per generation,
\[
F_{t} = 1 - \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{2N_{e}}\Bigr)^{t} \approx \frac{t}{2N_{e}},
\qquad H_{t} = H_{0}\Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{2N_{e}}\Bigr)^{t} ,
\]
and the \emph{heterozygosity} $H$ decays at the same rate. Franklin's
rule of thumb, ``50/500'': $N_{e} \ge 50$ keeps inbreeding below
\qty{1}{\%} a generation, the rate animal breeders tolerate; $N_{e} \ge
500$ lets mutation replace the variation drift removes. Since $N_{e}$
is typically a tenth of the census size, the rule asks for populations
of five hundred to five thousand.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Two gametes drawn at random from the population are copies of the same
parental allele with probability $1/2N$ in the ideal population, which
is $F$'s increment; heterozygosity is $1 - F$ scaled, so each
generation multiplies it by $(1 - 1/2N_{e})$, giving the exponential
decay and, for $t \ll N_{e}$, the linear rise of $F$. \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{Sex ratio}: half
the genes come from each sex, so the chance two gametes share a
parental allele is $\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2N_{m}} +
\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2N_{f}}$ (a quarter of pairs are both
paternal, a quarter both maternal); setting this equal to $1/2N_{e}$
gives $1/N_{e} = 1/(4N_{m}) + 1/(4N_{f})$, the formula. Fluctuation:
heterozygosity after $t$ generations is $H_{0}\prod(1 - 1/2N_{i})
\approx H_{0}\exp(-\sum 1/2N_{i})$, equal to $H_{0}\exp(-t/2N_{e})$
when $1/N_{e}$ is the average of the $1/N_{i}$. Inbreeding lowers
fitness because it exposes recessive deleterious alleles as
homozygotes --- \emph{inbreeding depression}, a fall of several per cent
in survival or fecundity per \qty{10}{\%} of $F$ in most outbreeding
species.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
The Florida panther, down to some twenty-five animals in the 1990s,
showed kinked tails, undescended testicles, heart defects and sperm of
which \qty{90}{\%} were abnormal --- inbreeding depression made visible;
eight females introduced from Texas in 1995 (\emph{genetic rescue})
tripled the population and the defects receded. The wolves of Isle
Royale, founded by a pair in 1949 and isolated, declined to two
grossly inbred individuals by 2018 with spinal deformities in most
skeletons examined. The greater prairie chicken of Illinois fell from
thousands to fifty, and its egg hatching rate from \qty{93}{\%} to
\qty{56}{\%}, recovering after birds were brought from other states.
Museum skins and ancient DNA let the loss of heterozygosity be
measured directly against the pre-decline population in each case.
\end{proof}

\section{Habitat: how much, and in what shape}

\begin{proposition}[Species and area]\label{prop:b3:conservation-biology:area}
\index{species--area relationship}\index{habitat fragmentation}\index{island biogeography}\index{corridor}\index{SLOSS debate}\index{edge effect}
Across islands, and across habitat fragments that behave like islands,
the number of species $S$ rises with area $A$ as a power law,
\[
S = c\,A^{z}, \qquad z \approx 0.15\text{--}0.35,
\]
the \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:conservation-biology:area}{species--area relationship}} (Arrhenius, 1921; MacArthur and
Wilson's \omterm{prop:b3:conservation-biology:area}{island biogeography}, 1967, explained it as a balance of
immigration and extinction, extinction being faster on small islands).
With $z = 0.25$, losing \qty{90}{\%} of a habitat's area eventually loses
$1 - 0.1^{0.25} = \qty{44}{\%}$ of its species; losing half loses
\qty{16}{\%}. The loss is not immediate --- an \emph{extinction debt} is
paid over decades as populations too small for their fragments dwindle
--- and \emph{fragmentation} of a fixed area into pieces adds losses of
its own: each fragment holds a smaller population, \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:conservation-biology:area}{edge effects}}
(wind, heat, predators, weeds) penetrate hundreds of metres into
forest, and species that need the interior lose more than the area
accounts for. Whether a single large reserve or several small ones of
the same total area saves more species (the \emph{SLOSS} debate) depends
on the species: the large one holds larger populations and interior
habitat, the small ones spread the risk of a catastrophe and sample
more habitat types. \emph{Corridors} that connect fragments let
individuals and genes move between them, turning isolated populations
into a metapopulation whose parts can be recolonised after local
extinction and rescued from inbreeding --- which is why the land
between reserves has become as much the concern as the reserves.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The power law is empirical, fitted on log--log axes to archipelagos,
lakes, mountaintops and forest fragments the world over; the value of
$z$ is lower for pieces of a mainland (immigration keeps species
present) and higher for true islands. The fractional loss follows
directly: $S'/S = (A'/A)^{z}$. \omterm{prop:b3:conservation-biology:area}{Island biogeography}'s mechanism was
tested by Simberloff and Wilson (1969), who fumigated small mangrove
islets in Florida, killing every arthropod, and watched species
numbers return to their previous values within a year --- an
equilibrium, with different species than before: the number is set by
area and distance, the identities by chance.
\end{proof}

\section{Predicting and preventing}

\begin{method}[Population viability analysis]\label{met:b3:conservation-biology:pva}
\index{population viability analysis}\index{quasi-extinction}
To estimate a population's risk of extinction and what would reduce it:
(1) build a model of its dynamics --- a matrix of age- or stage-specific
survival and fecundity, as in the Year~2 volume's demography, estimated
from marked individuals over several years; (2) add stochasticity ---
demographic, by drawing each individual's fate at random, and
environmental, by drawing each year's rates from their observed
variance, with occasional catastrophes; (3) add density dependence and
the genetic decline of inbreeding where data allow; (4) simulate
thousands of trajectories from the current size over the horizon of
interest and record the fraction that fall below a \emph{quasi-extinction}
threshold (a size from which recovery is judged impossible); (5) vary
the inputs --- adult survival, juvenile survival, habitat area, a
translocation of ten animals --- and see which change most reduces the
risk; that is where effort should go. The output is a probability with
wide uncertainty, not a prediction; its value is comparative, and it
has repeatedly shown that adult survival, not fecundity, is the lever
in long-lived species, which is why fishing lines kill albatross
populations that lay one egg every two years faster than any loss of
nests could.
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[Time to extinction]\label{prop:b3:conservation-biology:time}
\index{extinction time}
For a population that fluctuates because the environment does, with
mean growth rate $\bar r$ near zero and variance $\sigma^{2}$ in the
annual growth rate, the log of population size performs a random walk
with drift, and the expected time to extinction from size $N$ grows
only as the \emph{logarithm} of $N$ when $\bar r < 0$ --- doubling the
population adds a fixed number of years --- and as a \emph{power} of
$N$ when $\bar r > 0$, with exponent $2\bar r/\sigma^{2} - 1$; under
\omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{demographic stochasticity} alone it grows \emph{exponentially} with
$N$, so that a population safe from demographic chance at a hundred
can still be lost to environmental variance at ten thousand. The
practical consequence is that reducing the variance of a population's
growth --- buffering it against bad years with more habitat, more
sites, a wider diet --- can do more for its persistence than raising
its mean.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{definition}[The tools]\label{def:b3:conservation-biology:tools}
\index{IUCN Red List}\index{invasive species}\index{ex situ conservation}\index{captive breeding}\index{reintroduction}\index{rewilding}\index{trophic cascade}
The \emph{IUCN Red List} classifies species by quantitative criteria:
a decline of \qty{30}{\%}, \qty{50}{\%} or \qty{80}{\%} over ten years or
three generations, a range below \qty{20000}{}, \qty{5000}{} or
\qty{100}{km^2}, a population below \qty{10000}{}, \qty{2500}{} or
\qty{250}{} mature individuals, or a modelled extinction probability,
place a species as Vulnerable, Endangered or Critically Endangered; a
quarter of mammals and an eighth of birds qualify. The causes are
habitat loss first, then over-harvest, \emph{invasive species}
(predators and competitors carried to islands whose species had never
met them: rats, cats and stoats have taken most of New Zealand's birds,
a single brown tree snake species most of Guam's), pollution and
climate change. The responses: protected areas covering a sixth of the
land; the control or eradication of invaders (a hundred and fifty
islands cleared of rats); \emph{ex situ} conservation --- seed banks
holding a million accessions in a vault in the Arctic permafrost,
frozen embryos, and \emph{captive breeding} with pedigrees managed to
minimise kinship, which brought the California condor back from
twenty-two birds in 1987 and the black-footed ferret from eighteen;
\emph{reintroduction}, which succeeds about a third of the time and
more often when the cause of the original loss has been removed; and
\emph{rewilding}, the return of large animals and the processes they
drive --- Yellowstone's wolves, whose predation and the fear of it moved
the elk off the riverbanks and let willow, aspen, beaver and songbirds
return, a \emph{trophic cascade} that runs from a carnivore to the shape
of a river.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Three recoveries]\label{ex:b3:conservation-biology:recoveries}
The kakapo: fifty-one birds in 1995, all moved to islands cleared of
predators; every bird carries a transmitter, every nest is watched by
camera, chicks are hand-reared when a mother fails, males are fed to
bring them into breeding condition in the years the rimu trees fruit,
and the pedigree is managed to keep the last Fiordland male's rare
genes in the population; every kakapo's genome has been sequenced.
Two hundred and fifty now, and a $N_{e}$ that the genomes put near a
hundred. The whooping crane: fifteen birds in 1941, a single migratory
flock, bred in captivity from eggs taken from wild nests, taught new
migration routes behind ultralight aircraft, and now some eight
hundred. The humpback whale: hunted to a few thousand, protected in
1966, and past a hundred thousand, an increase near \qty{8}{\%} a year
that shows what a long-lived animal does when the single cause of its
decline is removed. Each recovery cost tens of millions and decades;
the arithmetic of the chapter says why the effort had to be spent
before the numbers reached the vortex, and why it was cheaper than any
later rescue would have been.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The arithmetic of keeping]\label{rem:b3:conservation-biology:remark}
Every chapter of this course has ended in a number, and this one ends
in several: an effective size, an \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{inbreeding coefficient} rising by
$1/2N_{e}$ a generation, an exponent $z$ that turns lost hectares into
lost species, a probability of extinction over a century. They are
crude --- the census is uncertain, the variance is guessed, the model
is a caricature --- and they are what turns a sentiment about
vanishing birds into a decision about which fifty hectares to buy and
which eight animals to move. The biology is the same biology as the
rest of the book: drift, selection, demography, behaviour, the
dependence of a population on its habitat and of a habitat on its
populations. What is new is only the urgency, and the fact that the
experiment is being run once, without a control, on the species that
are left.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:1}
Define \omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{demographic stochasticity}, \omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{environmental stochasticity} and the
\omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{Allee effect}, and give one example of each from the chapter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:2}
What is the \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{effective population size}, and name three features of a
real population that make it smaller than the census.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:3}
State the species--area law. If $z = 0.25$, what fraction of species
does a habitat keep after losing half its area? Nine tenths? Ninety-nine
hundredths?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:4}
Explain the 50/500 rule, and what each number protects against.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:5}
A herd of 200 elephant seals has 8 breeding males and 120 breeding
females. Compute $N_{e}$ and the loss of heterozygosity per generation.
How many generations to lose a quarter of it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:6}
A population numbered 1000, 1000, 20, 1000, 1000 in five successive
generations. Compute $N_{e}$ over the span, and the heterozygosity
retained, and compare with a constant 1000.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:7}
Twenty-five Florida panthers with $N_{e} \approx 10$: inbreeding
accumulated in 5 generations? If fitness falls \qty{5}{\%} for every
\qty{10}{\%} of $F$, by how much has fitness fallen? What does adding
eight unrelated females do to $F$ in the next generation?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:8}
Yellowstone: 31 wolves released in 1995--96, about 100 in the park by
2003. Estimate $r$; how many would there be in 2020 if growth continued,
and why did it not?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:9}
Classify by the IUCN criteria in the chapter: (a) a frog whose
population fell \qty{60}{\%} in ten years; (b) a plant of 180 mature
individuals; (c) a fish whose range is \qty{3000}{km^2}; (d) a bird of
\num{30000} individuals declining \qty{10}{\%} a decade.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:10}
A population of $N$ pairs each producing, with probability $1/2$, two
surviving offspring and otherwise none (\omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{demographic stochasticity}).
Compute the probability that the whole population fails in one
generation for $N = 2$, $5$, $10$, $20$, and comment on how
demographic risk scales with $N$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:11}
The species--area law with $z = 0.25$, and a forest cut into 10 equal
isolated fragments. Compare the species expected in one fragment with
a tenth of the original, and argue why the total across fragments is
not simply the original --- what determines whether the fragments
together hold more or fewer species than the whole did?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:conservation-biology:12}
Why does adding a few migrants per generation ($m N_{e} \approx 1$)
largely stop the loss of variation in a small population, and what does
it cost in local adaptation? Use the balance between drift, $1/2N_{e}$,
and the fraction of alleles replaced by migrants, $m$, to argue.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: Fifty and Five Hundred}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a recovery programme in numbers: the
effective size of a rescued parrot population and its inbreeding, a
genetic rescue, the species a shrinking forest will keep, the growth
of reintroduced wolves, and the categories and costs, ending on $N_{e}$,
the inbreeding after ten generations and the fraction of species kept}]
\label{pb:b3:conservation-biology:1}
Data: a parrot population of 60 adults, 20 males and 40 females, all
breeding; generation time \qty{10}{yr}. Population history over the last
five generations: 500, 100, 60, 51, 60. Inbreeding depression:
fitness falls by \qty{6}{\%} for each \qty{0.1}{} of $F$. Rescue: 10
unrelated birds added to 60. Forest: \qty{2000}{km^2} reduced to
\qty{300}{km^2}, $z = 0.25$; species now 180. Wolves: 31 released, 100
after 8 years, carrying capacity in the park about 120. Cost: four
million dollars a year for the parrots.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Effective size.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $N_{e}$ from the \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{sex ratio} of the current 60. Compare with the
        census.
  \item Harmonic-mean $N_{e}$ over the five generations of history.
        Which generation dominates?
  \item Inbreeding accumulated over those five generations, using the
        harmonic-mean $N_{e}$ (exact formula and linear approximation).
  \item Fitness lost to that inbreeding.
  \item Heterozygosity retained after the five generations, as a
        fraction of the original.
  \item If the population is now held at 60 with the current \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{sex
        ratio}, what $F$ will it reach in ten more generations, and what
        fitness loss? How many years is that?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Rescue.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Ten unrelated birds join the 60. The fraction of alleles in the
        next generation from the newcomers is about $10/70$; the
        \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{inbreeding coefficient} of offspring with one native and one
        newcomer parent is $0$. Estimate the population's mean $F$ in
        the next generation if mating is random.
  \item Explain why $F$ falls at once but heterozygosity rises only as
        the migrants' alleles spread, and why a second rescue may be
        needed.
  \item To reach $N_{e} = 500$ with an equal \omterm{thm:b3:conservation-biology:ne}{sex ratio}, how many adults
        are needed? At $r = \qty{0.05}{yr^{-1}}$ from 70 birds, how many
        years?
  \item The programme removes eggs from poor mothers for hand-rearing,
        raising the mean number of fledged chicks per pair but equalising
        family sizes. Why does equalising family sizes \emph{raise}
        $N_{e}$ above the census (toward $2N$)?
  \item The last male of a distinct southern lineage is sterile in the
        wild. Propose two interventions and their costs to the gene
        pool.
  \item At four million dollars a year over the 40 years to reach
        $N_{e} = 500$, total cost, and cost per bird added. Is that expensive? Against
        what?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Forest.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Fraction of species the forest will keep at \qty{300}{km^2};
        expected number of species, and the extinction debt (species
        present now that will be lost).
  \item If instead the \qty{300}{km^2} were three isolated fragments of
        \qty{100}{km^2}, species per fragment; total if the fragments
        held entirely different species; total if identical. What
        decides?
  \item A corridor of \qty{20}{km^2} joins the three fragments. Treat
        them as one area of \qty{320}{km^2}: species expected. Compare
        with question~14's identical-species case.
  \item The forest's largest predator needs \qty{50}{km^2} per pair and
        an $N_{e}$ of 50 to persist. Can the whole \qty{300}{km^2} hold
        it? Can a fragment? What does this say about area versus number
        of species as a criterion?
  \item Estimate how long the extinction debt takes to be paid if the
        species lost have populations declining at \qty{3}{\%} a year
        from 200 to a quasi-extinction threshold of 10.
  \item Why does the species--area law underestimate the loss when
        fragmentation adds \omterm{prop:b3:conservation-biology:area}{edge effects}, and overestimate it when the
        surrounding land is partly usable?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Wolves and lists.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Wolves: $r$ from $31 \to 100$ in 8 years, and the doubling time.
  \item Logistic growth toward $K = 120$: population after 8 years
        under logistic rather than exponential growth, starting from 31
        with the $r$ of question~19 (use $N(t) = K/[1 + (K/N_{0} -
        1)\mathrm{e}^{-rt}]$). Which fits the observed 100 better, and what
        does that say about density dependence in the first years?
  \item The elk numbered \num{17000} in 1995 and \num{4000} in 2015.
        Average annual rate of decline. How much of it can wolves eating
        \num{20} elk each a year account for at 100 wolves?
  \item Classify the parrot (60 mature individuals) and the pre-rescue
        forest predator by the IUCN criteria.
  \item Passenger pigeon: five billion in 1800, extinct in 1914. If the
        decline was exponential from 1870, when there were still
        billions, to one bird in 1914, what annual rate does that imply?
        Why does the \omterm{def:b3:conservation-biology:small}{Allee effect} make the last decades faster than
        exponential?
  \item Explain in a paragraph why a PVA would have said the passenger
        pigeon was safe in 1850, and what it would have missed.
  \item Summarise: the current $N_{e}$ (question~1), $F$ after ten more
        generations at 60 (question~6), and the fraction of species the
        shrunken forest keeps (question~13).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
