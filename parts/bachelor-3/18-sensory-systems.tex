\chapter{Sensory Systems}\label{ch:b3:sensory-systems}

On a dark night a human eye can detect a flash of a few photons ---
the smallest amount of light that physics allows to be detected at all.
The ear hears a sound that moves the eardrum by less than the diameter
of a hydrogen atom, and tells a tone from another that differs by a
fifth of a per cent. A dog follows a trail of a few molecules per
cubic centimetre; a shark finds a flatfish under the sand by the
electric field of its heartbeat. Every sense begins with a cell that
converts one kind of energy into a change of membrane potential, and
every sense ends as spikes in a nerve; between the two lie the
problems that this chapter treats: how a receptor amplifies a single
molecule or photon into a signal a neuron can read, how a range of a
trillionfold in intensity is squeezed into a rate of a few hundred
spikes a second, how a mechanical wave is sorted by frequency, how
ten thousand smells are told apart with four hundred receptors, and
how the brain, which never receives anything but spikes, knows which
sense they came from.

\section{Principles common to every sense}

\begin{definition}[Transduction, coding and adaptation]\label{def:b3:sensory-systems:principles}
\index{sensory transduction}\index{receptor potential}\index{labelled line}\index{receptive field}\index{adaptation!sensory}\index{lateral inhibition}
A \emph{sensory receptor cell} contains a \emph{transduction} mechanism
--- a protein or cascade --- that converts a stimulus (a photon, a
molecule, a displacement, heat, an electric field) into a change of
membrane conductance and hence a \emph{receptor potential}, graded with
the stimulus; the cell, or the neuron it synapses on, turns this into a
train of action potentials whose rate encodes intensity. The
\emph{modality} of a signal is given not by the spikes, which are
identical in every nerve, but by the wire they travel on and where it
ends in the brain: a \emph{labelled line}. A sensory neuron responds to
stimuli within a \emph{receptive field} --- a patch of skin, a region of
the visual field, a band of frequencies --- and neighbouring fields
sharpen one another by \emph{lateral inhibition}, each neuron
suppressing its neighbours so that edges and contrasts are exaggerated.
Most receptors \emph{adapt}: their response to a maintained stimulus
declines, so that they report change rather than level, and their
working range shifts to sit around the prevailing background.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Weber, Fechner and Stevens]\label{thm:b3:sensory-systems:weber}
\index{Weber's law}\index{Fechner's law}\index{Stevens' power law}
Weber's observation (1834) is that the smallest detectable change
$\Delta I$ in a stimulus of intensity $I$ is a fixed fraction of it:
$\Delta I/I = k$, with $k$ about $0.02$ for weight, $0.08$ for
brightness, $0.1$ for loudness. If each just-noticeable difference is
counted as one unit of sensation $S$, then $\mathrm{d}S = \mathrm{d}I/
(kI)$ and
\[
S = \frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{I}{I_{0}} ,
\]
where $I_{0}$ is the threshold: sensation grows as the logarithm of the
stimulus (Fechner, 1860), which is why intensities are measured in
\omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{decibels} and magnitudes, and why a candle added to a candle is
noticed and a candle added to a floodlight is not. Direct scaling
experiments, in which subjects assign numbers to sensations, give
instead a power law $S \propto I^{n}$ (Stevens, 1957), with $n \approx
0.3$ for loudness and brightness, $1$ for line length, and $3.5$ for
electric shock; a logarithm and a small power are nearly
indistinguishable over a few decades, and the two laws agree on the
essential: the nervous system compresses.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
From $\Delta I = kI$ for one unit of $S$, the increment of sensation
per increment of stimulus is $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}I = 1/(kI)$;
integrating from the threshold $I_{0}$, where $S = 0$, gives $S = (1/k)
\ln(I/I_{0})$. The power law is the alternative assumption that a fixed
\emph{ratio} of stimuli produces a fixed \emph{ratio} of sensations,
$\mathrm{d}S/S = n\,\mathrm{d}I/I$, whose integral is $S \propto I^{n}$;
for $n \to 0$ with suitable scaling it tends to the logarithm.
\end{proof}

\section{Vision}

\begin{definition}[The retina]\label{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}
\index{retina}\index{rod}\index{cone}\index{phototransduction}\index{rhodopsin}\index{ganglion cell}\index{centre--surround}
The \emph{retina} is a sheet of brain at the back of the eye, three
layers of cells with the photoreceptors, paradoxically, at the back,
against the pigment epithelium. \emph{Rods} --- $10^{8}$ per eye, one
pigment, \emph{rhodopsin} --- serve dim light and saturate in daylight;
\emph{cones} --- $6\times 10^{6}$, three pigments peaking in the blue,
green and red, packed into the fovea --- serve daylight, colour and
acuity. \emph{Phototransduction} is a cascade: a photon isomerises the
retinal chromophore of one rhodopsin; the activated rhodopsin turns on
hundreds of molecules of the G~protein transducin, each of which
activates a phosphodiesterase that destroys cyclic GMP; the fall in
cGMP closes the cation channels that the cGMP held open in the dark,
the inward ``dark current'' stops, and the cell \emph{hyperpolarises} ---
light turns a photoreceptor off, and it releases less glutamate. In the
dark the cell is depolarised and releasing continuously. The signal
passes to \emph{bipolar cells} (of ON and OFF types, which invert or
preserve it) and on to the \emph{\omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:comparative}{ganglion} cells}, a million per eye,
whose axons form the optic nerve --- a hundredfold compression.
Horizontal and amacrine cells connect laterally, and their inhibition
gives each \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:comparative}{ganglion} cell a \emph{centre--surround} \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:principles}{receptive field}:
excited by light in a central disc and inhibited by light in the ring
around it (or the reverse), so that the retina reports local contrast
and edges rather than absolute light.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Counting photons: the frequency of seeing]\label{thm:b3:sensory-systems:hecht}
\index{single-photon detection}\index{frequency-of-seeing curve}\index{Poisson!photon counting}
A flash that delivers on average $a$ absorbed photons to a patch of
\omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rods} delivers, on any one presentation, a number $k$ that is Poisson
distributed: $P(k) = e^{-a}a^{k}/k!$. If the observer reports seeing
the flash whenever at least $n$ photons are absorbed, the probability
of seeing is
\[
P_{\text{see}}(a) = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} e^{-a}\,\frac{a^{k}}{k!} ,
\]
a curve that rises from $0$ to $1$ as $a$ increases, and whose
\emph{steepness} on a logarithmic scale of $a$ depends only on $n$:
the larger the threshold count, the steeper the curve. Fitting
the measured \omterm{thm:b3:sensory-systems:hecht}{frequency-of-seeing curve} therefore gives $n$ without
knowing how many of the delivered photons were actually absorbed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Photons from a weak, steady source arrive independently at random, and
the number absorbed in a short flash from a mean of $a$ is Poisson
(the limit of a binomial with many photons each absorbed with small
probability). Seeing requires $k \ge n$, whose probability is one
minus the sum of the first $n$ Poisson terms. Scaling the source by a
factor $c$ multiplies $a$ by $c$ and shifts the curve along the $\log a$
axis without changing its shape, so the shape identifies $n$: for $n =
1$, $P = 1 - e^{-a}$ rises over two decades of $a$; for $n = 6$ it
rises over less than one.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Hecht, Shlaer and Pirenne (1942) sat observers in the dark for half
an hour, then flashed a tiny green spot on the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{retina}'s rod-rich
periphery for a millisecond, hundreds of times at several intensities,
and recorded the fraction of flashes seen. The threshold flash
contained, at the cornea, some $90$ photons, of which (after
reflection, absorption in the eye's media and the $20\,\%$ chance of a
photon that reaches a \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rod} being absorbed by \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rhodopsin}) about $5$--$14$
were absorbed. The \omterm{thm:b3:sensory-systems:hecht}{frequency-of-seeing curves} had the steepness of
$n = 5$ to $7$: the observer said ``yes'' when about six \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rods}, out of
the five hundred under the spot, had each caught a single photon. A
\omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rod} detects one photon; the brain demands a few in coincidence, to
keep the false alarms from the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rods}' spontaneous isomerisations ---
one per \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rod} per forty seconds --- below the rate of the stars.
\end{proof}

\section{Hearing and balance}

\begin{definition}[The cochlea]\label{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}
\index{cochlea}\index{basilar membrane}\index{tonotopy}\index{hair cell}\index{stereocilia}\index{tip link}\index{decibel}
Sound is a pressure wave; the ear's problem is to detect pressure
changes of a few tens of micropascals in air and to sort them by
frequency. The eardrum and the three middle-ear bones concentrate the
force from the drum's \qty{55}{mm^2} onto the \qty{3}{mm^2} of the oval
window, raising the pressure some twentyfold --- the impedance match
between air and the fluid of the inner ear, without which most of the
sound would reflect. Inside the \emph{cochlea}, a coiled tube
\qty{35}{mm} long, the pressure wave travels along the \emph{basilar
membrane}, which is narrow and stiff at the base and wide and floppy at
the apex; each frequency makes the membrane vibrate most at one place
--- high frequencies near the base, low near the apex --- a
\emph{tonotopic} map that the brain reads as pitch. On the membrane sit
the \emph{hair cells}: \num{3500} inner hair cells in a single row,
each crowned with a bundle of \emph{stereocilia} joined tip to tip by
fine \emph{tip links}; bending the bundle toward its tallest \omterm{def:b3:cytoskeleton-motility:cilia}{cilium}
stretches the links and pulls open cation channels within microseconds,
without any second messenger, and potassium flows in from the
endolymph, whose unusual composition and $+\qty{80}{mV}$ potential give
a driving force of \qty{150}{mV}. The inner hair cells signal the
auditory nerve; the \num{12000} outer hair cells, driven by the same
motion, contract and lengthen with the sound through the motor protein
prestin and pump energy back into the membrane's vibration --- an
amplifier that sharpens tuning a hundredfold and is what fails in most
deafness. Intensity is measured in \emph{decibels}: $L = 10\log_{10}
(I/I_{0})$, with $I_{0} = \qty{1e-12}{W/m^2}$ the threshold of hearing;
the ear works over \qty{120}{dB}, a trillionfold in intensity.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Von B\'ek\'esy (1940s) opened the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{cochleas} of cadavers, sprinkled
silver particles on the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{basilar membrane} and watched under
stroboscopic light as pure tones set up a travelling wave whose peak
lay nearer the base the higher the tone --- the place code, for which
he received the Nobel prize in 1961. Hudspeth (1980s) pushed a single
hair bundle with a glass fibre and recorded the current: it flowed
within \qty{10}{\micro s} of the push, far too fast for any enzyme
cascade, in proportion to how far the bundle moved toward its tallest
row, and it vanished when the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{tip links} were cut with a calcium
chelator. Gating is mechanical --- the link pulls the channel open ---
which is why hearing is the fastest of the senses and why its
transducer is destroyed by the loud sounds that snap the links.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The ear's numbers]\label{ex:b3:sensory-systems:ear-numbers}
At the threshold of hearing the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{basilar membrane} moves about
\qty{0.1}{nm} and the pressure amplitude is \qty{20}{\micro Pa}, a
two-billionth of atmospheric; at \qty{120}{dB} the pressure is a
million times greater and the membrane's motion tens of nanometres. A
young ear hears from \qty{20}{Hz} to \qty{20}{kHz} and separates tones
\qty{0.3}{\%} apart, the discrimination of the place code sharpened by
the outer \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{hair cells}. The auditory nerve's \num{30000} fibres fire in
step with the pressure wave up to about \qty{4}{kHz} (phase locking),
carrying timing to a precision of tens of microseconds, from which the
brain computes a sound's direction from the difference in arrival at
the two ears --- as little as \qty{10}{\micro s}. The balance organs use
the same \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{hair cells}: in the \emph{semicircular canals} the fluid's
inertia bends them when the head turns (angular acceleration), and in
the otolith organs a layer of calcium carbonate crystals loads them
with gravity and linear acceleration.
\end{example}

\section{Chemical senses}

\begin{definition}[Olfaction and taste]\label{def:b3:sensory-systems:chemical}
\index{olfactory receptor}\index{combinatorial code}\index{glomerulus}\index{taste}
Smell begins in a patch of epithelium at the top of the nose, where
some ten million \emph{olfactory sensory neurons} each express one
\emph{olfactory receptor} gene out of about $400$ in humans (\num{1100}
in a mouse, the largest gene family in either genome) --- G-protein-
coupled receptors that bind odorant molecules in a pocket and, through
cyclic AMP, open a channel. Each receptor binds several odorants and
each odorant binds several receptors, so an odour is a
\emph{combinatorial code}, a pattern of activity across the $400$
receptor types, and a change of one carbon in a molecule changes the
pattern and the smell. All the neurons expressing one receptor send
their axons to the same one or two \emph{glomeruli} in the olfactory
bulb, so the bulb holds a map in which each odour is a spatial
pattern of active glomeruli, read by the cortex without passing
through the \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{thalamus}. Taste is simpler: five modalities, each a
\omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:principles}{labelled line} from its own receptor cells --- sweet, umami and bitter
through G-protein-coupled receptors (one sweet receptor, one umami,
some twenty-five bitter), salt through a sodium channel, sour through
a proton channel --- and the rest of what we call flavour is smell,
reaching the nose through the back of the mouth.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Buck and Axel (1991) reasoned that odorant receptors would be
G-protein-coupled receptors expressed only in the olfactory epithelium,
and found by PCR with degenerate \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:pcr}{primers} a family of hundreds of
such genes, expressed there and nowhere else --- the receptors for
smell, unknown for a century. In-situ hybridisation showed one receptor
per neuron and the convergence of like neurons on single glomeruli;
Malnic, Buck and colleagues (1999) recorded single neurons and showed
that each odorant activated several receptor types and each receptor
several odorants --- the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:chemical}{combinatorial code}. Bushdid and colleagues
(2014) asked subjects to discriminate mixtures and estimated that
humans can tell apart more than a trillion odours, far beyond the
few thousand of common belief.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The capacity of a combinatorial code]\label{prop:b3:sensory-systems:combinatorial}
\index{combinatorial code!capacity}
With $R$ receptor types each either active or silent, an odour can be
represented by any of $2^{R}$ patterns; with $R = 400$ that is
$10^{120}$, and even if only patterns with exactly $10$ active types
are considered there are $\binom{400}{10} \approx 3\times 10^{20}$.
Discrimination is limited not by the code but by the noise of the
receptors and by the brain's reading: a labelled-line code with one
receptor per odour would allow $400$ smells, a combinatorial one
allows more than there are molecules to smell. The cost is that a
single receptor tells the brain little on its own; the pattern must be
read as a whole, which is what the glomerular map and the olfactory
cortex do.
\end{proposition}

\section{Touch, temperature, pain and the senses we lack}

\begin{definition}[Somatosensation]\label{def:b3:sensory-systems:somato}
\index{mechanoreceptor}\index{Piezo channel}\index{TRP channel}\index{nociceptor}\index{proprioception}
The skin holds several kinds of \emph{mechanoreceptor}, each an axon
ending in its own capsule: Merkel cells for fine pressure and texture
(slowly adapting, small fields), Meissner corpuscles for light touch
and slip (rapidly adapting), Pacinian corpuscles deep in the skin for
vibration (very rapidly adapting, huge fields), Ruffini endings for
stretch. The transducer in most of them is \emph{Piezo2}, a giant
ion channel with a propeller of blades that opens when the membrane
is stretched; without it touch and the sense of body position are
lost, and the same channel in the lungs and bladder senses their
filling. \emph{Temperature} is sensed by channels of the TRP family
tuned to different ranges --- TRPV1, opened by heat above
\qty{43}{\celsius} and by capsaicin, which is why chilli is ``hot'';
TRPM8, opened by cold and by menthol --- and \emph{pain} by free nerve
endings, the \emph{nociceptors}, that respond to damaging heat,
pressure or chemicals (protons, ATP, bradykinin) and whose thresholds
are lowered by \omterm{def:b3:innate-immunity:inflammation}{inflammation} (\cref{ch:b3:innate-immunity}).
\emph{Proprioception}, the sense of where the limbs are, comes from
the muscle spindles and tendon organs of \cref{ch:b3:nervous-systems}
and from Piezo2 in joint capsules. The density of receptors sets
acuity: two points \qty{2}{mm} apart are told apart on a fingertip,
\qty{40}{mm} apart on the back.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Measuring a sense]\label{met:b3:sensory-systems:psychophysics}
\index{psychophysics}\index{threshold}\index{two-point discrimination}
To characterise a sensory channel: (1) find the \emph{absolute
threshold} by presenting stimuli of graded intensity many times and
fitting the fraction detected --- the intensity detected half the time
--- as Hecht did; (2) find the \emph{difference threshold} at several
intensities and check \omterm{thm:b3:sensory-systems:weber}{Weber's law}; (3) map the \emph{spatial acuity} with
two stimuli at varying separation (the two-point test on skin, a
grating on the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{retina}); (4) map the \emph{temporal acuity} with flicker
or clicks (a flicker fuses at about \qty{60}{Hz} in daylight vision);
(5) in an animal, record from single afferent fibres while applying
the same stimuli and match the neural to the behavioural thresholds
--- the psychophysics and the physiology should agree, and where they
do not the difference is what the brain adds.
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Other animals, other senses]\label{rem:b3:sensory-systems:others}
Every sense here has a version an animal has pushed further, and there
are senses we lack. Sharks and rays sense the microvolt fields of a
prey's heartbeat through jelly-filled canals; electric fish read
distortions of their own field to see in the dark. Pit vipers image
warm prey with infrared-sensitive pits; bats and dolphins echolocate,
timing echoes of their own calls to a few microseconds and steering the
call's frequency to track a moth; bees see ultraviolet and the
polarisation of skylight and navigate by it; migratory birds and
turtles sense the Earth's magnetic field by a mechanism still argued
over. A dog's nose has forty times our receptor neurons and a third
of its brain to read them; an owl hears a mouse under snow. The
principles do not change --- a transducer, a \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:principles}{labelled line}, a code,
compression, a map --- and the variety is what evolution has done with
them.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:1}
Explain how the brain knows whether a train of spikes comes from the
eye or the ear, given that the spikes are identical.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:2}
Why does light hyperpolarise a photoreceptor, and what is the dark
current?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:3}
A whisper is \qty{20}{dB}, a conversation \qty{60}{dB}, a rock concert
\qty{110}{dB}. Compute the intensity of each in W/m$^{2}$ and the
ratio between the loudest and the softest.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:4}
State the five \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:chemical}{taste} modalities and their receptor types, and explain
why a cold blocks the ``\omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:chemical}{taste}'' of food.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:5}
Weber's fraction for weight is $0.02$. Starting from a \qty{100}{g}
weight, how many just-noticeable steps lie between it and
\qty{10}{kg}? Use Fechner's formula and check by counting steps of
factor $1.02$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:6}
Using \cref{thm:b3:sensory-systems:hecht}, compute $P_{\text{see}}$ for
$a = 2$, $6$ and $12$ absorbed photons with a threshold of $n = 6$.
At what $a$ is the flash seen half the time? If \qty{10}{\%} of photons
at the cornea are absorbed, how many must the flash deliver?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:7}
The middle ear concentrates force from \qty{55}{mm^2} onto
\qty{3}{mm^2} with a lever of $1.3$. By what factor is the pressure
raised, and in \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{decibels}? Why is the ear of a person with fused
middle-ear bones (otosclerosis) duller by tens of \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{decibels}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:8}
Explain why the transduction current of a \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{hair cell} appears within
microseconds while that of a photoreceptor takes tens of
milliseconds, and what each sense gains from its design.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:9}
A mouse has \num{1100} receptor types and a human $400$. If an odour
activates about \qty{5}{\%} of the types, how many patterns of exactly
that size are available to each (use $\binom{R}{k}$ with $k = 0.05R$,
and Stirling's approximation $\ln\binom{R}{k} \approx R\,H(k/R)$ with
$H(p) = -p\ln p - (1-p)\ln(1-p)$)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:10}
A \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rod} isomerises a \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rhodopsin} spontaneously once every \qty{40}{s} in
the dark. In a patch of $500$ \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rods} observed for a \qty{0.1}{s} window,
what is the mean number of spontaneous events, and the probability
that six or more occur by chance? Explain why the brain sets the
threshold at about six and not at one.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:11}
The auditory nerve locks its spikes to the sound's phase up to
\qty{4}{kHz}, with a jitter of about \qty{20}{\micro s}. Sound travels
at \qty{340}{m/s} and the ears are \qty{20}{cm} apart. What is the
largest interaural delay, what angular resolution does a
\qty{10}{\micro s} discrimination give near the midline, and why does
the place code, not timing, serve above \qty{4}{kHz}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:sensory-systems:12}
\omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:principles}{Lateral inhibition} makes a uniform grey field next to a dark one look
lighter at the border. Model a row of receptors each excited by its own
light $I_{i}$ and inhibited by a fraction $w$ of each neighbour's,
$r_{i} = I_{i} - w(I_{i-1} + I_{i+1})$, and compute the response across
a step from $I = 1$ to $I = 2$ with $w = 0.2$. What does the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{retina} gain
and what does it lose by this?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A Candle Seen from Afar}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a candle's photons counted into
a distant eye, the flash's detection computed from Poisson statistics
and the rod's amplifier, a concert's decibels turned into eardrum
motion and hair-cell current, and a nose's code enumerated, ending on
the distance at which the candle is seen, the pressure at the eardrum
and the patterns a nose can form}]
\label{pb:b3:sensory-systems:1}
Data: a candle emits about \qty{0.01}{W} of visible light, at
\qty{550}{nm} (photon energy $3.6\times 10^{-19}$ J). A dark-adapted
pupil is \qty{7}{mm} across; \qty{10}{\%} of photons entering the eye
are absorbed by \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rhodopsin}; the eye integrates over \qty{0.1}{s}; the
threshold is $6$ absorbed photons. Each absorbed photon closes $200$
channels and reduces the dark current by \qty{1}{pA} for \qty{0.2}{s};
a \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rod}'s dark current is \qty{30}{pA}. Sound: $I_{0} =
\qty{1e-12}{W/m^2}$; the eardrum has area \qty{55}{mm^2}; a hair bundle
of \qty{5}{\micro m} height at threshold deflects by \qty{0.3}{nm}.
Olfaction: $R = 400$ receptors; a \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{hair cell}'s transduction current is
\qty{100}{pA} at saturation.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Photons.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many photons per second does the candle emit?
  \item At distance $d$ the photons are spread over a sphere of area
        $4\pi d^{2}$. How many enter the pupil per second at
        \qty{1}{km}? At \qty{10}{km}?
  \item How many are absorbed in one integration window at each
        distance? Is the candle seen (threshold $6$)?
  \item Find the distance at which the mean absorbed number in a window
        equals $6$. With Poisson statistics, what fraction of windows
        exceed threshold there?
  \item At the distance of question 4, what is the probability that a
        given \qty{0.1}{s} window contains no photon at all? Why does a
        faint star seem to flicker?
  \item Under a full moon the same \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rods} absorb $10^{4}$ photons per
        window from the sky. Explain why the candle at \qty{10}{km} is
        then invisible although it delivers the same photons.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rod}'s amplifier.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item One photon closes $200$ channels and reduces the current by
        \qty{1}{pA}. What current does one channel carry, and how many
        cations per second is that ($e = \qty{1.6e-19}{C}$)?
  \item What fraction of the dark current does one photon remove? How
        many simultaneous photons would shut the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rod} down completely
        (saturation)?
  \item The response lasts \qty{0.2}{s}. How many cations does one
        photon prevent from entering? What is the gain in charge per
        photon?
  \item In daylight $10^{5}$ photons a second reach a \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rod}. What happens
        to it, and why are \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{cones}, which adapt and saturate less, the
        daylight receptors?
  \item \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{Cones} need about $100$ photons to signal. Explain in terms of
        the trade between sensitivity and speed why \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{cones} respond in
        \qty{20}{ms} and \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rods} in \qty{200}{ms}.
  \item A \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rhodopsin} isomerises spontaneously once in \qty{40}{s}. Over
        the $10^{8}$ \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{rods} of an eye, how many false photons per second
        does the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:retina}{retina} generate, and why does that not blind the dark
        eye with noise? (Think of the threshold and the \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:principles}{receptive
        field}.)
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{Decibels}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A concert at \qty{110}{dB}: intensity in W/m$^{2}$ and power
        falling on one eardrum.
  \item How much acoustic energy does the eardrum receive in a two-hour
        concert? Compare with the energy of a falling raindrop
        (\qty{1e-4}{J}).
  \item The threshold of hearing at \qty{0}{dB}: power on the eardrum
        and energy per \qty{10}{ms} --- compare with the energy of one
        visible photon.
  \item The hair bundle deflects \qty{0.3}{nm} at threshold on a height
        of \qty{5}{\micro m}. What angle is that, in degrees? Compare
        the deflection with the diameter of a hydrogen atom
        (\qty{0.1}{nm}).
  \item Prolonged exposure above \qty{85}{dB} damages \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{hair cells}, and
        \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{hair cells} do not regenerate in mammals. Two hours at
        \qty{110}{dB} deliver as much energy as how many hours at
        \qty{85}{dB}?
  \item The range of hearing is \qty{120}{dB}. How many just-noticeable
        steps of loudness is that, if Weber's fraction for intensity is
        about $0.25$ (one \omterm{def:b3:sensory-systems:cochlea}{decibel})?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The code of smell.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item With $400$ receptors each on or off, how many patterns? Write
        the answer as a power of ten.
  \item If an odour typically activates $20$ receptors, how many
        distinct such patterns are there ($\binom{400}{20}$, use
        Stirling or logarithms)?
  \item Two odorants share $15$ of their $20$ receptors. Propose a
        measure of their distance in the code and explain why they
        smell alike.
  \item Humans discriminate about a trillion odours. What fraction of
        the patterns of question 20 is that, and what limits the number
        far below the code's capacity?
  \item A mouse with \num{1100} receptors: how many more patterns of
        $20$ than a human, as a power of ten?
  \item A mutation deletes one receptor gene. Predict its effect on
        the ability to smell in general and on the perception of one
        odorant that binds that receptor strongly (specific anosmia).
  \item Summarise: the distance at which the candle is seen
        (question~4), the acoustic energy on the eardrum at threshold
        in \qty{10}{ms} (question~15), and the number of twenty-receptor
        patterns a human nose can form (question~20).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
