\chapter{Animal Body Plans and Tissues}\label{ch:b1:body-plans-tissues}

Four animals on a laboratory bench: a hydra a centimetre long, an
earthworm, a crayfish, a frog. Under the differences of size and habit
they are built on a handful of shared decisions --- whether the body has
a front and a back, how many layers of cells it grew from, whether there
is a cavity around the gut, whether the body is made of repeated
segments, where the skeleton is --- and every one of them is built from
the same four kinds of \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue}. This chapter sets out the \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:bodyplan}{body plans} of
animals and the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} that build them, and ends where the previous
chapters left off: at the wall of a mammalian gut, seen as \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}
assembled into an \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ}.

\section{Body plans}

\begin{definition}[Symmetry and cephalisation]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:symmetry}
An animal has \emph{radial symmetry}\index{radial symmetry} when its
body is organised around a central axis with no left and right, like a
hydra or a jellyfish: it meets its environment equally from all sides,
and is usually fixed or drifting. It has \emph{bilateral
symmetry}\index{bilateral symmetry} when one plane divides it into
mirror halves, defining a front (anterior) and rear (posterior), a back
(dorsal) and a belly (ventral): the plan of an animal that moves head
first, with sense \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} and nervous centres concentrated at the front
(\omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:bodyplan}{cephalisation}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Germ layers, body cavities]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}
The embryo of an animal forms two or three layers of cells, the
\emph{germ layers}\index{germ layer}, from which all \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} derive:
the outer \emph{ectoderm}\index{ectoderm} (skin, nervous system), the
inner \emph{endoderm}\index{endoderm} (lining of the gut and its
outgrowths) and, in all but the sponges and cnidarians, a middle
\emph{mesoderm}\index{mesoderm} (muscle, skeleton, \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{connective tissue},
blood, kidneys, gonads). Cnidarians are \emph{diploblastic}, the rest
\emph{triploblastic}. Among triploblastic animals, the mesoderm may
fill the space between gut and body wall (\emph{acoelomate}: flatworms),
or leave a cavity between them: a \emph{coelom}\index{coelom} when the
cavity is entirely lined by mesoderm (annelids, molluscs, arthropods,
echinoderms, vertebrates). The coelom suspends the gut in its own
compartment, lets it move independently of the body wall, and gives the
soft-bodied a \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:segments}{hydrostatic skeleton}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Protostomes and deuterostomes]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:proto}
Among coelomate animals, the fate of the first opening of the embryonic
gut divides two great lineages: in \emph{protostomes}\index{protostome}
(annelids, molluscs, arthropods) it becomes the mouth, the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{coelom}
forms by splitting of the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{mesoderm}, and the early cleavages of the egg
are spiral and their fate fixed early; in
\emph{deuterostomes}\index{deuterostome} (echinoderms, vertebrates) it
becomes the anus, the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{coelom} forms by outpocketing of the gut, and the
early cells remain flexible in fate.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Segmentation, skeletons]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:segments}
A body is \emph{segmented}\index{segmentation} when it is built of
repeated units along its axis (the rings of an earthworm, the segments
of a crayfish, the vertebrae and muscle blocks of a fish), each with its
own set of muscles, nerves and often appendages. Segments may be alike
or specialised into regions (head, thorax, abdomen). The
\emph{skeleton}\index{skeleton}, against which the muscles pull, is
\emph{hydrostatic}\index{hydrostatic skeleton} (a fluid under pressure in
a \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{coelom}, as in the earthworm), an \emph{exoskeleton}\index{exoskeleton}
(a rigid \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{cuticle} outside the body, jointed, moulted to grow, as in
arthropods), or an \emph{endoskeleton}\index{endoskeleton} (cartilage or
bone inside the body, growing with it, as in vertebrates).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Reading an animal from its plan]\label{ex:b1:body-plans-tissues:reading}
An animal with a head end, a through gut, a body of similar rings and no
hard parts: a coelomate \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:proto}{protostome} moving by a \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:segments}{hydrostatic skeleton},
each segment's coelomic fluid pressurised by circular and longitudinal
muscles in turn --- an earthworm burrowing. An animal with a jointed
\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{cuticle} and specialised segments: an arthropod, whose growth must pass
through moults. Bilateral, \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:segments}{segmented} muscle blocks around a dorsal axis
of vertebrae: a vertebrate, whose \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:segments}{endoskeleton} grows continuously.
\end{example}

\section{The four tissue types}

\begin{definition}[Tissue]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}
A \emph{tissue}\index{tissue} is a group of cells of similar structure
and origin, together with the extracellular material they produce,
organised to perform a common function. Animal tissues fall into four
types: epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous. Every \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} is a
combination of at least two of them.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Epithelial tissue]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}
An \emph{epithelium}\index{epithelium} is a sheet of closely packed
cells covering a surface or lining a cavity, resting on a thin layer of
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{extracellular matrix}, the \emph{basal lamina}\index{basal lamina}. Its
cells are \emph{polarised}\index{cell polarity}: the apical face looks
at the outside or the lumen, the basal face at the lamina, and the two
carry different proteins. They are joined by \emph{cell
junctions}\index{cell junction}: \emph{tight junctions} near the apex,
which seal the space between cells so that nothing crosses the sheet
except through the cells; \emph{adherens junctions} and
\emph{desmosomes}, which fasten the cells mechanically; \emph{gap
junctions}, channels that let ions and small molecules pass from cell to
cell. Epithelia are classified by the number of layers (simple,
stratified) and the shape of the cells (squamous, cuboidal, columnar),
and include the glands, which are epithelial cells specialised in
secretion. They cover, protect, absorb and secrete.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Connective tissue]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}
\emph{Connective tissue}\index{connective tissue} is made of scattered
cells in an abundant \emph{extracellular matrix}\index{extracellular
matrix} that they secrete: protein fibres --- \emph{collagen}\index{collagen}
(tensile strength; a quarter of all the protein in a mammal),
\emph{elastin}\index{elastin} (recoil) --- embedded in a hydrated
\emph{ground substance} of polysaccharides. The proportions define its
kinds: loose connective \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} under epithelia and around \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs}
(fibroblasts, few fibres, much ground substance); dense connective
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} of tendons and ligaments (parallel collagen); adipose \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue}
(fat-storing cells); cartilage (a firm gel of collagen and
polysaccharides, no vessels); bone (collagen mineralised with calcium
phosphate, living cells in cavities, vessels); and blood, whose matrix
is the plasma. It binds, supports, stores, transports and defends.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Muscle tissue]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:muscle}
\emph{Muscle tissue}\index{muscle tissue} is made of elongated cells
packed with contractile protein filaments (actin and myosin). Three
kinds: \emph{skeletal muscle}, of long multinucleate fibres showing
cross-striations, attached to the skeleton and under voluntary control;
\emph{cardiac muscle}, of branched striated cells joined end to end by
junctions that pass the beat from cell to cell, contracting on its own
rhythm; \emph{smooth muscle}, of spindle-shaped unstriated cells in the
walls of hollow \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} (gut, vessels, uterus), contracting slowly and
involuntarily. The mechanism of contraction is treated in the Year 2
volume.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Nervous tissue]\label{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:nervous}
\emph{Nervous tissue}\index{nervous tissue} is made of
\emph{neurons}\index{neuron}, cells with a body and long extensions
(dendrites receiving signals, an axon carrying them away) that conduct
electrical signals and pass them to other cells at synapses, and of
\emph{glial cells}\index{glial cell}, several times more numerous, which
support, insulate and feed the neurons. It senses, integrates and
commands.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Where the tissues come from]\label{ex:b1:body-plans-tissues:origin}
Epithelia derive from all three \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{germ layers} (skin from \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{ectoderm}, gut
lining from \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{endoderm}, the lining of vessels and of the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{coelom} from
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{mesoderm}); \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{connective tissue}, muscle and blood from \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{mesoderm}; \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:nervous}{nervous
tissue} from \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{ectoderm}. The frog's skin is an ectodermal \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} on
mesodermal \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{connective tissue}; its gut, an endodermal \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} wrapped
in mesodermal muscle; its brain, \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{ectoderm} folded inward.
\end{example}

\section{From tissues to an organ: the wall of the gut}

\begin{proposition}[The four layers of the gut wall]\label{prop:b1:body-plans-tissues:gutwall}
\index{gut wall}
From the lumen outward, the wall of the vertebrate digestive tube is
built of four concentric layers, each a combination of \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}: the
\emph{mucosa} (an \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} --- absorptive and secretory --- on a loose
\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{connective tissue} carrying vessels and lymphatics, with a thin sheet of
smooth muscle beneath); the \emph{submucosa} (dense \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{connective tissue}
with larger vessels and a nerve network); the \emph{muscularis} (two
layers of smooth muscle, inner circular and outer longitudinal, with a
nerve network between them, producing the mixing and propulsive
movements); and the \emph{serosa} (a thin connective layer covered by
the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} of the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{coelom}). The same four layers run from oesophagus
to rectum; what changes is the mucosa --- folded into villi in the
small intestine, glandular in the stomach.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Reading a histological section]\label{met:b1:body-plans-tissues:histology}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Find the free surface or the lumen: the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} lining it is
        the first layer. Count its rows of nuclei (simple or
        stratified) and note the shape of the cells.
  \item Below the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} look for the pale, fibrous zone with
        scattered nuclei and vessels: \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{connective tissue}.
  \item Muscle appears as dense bundles of elongated cells: striated
        with peripheral nuclei (skeletal), striated and branched
        (cardiac), unstriated with a central nucleus (smooth). Note the
        direction of the fibres relative to the section.
  \item \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:nervous}{Nervous tissue} in an \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} wall appears as clusters of large
        pale \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:nervous}{neuron} bodies between the muscle layers.
  \item Name the \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organ} from the sequence of layers and the
        specialisations of the mucosa (villi, glands, keratin).
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[The villus as an exchange surface]\label{ex:b1:body-plans-tissues:villus}
A villus of the human small intestine is a finger \qty{0.6}{mm} high and
\qty{0.15}{mm} across, covered by a single layer of columnar cells whose
apical faces each carry some \qty{1500}{} microvilli. Villi multiply the
surface of the tube about sevenfold and microvilli twentyfold again:
the \qty{0.6}{m^2} of a plain tube becomes tens of square metres of
absorptive \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium}, one cell thick, each cell within a few
micrometres of a capillary and a lymphatic in the connective core of
its villus (\cref{ch:b1:digestion-absorption}). The \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} is
renewed from the glands at its base every four days: the sheet, its
junctions and its polarity are rebuilt continuously.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The tissues of the two model organisms]\label{rem:b1:body-plans-tissues:plants}
The plant's three \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} systems of \cref{ch:b1:flowering-plant-organization}
have no exact counterparts here. Its epidermis is an \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} in
function but its cells are held by walls, not junctions; its \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:tissues}{ground
tissue} is at once storage, support and photosynthetic; it has neither
muscle nor nerve. The animal's four \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} are the tool-kit of a body
that moves and senses; the plant's three, of a body that grows.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Wall of the Small Intestine}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a histological series of the human
small intestine read from the tube down to the microvillus: layers,
villi, cells, junctions and renewal, ending on the absorptive surface
of a single villus}]
\label{pb:b1:body-plans-tissues:1}
The human small intestine is a tube \qty{6}{m} long and \qty{3}{cm} in
internal diameter. Circular folds of its wall triple the surface of the
plain tube. Its mucosa bears \qty{25}{} villi per square millimetre of
folded surface, each a cylinder \qty{0.6}{mm} high and \qty{0.15}{mm} in
diameter. The villi are covered by columnar cells whose apical face is
a square of side \qty{5}{\micro m}; each apical face carries
\qty{1500}{} microvilli, cylinders \qty{1}{\micro m} long and
\qty{0.1}{\micro m} in diameter. The \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} is renewed every
\qty{4}{} days.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The layers.}
A transverse section is examined under the microscope.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item List the four layers of the wall from the lumen outward and the
        \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} types in each.
  \item The section shows villi. Which region of the digestive tube
        is it? What would a section of the stomach show instead?
  \item Between the two muscle layers of the muscularis lie clusters of
        large pale cells. What are they, and what do they control?
  \item Explain why the two muscle layers run in perpendicular
        directions.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Surfaces.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the internal surface of the plain tube.
  \item Compute the surface after the circular folds.
  \item Compute the lateral surface of one villus (cylinder, ignoring
        the tip).
  \item Compute the villus surface per square millimetre of folded
        mucosa and the factor by which the villi multiply the surface.
  \item Compute the surface after the villi.
  \item Compute the lateral surface of one microvillus and of the
        \qty{1500}{} on one cell.
  \item Compute the factor by which the microvilli multiply the apical
        surface of a cell.
  \item Compute the total absorptive surface of the small intestine.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Cells.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the number of epithelial cells covering one villus.
  \item Compute the number of villi in the small intestine and the
        number of epithelial cells covering them.
  \item The \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} is renewed every \qty{4}{} days. How many cells
        are shed into the lumen per day, and per second?
  \item Where are the replacement cells produced, and what does this
        say about the position of the dividing cells relative to the
        absorbing ones?
  \item An epithelial cell is \qty{25}{\micro m} tall. Compute the
        volume of \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} in the small intestine and its mass
        (density \qty{1.05}{g/cm^3}).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Junctions, matrix and the villus.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Each cell is sealed to its neighbours by a tight junction
        running around its apex. Compute the length of tight junction
        per cell and the total length in the small intestine.
  \item Explain why a substance in the lumen can reach the blood only
        by crossing two cell membranes.
  \item The core of the villus is loose \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{connective tissue} with a
        capillary network and a central lymphatic vessel. Name the two
        kinds of extracellular fibre it contains and the cell that
        secretes them.
  \item Name the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:layers}{germ layer} of origin of the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:epithelium}{epithelium} and of the
        core.
  \item The villus also contains a strand of smooth muscle that makes
        it shorten rhythmically. Propose its function.
  \item A disease flattens the villi (the surface returns to that of
        the folded tube). By what factor does the absorptive surface
        fall, and what are the consequences?
  \item Compute the absorptive surface of one villus with its
        microvilli, in square millimetres.
  \item State the result: the absorptive surface of a single villus,
        and the number of villi in the small intestine.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
