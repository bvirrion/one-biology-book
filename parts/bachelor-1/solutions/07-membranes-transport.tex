\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b1:membranes-transport} --- Membranes and Membrane Transport}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:1}
A bilayer of phospholipids, tails inward, with cholesterol, forms a
\qty{5}{nm} sheet. Proteins are embedded across it or attached to one
face, as separate units. Lipids and most proteins diffuse laterally in
the plane, which is a two-dimensional fluid. The two leaflets differ
in lipid composition, and the sugar chains are on the outer face only.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:2}
$\mathrm{O_2}$ (small, non-polar) $>$ ethanol (small, weakly polar)
$>$ water (small, polar) $\gg$ glucose (large, polar) $\gg$
$\mathrm{Na^+}$ (charged, hydrated). Permeability falls with polarity,
size and above all charge, because the solute must dissolve in the
hydrophobic core.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:3}
Sucrose: $-2.48\times 0.1 = \qty{-0.25}{MPa}$. NaCl dissociates into
two ions, \qty{0.2}{osmol/L}: \qty{-0.50}{MPa}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:4}
$E_{Cl} = (61.5/(-1))\log(120/4) = -61.5\times 1.48 = \qty{-91}{mV}$.
At \qty{-89}{mV} chloride is within \qty{2}{mV} of equilibrium: it is
distributed passively.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:5}
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{Cell} surface $\num{4.7e9}\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m^2} =
\qty{0.47}{m^2}$; ratio $0.92/0.47 = 1.96 \approx 2$: the lipid covers
the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} twice, so the membrane is a bilayer.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:6}
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{Cell} $\Psi = -0.9 + 0.5 = \qty{-0.4}{MPa}$; solution \qty{-0.6}{MPa}:
water \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}. Equilibrium when $\Psi_{\text{cell}} = -0.6$:
$\Psi_p = -0.6 + 0.9 = \qty{0.3}{MPa}$; the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} loses some \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:plantcell}{turgor} but
stays turgid.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:7}
Doubling the concentration from 10 to 20 raises the rate by only
\qty{15}{\%}: saturation. Plotting $1/v$ against $1/c$ (or noting that
$v = 1.0$ at \qty{5}{mmol/L} and about $1.67$ at infinite $c$) gives
$V_{\max} \approx \qty{1.7}{\micro mol/min}$ and half-maximum at about
\qty{3}{mmol/L}: \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{facilitated diffusion} by a carrier.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:8}
At rest the membrane's permeability is dominated by open potassium
\omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels}; potassium leaks out until the inside is negative enough to
hold it, i.e.\ near $E_K$. If sodium \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels} opened, the permeability
would be dominated by sodium and the potential would swing toward
$E_{Na}$, about \qty{+60}{mV} --- the action potential.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:9}
Per mole $\mathrm{Na^+}$ in: $RT\ln(12/145) + F(-0.060) = -6.4 - 5.8
= \qty{-12.2}{kJ}$; two: \qty{-24.4}{kJ}. Glucose uphill costs
$RT\ln(c_{\text{in}}/c_{\text{out}})$; equality gives
$\ln(c_{\text{in}}/c_{\text{out}}) = 24\,400/2577 = 9.5$, a ratio of
about \num{13000}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:10}
Fluidity falls (tails pack, the bilayer approaches a gel); oxygen
diffusion slows but continues; the pump, an enzyme, nearly stops;
the leaks continue, so over hours sodium enters and potassium \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}
and the gradients decay; the potential falls toward zero as the
gradients fade; with sodium entering and the proteins' osmotic pull no
longer balanced, water enters and the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} swells --- cold-stored \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}
and \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} swell for exactly this reason.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:11}
Mixing at \qty{37}{\celsius} but not at \qty{4}{\celsius} implies a
temperature-dependent process --- diffusion in a fluid that becomes
solid in the cold. No mixing without ATP would imply an active,
energy-driven redistribution (motors, vesicle cycling) rather than
diffusion. The corrected result, that mixing needs no ATP, is what the
\omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:membrane}{fluid mosaic model} requires: proteins move by thermal diffusion in a
two-dimensional fluid, without the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} doing work.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:membranes-transport:12}
The potential is the value at which the electrical force balances the
diffusion of the most permeant ion, given by Nernst; the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} sets the
gradients (by the pump) and the permeabilities (by its \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels}), and
the potential follows. A membrane of \qty{1}{\micro F/cm^2} at
\qty{-70}{mV} carries $\qty{7e-8}{C/cm^2}$, about $\num{4e11}$ ions per
square centimetre --- for a \qty{20}{\micro m} \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} some $10^5$ ions,
against $10^{10}$ potassium ions inside: a negligible fraction of the
ions, moving a negligible distance, charges the capacitor. The
gradients are the store; the potential is the reading on it, and the
pump's direct contribution (three charges out for two in) is a few
millivolts.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b1:membranes-transport:1}
\textbf{1.} $E_{Na} = 26.7\ln(145/12) = \qty{+66}{mV}$; $E_K =
26.7\ln(5/140) = \qty{-89}{mV}$.
\textbf{2.} $\mathrm{K^+}$ (\qty{29}{mV} away) is nearer than
$\mathrm{Na^+}$ (\qty{126}{mV} away): the membrane is much more
permeable to $\mathrm{K^+}$.
\textbf{3.} $\mathrm{Na^+}$ in: $RT\ln(12/145) + F(-0.060) = -6.4 -
5.8 = \qty{-12.2}{kJ/mol}$. $\mathrm{K^+}$ out: $RT\ln(5/140) -
F(-0.060) = -8.6 + 5.8 = \qty{-2.8}{kJ/mol}$.
\textbf{4.} Cost $= 3\times 12.2 + 2\times 2.8 = \qty{42.2}{kJ}$ per
cycle against \qty{50}{kJ} per ATP: efficiency \qty{84}{\%}.
\textbf{5.} The pump keeps cytosolic sodium low; the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} on the
apical face uses the inward sodium gradient to pull glucose in from
the lumen; the pump must be on the other face so that the sodium it
exports goes to the blood, not back into the lumen, which would leave
the lumen's sodium to recycle and the net movement zero.
\textbf{6.} $2\times 12.2 = \qty{24.4}{kJ/mol}$ of glucose.
\textbf{7.} $\Delta G = RT\ln(c_C/c_L)$ (glucose is uncharged).
\textbf{8.} $\ln(c_C/c_L) = 24\,400/2577 = 9.47$; $c_C/c_L =
\num{1.3e4}$.
\textbf{9.} Up to \qty{1300}{mmol/L} in principle: the ratio is never
reached because glucose \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} through the basolateral carrier; in
practice the \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol} stays near \qtyrange{5}{20}{mmol/L}.
\textbf{10.} The \omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol} (above \qty{5}{mmol/L}) is more concentrated
than the blood; the carrier lets glucose diffuse down that gradient
into the blood, passively.
\textbf{11.} Reversed, the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} would pump glucose from the blood
into the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} and the carrier would let it leak into the lumen: the
gut would secrete glucose. Polarity of the transporters is the
direction of absorption.
\textbf{12.} The \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} takes up sodium only with glucose; glucose
in the lumen therefore drives sodium absorption through it even when
the cholera toxin blocks other routes; chloride follows sodium
electrically and water follows the salt osmotically.
\textbf{13.} $\qty{4e-15}{mol/s}$ of $\mathrm{Na^+}$, i.e.\
$\num{2.4e9}$ ions per second; at three per cycle, $\num{8e8}$ cycles
per second.
\textbf{14.} $\num{8e8}$ ATP per second $= \qty{1.3e-15}{mol/s}$; at
30 ATP per glucose, \qty{4.4e-17}{mol/s} of glucose, \qty{2.2}{\%} of
the glucose absorbed.
\textbf{15.} $\num{1.2e9}$ glucose per second at 50 per \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter}:
$\num{2.4e7}$ \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporters}; membrane \qty{500}{\micro m^2}: \num{48000}
per square micrometre, i.e.\ one per \qty{20}{nm} square --- a
membrane packed with transporters.
\textbf{16.} Osmoles entering: $4\times\qty{2e-15}{mol/s} =
\qty{8e-15}{osmol/s}$; at \qty{0.3}{osmol/L}, water
$= \qty{2.7e-14}{L/s} = \qty{27}{\micro m^3/s}$. \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{Cell} volume
$25\times 5\times 5 = \qty{625}{\micro m^3}$: doubled in \qty{23}{s}.
The water \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} as fast as it enters, across the basolateral
membrane, and this is how the gut absorbs water.
\textbf{17.} $\qty{2.7e-14}{L/s}$ of water is \qty{1.5e-12}{mol/s}; per glucose ($\qty{2e-15}{mol/s}$): about 750 water molecules.
\textbf{18.} $S = 2(25\times 5 + 25\times 5 + 5\times 5) =
\qty{550}{\micro m^2} = \qty{5.5e-6}{cm^2}$; $C = \qty{5.5e-12}{F}$.
\textbf{19.} $Q = CV = 5.5\times 10^{-12}\times 0.060 =
\qty{3.3e-13}{C}$; $/\num{1.6e-19} = \num{2.1e6}$ ions.
\textbf{20.} $\num{2.1e6}/550 = 3800$ ions per square micrometre, one excess charge per \qty{16}{nm} square of membrane.
\textbf{21.} $\mathrm{K^+}$ in the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}: $0.140\,\mathrm{mol/L}\times
\qty{6.25e-13}{L}\times\num{6e23} = \num{5.3e10}$ ions; the charge
needs \qty{0.004}{\%} of them: the concentrations are unchanged by
charging the membrane.
\textbf{22.} Net charge per cycle $+e$ out: current $\num{8e8}\times
\num{1.6e-19} = \qty{1.3e-10}{A}$; in one second the membrane would
gain \qty{1.3e-10}{C}, i.e.\ $\Delta V = Q/C = \qty{24}{V}$. It does
not, because every charge pumped out is immediately balanced by ions
flowing back through \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels} (mainly $\mathrm{K^+}$ leaking in less,
or $\mathrm{Na^+}$ re-entering): the membrane's conductance shorts
the pump's current, and the pump adds only millivolts.
\textbf{23.} Cytosolic sodium rises over minutes; the \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter} loses
its driving force and glucose absorption stops; the potential decays
toward zero as the gradients run down; sodium and water enter and the
\omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} swells.
\textbf{24.} With the potassium leak blocked, the sodium leak
dominates: the potential moves toward $E_{Na}$, becoming much less
negative (depolarisation).
\textbf{25.} A ratio of about \num{13000} (\omterm{def:b1:eukaryotic-cell:organelle}{cytosol} over lumen), set by
two sodium ions per glucose at \qty{-60}{mV}; made possible by the
\omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{sodium--potassium pump} (basolateral) and the sodium--glucose \omterm{prop:b1:membranes-transport:secondary}{symporter}
(apical).
\end{solution}
