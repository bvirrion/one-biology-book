\chapter{The Chemical Makeup of Living Things}\label{ch:g10:chemistry-of-life}

Leave a slice of bread too long on the grill and it turns black: what is
left is mostly carbon. Weigh a fresh apple slice, dry it for a day in a
warm oven, weigh it again: five sixths of its mass has left as water
vapour. Bread, apple, oak, jellyfish, you --- every living thing is
built, at the bottom, from the same short list of chemical ingredients,
and that list is not the list the rocks are made of. This chapter takes
living matter apart, ingredient by ingredient, and asks what makes it
distinctive.

\section{Elements: the same atoms as the rocks, in different proportions}

\begin{definition}[Chemical composition of living matter]\label{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:composition}
The \emph{chemical composition}\index{chemical composition} of a
sample of living matter is the list of the chemical elements it
contains, with the fraction of the sample's mass that each one
represents. It is measured by taking the sample apart chemically ---
burning it, weighing what evaporates and what remains, analysing the
gases released --- not by looking at it.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Four elements make up 96\% of a living body]\label{prop:g10:chemistry-of-life:four}
By mass, living matter is made almost entirely of four elements:
oxygen (O), carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and nitrogen (N). In the human
body they account for about 65\%, 18.5\%, 9.5\% and 3.2\% of the mass
--- 96\% together. Calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, sodium,
chlorine and magnesium share most of the remaining 4\%, and a dozen
\emph{trace elements} (iron, zinc, iodine, copper\dots) are present at
far below 0.1\% yet are indispensable. The same four elements dominate
in a bacterium, a wheat plant or a whale, in similar proportions.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Living matter versus rock]\label{ex:g10:chemistry-of-life:crust}
The Earth's crust is 46\% oxygen too, but its next elements are silicon
(28\%), aluminium (8\%) and iron (5.6\%). Carbon makes up 0.02\% of the
crust and nitrogen 0.002\%, against 18.5\% and 3.2\% of a body. A living
thing is therefore not a sample of its surroundings: it
\emph{concentrates} a few elements by a factor of a thousand or more.
That selection is the first chemical signature of life.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Where the atoms come from]\label{rem:g10:chemistry-of-life:origin}
Every one of those atoms is ordinary: the carbon of your muscles was
carbon dioxide of the air before a plant fixed it, the nitrogen of your
\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} was nitrogen of the air before soil bacteria captured it. What
is special about life is not the atoms but the way they are assembled
and the proportions in which they are gathered.
\end{remark}

\section{Water and mineral salts}

\begin{definition}[Mineral matter and organic matter]\label{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:mineral-organic}
The molecules of a living thing fall into two groups.
\emph{Mineral matter}\index{mineral matter} is made of molecules that
also exist outside living things: water and the dissolved
\emph{mineral salts}\index{mineral salts} (ions such as
$\mathrm{Na^+}$, $\mathrm{K^+}$, $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$, $\mathrm{Cl^-}$,
phosphate and bicarbonate). \emph{Organic matter}\index{organic matter}
is made of molecules built on a skeleton of carbon atoms bonded to
hydrogen, and usually to oxygen and nitrogen --- molecules that, on
Earth, are made only by living things or from their remains.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Water is the main constituent]\label{prop:g10:chemistry-of-life:water}
Water is the most abundant molecule of every living thing: about 60\%
to 65\% of an adult human's mass, 75\% of a newborn's, 85\% of an
apple's, 95\% of a jellyfish's, but only 13\% of a dormant wheat grain.
The water content of a sample is measured by weighing it, drying it at
about \qty{105}{\celsius} until its mass stops falling, and weighing
it again: the lost mass is the water.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Drying an apple slice]\label{ex:g10:chemistry-of-life:apple}
A slice of fresh apple of mass \qty{24.0}{g} is dried until its mass
settles at \qty{3.6}{g}. Water lost: $24.0 - 3.6 = \qty{20.4}{g}$, i.e.
$20.4/24.0 = 0.85$, or 85\% of the fresh mass. The \qty{3.6}{g} that
remain are the \emph{dry matter}: organic molecules plus \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:mineral-organic}{mineral salts}.
Burn that dry matter and about \qty{0.1}{g} of pale ash --- the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:mineral-organic}{mineral
salts} --- survives; the rest, the organic part, has left as carbon
dioxide and water vapour.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[What water does]\label{prop:g10:chemistry-of-life:water-roles}
Water is where the chemistry of a cell happens: it dissolves salts,
sugars and most small organic molecules, so that they can meet and
react; it carries them from one place to another (blood plasma is 90\%
water, sap much more); and because it takes a lot of energy to heat,
it buffers the temperature of the organism against sudden change. A
loss of 10\% of the body's water is a medical emergency; a dormant
seed, at 13\%, has simply switched its chemistry off until water
returns.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Mineral salts you can name]\label{ex:g10:chemistry-of-life:salts}
Calcium phosphate stiffens bone and teeth (calcium is 1.5\% of body
mass, almost all in the skeleton). Sodium and chloride ions set the
salinity of blood, close to \qty{9}{g/L}; potassium ions are
concentrated inside cells; iron sits at the heart of haemoglobin;
iodine is needed to make the hormone of the thyroid gland. A mineral
salt is never a source of energy, but a body deprived of one of them
fails at a precise task.
\end{example}

\section{The organic molecules: four families}

\begin{definition}[Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids]\label{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}
Organic molecules belong to four families.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Carbohydrates}\index{carbohydrate} (sugars) contain
        carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. The simple sugars are single
        units, such as glucose $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$; the complex
        carbohydrates are chains of hundreds or thousands of glucose
        units --- starch and cellulose in plants, glycogen in animals.
  \item \emph{Lipids}\index{lipid} (fats and oils) also contain only
        C, H and O, but with much less oxygen, and they do not dissolve
        in water. The commonest are the triglycerides, each made of one
        glycerol molecule joined to three long fatty-acid chains.
  \item \emph{Proteins}\index{protein} are chains of
        \emph{amino acids}\index{amino acid}: twenty kinds of amino
        acid, all containing nitrogen, linked in a precise order into
        chains of fifty to several thousand units that fold into a
        definite shape.
  \item \emph{Nucleic acids}\index{nucleic acid} (DNA and RNA) are
        chains of nucleotides, containing phosphorus as well as C, H,
        O and N; they store and transmit genetic information, and are
        the subject of \cref{ch:g10:universal-dna}.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Reading a food label]\label{ex:g10:chemistry-of-life:label}
A label on wholemeal bread reads, per \qty{100}{g}: \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrates}
\qty{41}{g} (of which sugars \qty{3}{g}), fat \qty{3}{g}, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}
\qty{9}{g}, fibre \qty{7}{g}, salt \qty{1.1}{g}. The rest --- about
\qty{39}{g} --- is water. "\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{Carbohydrates}" here is mostly starch, "sugars"
the simple ones, "fibre" is cellulose (a \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate} we cannot
digest), "fat" the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids}, "salt" a mineral salt. A label is a chemical
analysis, rounded.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[What each family is for]\label{prop:g10:chemistry-of-life:roles}
The families divide the work of a cell, with overlaps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrates} are the everyday fuel (glucose) and its short-term
        store (starch, glycogen), and cellulose is the building
        material of plant cell walls;
  \item \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids} are the compact long-term energy store (\qty{38}{kJ} per
        gram, against \qty{17}{kJ} per gram for \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrates} and
        \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins}), and membrane \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids} form the envelope of every cell;
  \item \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} are the machines: the enzymes that run every reaction,
        the fibres that contract muscle, the carriers, the antibodies,
        the receptors;
  \item \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{nucleic acids} hold the instructions for building the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins}.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{remark}[Large molecules and the shape of life]\label{rem:g10:chemistry-of-life:macromolecules}
The molecules of rock are small and repetitive (a crystal of quartz is
$\mathrm{SiO_2}$ repeated without end). The characteristic molecules of
life are huge --- a single \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} can contain ten thousand atoms, a
DNA molecule billions --- and \emph{informative}: the order of their
building blocks carries a message. Nothing in the mineral world
resembles a molecule whose sequence means something.
\end{remark}

\section{Identifying the constituents}

\begin{method}[Standard tests for the families]\label{met:g10:chemistry-of-life:tests}
Each family has a simple test that gives a visible result.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Water}: a strip of blue cobalt chloride paper turns pink;
        or dry the sample and measure the mass lost.
  \item \emph{Starch}: a drop of iodine solution turns from
        yellow-brown to blue-black.
  \item \emph{Simple sugars} (glucose, fructose\dots): heat with
        Fehling's or Benedict's reagent; the blue solution gives a
        brick-red precipitate.
  \item \emph{\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{Proteins}}: the biuret test --- a few drops of copper
        sulfate in alkaline solution turn violet.
  \item \emph{\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{Lipids}}: the sample leaves a translucent, non-drying spot
        on paper; the red dye Sudan III stains it.
  \item \emph{\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:mineral-organic}{Mineral salts}}: burn the dry matter completely; the ash
        that survives is mineral. Specific ions are identified by
        precipitation (silver nitrate for chloride, for instance).
\end{enumerate}
Always run a control with pure water, and one with a known positive
sample, to be sure the reagent works and that a colour change means
what you think.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Analysing a potato]\label{ex:g10:chemistry-of-life:potato}
A raw potato slice: cobalt chloride paper turns pink (water); iodine
turns blue-black at once (starch, abundant); Fehling's gives only a
faint orange tinge (little free sugar); biuret gives a pale violet
(some \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}, about 2\% of the fresh mass); no translucent spot
(almost no \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipid}). Drying leaves 22\% of the mass; burning that leaves
1\% of ash. The potato is a starch store wrapped in water --- exactly
what a plant needs to survive the winter underground.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Unity of composition]\label{prop:g10:chemistry-of-life:unity}
Whatever the organism --- bacterium, mushroom, plant, animal --- the
same tests find the same four families, made of the same building
blocks: the same twenty \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acids}, the same glucose, the same four
nucleotides. A living thing is distinctive not by owning special atoms
or special building blocks, but by the proportions it maintains, the
very large molecules it assembles, and the information those molecules
carry. This chemical unity is one of the strongest arguments that all
living things share a common origin, an argument \cref{ch:g10:common-ancestry}
will take up.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Problem: The Carbon in a Human Being}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- from a wheat field to a body: an
audit of matter, from the water in a grain to the number of kilograms of
carbon a person carries}]
\label{pb:g10:chemistry-of-life:1}
A class follows matter from a field of wheat to the body of one of its
students. Data: a wheat grain is 13\% water, and its dry matter is
about 70\% starch, 12\% \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}, 2\% \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipid}, 2\% mineral, the rest
cellulose. Bread flour keeps roughly the composition of the grain's
inner part. The student, Lea, has a mass of \qty{60}{kg}; use the
adult composition of the second figure (water 60\%, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} 17\%,
\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids} 16\%, minerals 6\%, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrates} 1\%). Element mass fractions
inside each family: take \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrates} as 40\% carbon, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids} as 77\%
carbon, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} as 53\% carbon and 16\% nitrogen.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The grain.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A grain of mass \qty{45}{mg} is dried. What mass of water does
        it lose, and what is its dry mass?
  \item Compute the masses of starch, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} and \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipid} in the dry
        matter of one grain.
  \item Which tests of \cref{met:g10:chemistry-of-life:tests} would
        confirm each of these three constituents on a crushed grain?
  \item The grain is planted and watered; three days later the seedling
        is 88\% water. Explain why germination cannot start at 13\%.
  \item A hectare yields \qty{8}{t} of grain. What mass of water is
        harvested with it, and what mass of dry matter?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The bread.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A loaf is baked from \qty{500}{g} of flour, \qty{320}{g} of
        water and \qty{10}{g} of salt; it loses \qty{130}{g} of water
        in the oven. What is the mass of the baked loaf, and its water
        content in per cent?
  \item Using the grain's dry composition for the flour, estimate the
        masses of starch and \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} in the loaf.
  \item Using \qty{17}{kJ/g} for \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrates} and \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins} and
        \qty{38}{kJ/g} for \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipids}, estimate the energy content of the

  \item Lea eats \qty{120}{g} of this bread. What fraction of a daily

\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The body: water and families.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the mass of water in Lea's body.
  \item Compute the masses of \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}, \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{lipid}, mineral and \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{carbohydrate}.
  \item Lea loses \qty{1.2}{kg} by sweating during a long run. What
        fraction of her body water is that? Why must it be replaced
        quickly?
  \item Her blood, about \qty{4.5}{L}, is 55\% plasma, and plasma is
        90\% water. Estimate the mass of water in her blood (take
        \qty{1}{kg} per litre). What fraction of her body water is
        that? Where is the rest?
  \item A newborn is 75\% water. Explain in one sentence why an infant
        is more vulnerable to dehydration than an adult.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Using the carbon fractions of each family, compute the mass of

        total mass of carbon.
  \item What fraction of her body mass is carbon? Compare with the
        18.5\% of \cref{prop:g10:chemistry-of-life:four} and comment

  \item Compute the mass of nitrogen in her \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{proteins}. Where did that

  \item Water is 89\% oxygen by mass. Compute the mass of oxygen

  \item State the result in one sentence: how many kilograms of carbon

\end{enumerate}
