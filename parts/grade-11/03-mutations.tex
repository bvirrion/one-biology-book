\chapter{Mutations and Genetic Variation}\label{ch:g11:mutations}

A child is born with no pigment at all --- white hair, pale skin, eyes
that cannot bear bright light --- to two ordinary parents from a family
with no history of the condition. A colony of bacteria that has never
met an antibiotic contains, out of a hundred million \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, a few that
already resist it. A gardener finds a single branch of a rose bush
bearing flowers of a different colour. In each case a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} sequence has
changed, once, in one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}; and in each case the change is copied
faithfully thereafter. This chapter is about those changes: what they
are, where they come from, how the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} limits them, and why life
depends on their never quite stopping.

\section{What a mutation is}

\begin{definition}[Mutation]\label{def:g11:mutations:mutation}
A \emph{mutation}\index{mutation} is a change in the sequence of
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} of a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} molecule, transmitted to the descendants of the
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} in which it occurs. The commonest are \emph{point mutations},
affecting one or a few \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{substitution}: one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide} replaced by another;
  \item an \emph{insertion}: one or more \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} added;
  \item a \emph{deletion}: one or more \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides} removed.
\end{itemize}
Larger changes --- a segment duplicated, inverted, moved, or a whole
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome} gained or lost --- are mutations too.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Where mutations happen, and to whom]\label{prop:g11:mutations:where}
A \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} occurring in a body \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} (\emph{somatic \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}}) is passed
to the descendants of that \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} only: a patch of tissue, at most, in
one individual. A \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} in a germ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} --- a gamete or one of its
precursors --- is passed to the offspring conceived from it and to
every one of their \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}: a \emph{germline \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}} is the only kind
that enters the heredity of the species. The rose bush's odd branch
is somatic; the pigmentless child's condition came from a germline
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} in some ancestor, carried silently for generations.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[The frequency of mutation]\label{ex:g11:mutations:frequency}
Replication leaves about one error per $10^9$ \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides}
(\cref{ch:g11:dna-replication}). A \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} of $\num{3000}$ base pairs is
therefore miscopied about once in every $\num{300000}$ replications;
a human, whose genome is copied some $10^{16}$ times in a lifetime,
accumulates \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} in every \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} in many \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. Each child is born
with some 60 new \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} that neither parent carried --- almost all
in the 98\% of the genome that is not \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}.
\end{example}

\section{Causes: spontaneous and induced}

\begin{proposition}[Spontaneous mutations]\label{prop:g11:mutations:spontaneous}
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{Mutations} arise in every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, in the absence of any external agent:
from the rare mispairings that escape the polymerase's checking, and
from the chemical instability of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} itself, whose bases are damaged
thousands of times a day per \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} by the water and the reactive
molecules of ordinary \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{metabolism}. Such \emph{spontaneous \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}}
are \emph{random}: they occur at any position, at a rate that does not
depend on whether the change would be useful to the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Lederberg (1952) spread bacteria never exposed to an antibiotic on a
plate, let them grow into colonies, then pressed a velvet pad onto the
plate and stamped its imprint --- the same colonies in the same
positions --- onto several plates containing the antibiotic. The few
resistant colonies appeared at the \emph{same positions} on every
antibiotic plate. Since they came from the same colonies of the
original plate, which had never seen the antibiotic, the resistance
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} existed before the exposure: the antibiotic revealed it and
did not cause it. The \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} had occurred at random during the
growth of the colonies, without regard to their future usefulness.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Mutagen]\label{def:g11:mutations:mutagen}
A \emph{mutagen}\index{mutagen} is a physical or chemical agent that
raises the rate of \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} by damaging \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}. Ultraviolet light welds
neighbouring thymines together on a strand; X-rays and radioactivity
break the strands; chemicals such as the benzopyrene of tobacco smoke
or the aflatoxin of mouldy grain attach to bases and distort the
helix. A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} exposed to a mutagen carries \emph{induced \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}}
in addition to its spontaneous ones --- still at random positions,
only more of them.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Ultraviolet on yeast]\label{ex:g11:mutations:uv}
Yeast \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} spread on plates and exposed to ultraviolet light for
increasing times: after \qty{10}{s} half the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} fail to form a
colony, after \qty{20}{s} a quarter, after \qty{30}{s} an eighth ---
each dose halves the survivors. Among the survivors, the proportion of
colonies with a visibly changed trait (colour, shape, inability to
grow on a certain sugar) rises from one in a million to one in a
thousand. The lamp kills by damaging \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} beyond repair, and mutates by
damaging it just short of that.
\end{example}

\section{The cell fights back: repair}

\begin{proposition}[DNA repair]\label{prop:g11:mutations:repair}
A \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} suffers tens of thousands of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} lesions a day and keeps almost
none of them: dedicated \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme} systems patrol the \omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:helix}{double helix},
recognise a damaged or mispaired base, cut out the faulty segment of
one strand and rebuild it using the other strand as template --- the
\omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:helix}{complementarity} of \cref{ch:g10:universal-dna} once again. A lesion
becomes a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} only if it escapes repair before the next
replication copies it. Repair is why the \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} rate is $10^{-9}$
and not $10^{-5}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
People with the hereditary condition xeroderma pigmentosum lack one of
the \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzymes} that excise ultraviolet damage. Their skin, exposed to
ordinary daylight, develops cancers a thousand times more often than
normal skin and from early childhood; protected from the sun, it is
normal. The same ultraviolet, the same lesions --- but without the
repair the lesions stay, and the \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} accumulate in the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}
that control \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} division (\cref{ch:g11:cancer}). Yeast and bacterial
strains lacking repair \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzymes} show the same hypersensitivity in the
laboratory.
\end{proof}

\section{Consequences of a mutation}

\begin{proposition}[From sequence to phenotype]\label{prop:g11:mutations:consequences}
A \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} changes an \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} into a new \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}. Its effect depends on
where it falls and what it does to the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} encodes
(\cref{ch:g11:gene-expression} gives the rules):
\begin{itemize}
  \item outside any \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, or at a position that does not change the
        \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}: \emph{no effect} --- the great majority;
  \item within a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, changing one \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{amino acid}: a \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} that works
        slightly differently, or not at all, or occasionally better;
  \item within a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, shifting or truncating the message: usually a
        \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} that does not work.
\end{itemize}
Whether a changed \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein} is harmful, neutral or useful depends on the
environment: the same \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of a haemoglobin \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is a burden in a
region without malaria and a protection where malaria is present.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Pigment lost, pigment kept]\label{ex:g11:mutations:pigment}
The pigment melanin is made by an \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme}. Dozens of different
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} of its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} are known, each producing a non-working \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme}
and, in a person carrying two such \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles}, the pigmentless
condition of the chapter's opening. Every one of them arose at random,
once, in a germ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}; the visible condition appears only when two
carriers pass their silent \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} to the same child. The same \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene},
in another \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}, makes a slightly less active \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme} --- and lighter
skin, common in populations that lived for millennia at high
latitudes, where a little less pigment lets the skin make more vitamin
D from a weak sun.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Mutation, the source of diversity]\label{prop:g11:mutations:source}
Every \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} of every \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} began as a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} of an earlier \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}.
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{Mutation} is the only process that creates new \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{genetic information};
the sexual shuffling of the final year only recombines what \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}
has made. Rare, random and mostly neutral or harmful in the
individual, \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} is nevertheless what supplies, over the
generations, the variation on which the evolution of
\cref{ch:g10:biodiversity-scales} depends.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Analysing a mutation]\label{met:g11:mutations:analyse}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compare the mutant sequence with the original, letter by
        letter, and name the change (substitution, insertion,
        deletion; how many \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotides}).
  \item Locate it: in a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} or outside; if in a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, whether the
        reading of the message is shifted.
  \item Identify the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} it occurred in: somatic (one individual, one
        tissue) or germline (transmitted).
  \item Look for a cause if the rate is unusual (\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutagen}{mutagen} exposure,
        defective repair); otherwise attribute it to chance.
  \item Judge the consequence at the level of the \omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}, the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell},
        the organism and the population --- in that order, and in a
        stated environment.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Randomness, precisely]\label{rem:g11:mutations:random}
"Random" does not mean that all positions mutate equally --- some
sequences are more fragile --- nor that \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} are causeless. It
means that a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}'s occurrence is independent of whether it would
help the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}: the antibiotic-resistant bacteria of the replica plates
were not made resistant by the antibiotic, and no organism can
"decide" to mutate in a useful direction. Selection does the
directing, afterwards; \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} only supplies.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Velvet Stamp}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- Lederberg's replica plating
reconstructed: colonies counted, resistances mapped, the origin of the

\label{pb:g11:mutations:1}
A student spreads about 200 bacteria, from a culture that has never met
the antibiotic streptomycin, on a plate without antibiotic. After a
night, 200 colonies have grown, each from one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} and each containing
about $10^7$ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. A velvet pad is pressed on the plate and stamped
onto three plates containing streptomycin. The next day, plate A shows
two resistant colonies, plate B two, plate C two --- at the same two
positions on all three.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The experiment.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Why must the colonies on the original plate come from single
        \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, and why is the number of bacteria spread kept small?
  \item Explain what the velvet transfers, and why the position of a
        colony is preserved.
  \item Two positions out of 200 give resistant colonies on every
        replica. What does the agreement between the three replicas
        show?
  \item Suppose instead that the antibiotic induced resistance in a
        small fraction of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} on contact. Describe the pattern the
        three replicas would then show.
  \item Which of the two hypotheses --- \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} before contact, or
        induction by contact --- does the observed pattern support?
        State the argument in one sentence.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Back to the original plate.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The student takes \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} from the two resistant positions on
        the \emph{original} plate (which has never touched the
        antibiotic) and from two other positions, and tests each
        sample on streptomycin. Predict the results.
  \item The two colonies at the resistant positions contain about
        $10^7$ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} each. Are all of their \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} resistant, or only
        some? What would you need to know to decide?
  \item A colony grows from one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} through about 23 divisions. If
        the \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} happened at the 20th division, what fraction of
        the colony's \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} carry it? And if at the 3rd division?
  \item Explain why testing a whole colony can reveal a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} that
        occurred in only a fraction of its \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}.
  \item Why does the experiment work only if the colonies on the
        replica plates grow \emph{exactly} where the original ones
        were?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Measuring the rate.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The student repeats the experiment with 50 original plates of
        200 colonies each: 10\,000 colonies, of which 30 give
        resistant replicas. What fraction of colonies carries at
        least one resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}?
  \item Each colony arose from one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} by about $10^7$ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}
        divisions. Estimate the probability that a given division
        produces a resistance \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}.
  \item Streptomycin resistance requires a change at one specific
        position of one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}. Compare your estimate with the
        replication error rate of $10^{-9}$ per \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide}, and
        comment.
  \item A culture of $10^9$ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} is grown from a single \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} in
        antibiotic-free broth. Using your rate, estimate how many
        resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} it is likely to contain when the antibiotic
        is finally added.
  \item If the resistance \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} occurred early in that culture's
        growth, how does the number of resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} change? Why do
        repeated cultures give very different counts?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- What it means.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Explain the difference between "\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} are random" and
        "\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} are rare", and which one the experiment
        establishes.
  \item The antibiotic did not create the resistance. What did it do
        to the population?
  \item Translate the experiment into the language of
        \cref{prop:g11:mutations:source}: what supplied the variation,
        and what sorted it?
  \item A student objects that the two resistant colonies on the
        original plate "looked exactly like the others". Explain why
        a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} can be invisible until the environment changes.
  \item State the result: what the identical positions on three
        velvet replicas proved about the origin of \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}, and the
        rate per division you measured.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
