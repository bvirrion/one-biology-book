\chapter{Amino Acids and Proteins}\label{ch:b1:proteins}

Boil an egg and the clear white turns opaque and firm; nothing has been
added or removed, but every \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} molecule in it has unfolded and
tangled with its neighbours. Cool it and it stays cooked. Yet a small
enzyme unfolded gently in a test tube, then left in plain \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffer}, folds
back within minutes into its exact working shape, with no help from
anything: the shape was written in the chain. \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{Proteins} are the working
molecules of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} --- its enzymes, pumps, motors, scaffolds,
antibodies, hormones --- and every one of them is a chain of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino
acids} folded into a shape that its sequence dictates. This chapter
describes the twenty \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids}, the bond that joins them, the four
levels of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} structure, the folding that turns a chain into a
machine, the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{cooperative} behaviour of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} with several subunits,
and the methods by which \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} are separated and seen.

\section{Amino acids}

\begin{definition}[Amino acid]\label{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}
An \emph{amino acid}\index{amino acid} has a central \emph{$\alpha$
carbon} bearing an amino group ($-\mathrm{NH_2}$), a carboxyl group
($-\mathrm{COOH}$), a hydrogen and a \emph{side chain}\index{side chain}
$R$ that distinguishes the twenty standard amino acids of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins}. At
the pH of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} the amino group is protonated and the carboxyl
ionised: the molecule is a \emph{zwitterion}\index{zwitterion},
$\mathrm{^+H_3N{-}CHR{-}COO^-}$, with no net charge. The $\alpha$ carbon
is chiral in all but glycine, and living things use the L enantiomer
only. Side chains group by their chemistry:
\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
class & amino acids (three- and one-letter codes) & side chain \\
\hline
non-polar, aliphatic & Gly G, Ala A, Val V, Leu L, Ile I, Met M, Pro P & hydrocarbon; Pro a ring \\
aromatic & Phe F, Tyr Y, Trp W & rings; Tyr and Trp absorb UV \\
polar, uncharged & Ser S, Thr T, Cys C, Asn N, Gln Q & $-$OH, $-$SH, amide \\
positively charged & Lys K, Arg R, His H & bases; His p$K_a$ 6.0 \\
negatively charged & Asp D, Glu E & carboxylates, p$K_a$ 4 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Nine of them (His, Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp, Val) are
\emph{essential} for humans: we cannot make them and must eat them.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Ionisation]\label{prop:b1:proteins:ionisation}
Each ionisable group of an \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid} has its own p$K_a$: about 2 for
the $\alpha$-carboxyl, 9.5 for the $\alpha$-amino, and for the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side
chains} 3.9 (Asp), 4.1 (Glu), 6.0 (His), 8.3 (Cys), 10.1 (Tyr), 10.5
(Lys), 12.5 (Arg). The \emph{isoelectric point}\index{isoelectric point} p$I$ is the pH at which the net charge is zero --- the mean of
the two p$K_a$ that bracket the neutral form; a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}'s p$I$ is set
by its content of charged \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chains}, and at that pH it is least
soluble and does not move in an electric field. Histidine, with a p$K_a$
close to 7, is the residue that changes charge within the physiological
range, and it sits in the active sites of many enzymes.
\end{proposition}

\section{The peptide bond}

\begin{definition}[Peptide bond, polypeptide]\label{def:b1:proteins:peptide}
The \emph{peptide bond}\index{peptide bond} joins the carboxyl of one
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acid} to the amino group of the next by condensation (an amide
bond, $\mathrm{-CO{-}NH-}$). A chain of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{amino acids} so joined is a
\emph{polypeptide}\index{polypeptide}: a \emph{backbone} of repeating
$\mathrm{N{-}C_\alpha{-}C}$ units from which the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chains} project,
with an \emph{N-terminus} (free amino group) and a \emph{C-terminus}
(free carboxyl). Sequences are written from N to C, the order of
synthesis (\cref{ch:b1:gene-expression}). A \emph{protein}\index{protein}
is one or more polypeptides folded into a definite shape; typical
chains have \qtyrange{100}{1000}{} residues of mean mass \qty{110}{Da},
so a protein of \qty{300}{} residues is \qty{33}{kDa}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Geometry of the peptide bond]\label{prop:b1:proteins:geometry}
The C--N bond of a peptide has partial double-bond character (the
amide resonance): it is short, cannot rotate, and holds the six atoms
$\mathrm{C_\alpha{-}C(=O){-}N(H){-}C_\alpha}$ in one plane, almost
always with the two $\alpha$ carbons \emph{trans}. The backbone is
therefore a chain of rigid planes hinged at the $\alpha$ carbons, each
hinge having two rotations, $\phi$ (about N--$\mathrm{C_\alpha}$) and
$\psi$ (about $\mathrm{C_\alpha}$--C), and steric clashes between \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side
chains} and backbone forbid most combinations of $\phi$ and $\psi$. The
conformation of a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} is essentially the list of its $\phi$ and
$\psi$ angles.
\end{proposition}

\section{Secondary structure}

\begin{definition}[Secondary structure]\label{def:b1:proteins:secondary}
The \emph{secondary structure}\index{secondary structure} of a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}
is the local, regular folding of its backbone, held by \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds}
between backbone C=O and N--H groups. Two patterns dominate. The
\emph{$\alpha$ helix}\index{alpha helix@$\alpha$ helix}: a right-handed
coil of 3.6 residues per turn, rising \qty{0.15}{nm} per residue
(\qty{0.54}{nm} per turn), each C=O bonded to the N--H four residues
ahead, with the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chains} pointing outward. The \emph{$\beta$
sheet}\index{beta sheet@$\beta$ sheet}: chains stretched almost fully
(\qty{0.35}{nm} per residue) and laid side by side, parallel or
antiparallel, bonded between neighbouring strands, with \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chains}
alternating above and below the sheet. Between them, \emph{turns} and
loops reverse the chain's direction. Proline, whose ring locks $\phi$,
breaks helices; glycine, with no \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chain}, allows turns that no other
residue can make.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Helix and sheet by the numbers]\label{ex:b1:proteins:numbers}
A membrane-spanning $\alpha$ helix must cross \qty{3}{nm} of
hydrophobic core: $3/0.15 = 20$ residues, and indeed the transmembrane
segments of \cref{ch:b1:membranes-transport} are runs of about twenty
hydrophobic residues. A $\beta$ strand of the same length spans
\qty{7}{nm}: silk fibroin is stacked antiparallel sheets of glycine and
alanine, and a silk thread is stronger than steel of the same weight
because the covalent backbones lie along the fibre.
\end{example}

\section{Tertiary and quaternary structure}

\begin{definition}[Tertiary and quaternary structure]\label{def:b1:proteins:tertiary}
The \emph{tertiary structure}\index{tertiary structure} is the complete
three-dimensional arrangement of one \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{polypeptide}: its helices, sheets
and loops packed together, often in several compact \emph{domains}
that fold independently. It is held by non-covalent forces ---
the \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:properties}{hydrophobic effect}} burying non-polar \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chains} in a core
away from water, \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds}, salt bridges between charged \omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side
chains}, \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:bonds}{van der Waals} packing --- and sometimes by \emph{disulfide
bridges}\index{disulfide bridge}, covalent S--S bonds between two
cysteines, in \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} secreted to the oxidising outside. The
\emph{quaternary structure}\index{quaternary structure} is the
assembly of several \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{polypeptides} (subunits) into one \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}:
haemoglobin is two $\alpha$ and two $\beta$ chains. \emph{Globular}
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} are compact and water-soluble (enzymes, carriers);
\emph{fibrous} \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} are extended and insoluble (\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{collagen}'s triple
helix, the coiled coils of keratin and myosin, silk).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The sequence determines the fold]\label{thm:b1:proteins:anfinsen}
\index{protein folding}
The three-dimensional structure of a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} is determined by its
amino-acid sequence: the native fold is the conformation of lowest \omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:gibbs}{free
energy} that the chain can reach in its cellular environment, and the
information needed to reach it is contained in the chain itself.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Anfinsen (1961) unfolded ribonuclease A, a \qty{124}{}-residue enzyme
with four \omterm{def:b1:proteins:tertiary}{disulfide bridges}, in concentrated urea with a reducing
agent that broke the bridges: the chain lost all activity. Removing the
urea and letting the cysteines re-oxidise slowly in air, he recovered
the enzyme fully active, with its four bridges re-formed between the
same eight cysteines out of the \qty{105}{} possible pairings --- the
chain had found its own fold. Re-oxidised while still in urea, it
formed random bridges and stayed inactive; a trace of the reducing
agent then let it shuffle them until the native set, the most stable,
was reached. Since then thousands of sequences have been folded from
scratch in the test tube, and the fold of a new sequence can now be
computed from it.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Denaturation]\label{prop:b1:proteins:denaturation}
\index{denaturation (protein)}
Heat, extremes of pH, urea, detergents or organic solvents unfold
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} by overwhelming the weak bonds that hold the fold: the chain
loses its shape and its function, exposes its hydrophobic core, and
aggregates with its neighbours. Small \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} refold when the
denaturant is removed; large ones, and \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} in a crowded \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell},
often cannot, and the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} keeps \emph{chaperones}\index{chaperone} ---
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} that bind unfolded chains, shield their hydrophobic surfaces,
and give them repeated chances to fold correctly --- and destroys what
stays misfolded. Fevers of a few degrees, and the \qty{42}{\celsius} at
which brain \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} begin to unfold, mark the narrow margin of
stability: a typical fold is only \qtyrange{20}{60}{kJ/mol} more stable
than the unfolded chain, the worth of a few \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Collagen]\label{ex:b1:proteins:collagen}
A quarter of a mammal's \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} is \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:connective}{collagen}: three chains, each a
left-handed helix of a thousand residues with glycine at every third
position (Gly--X--Y, Y often hydroxyproline), wound together into a
right-handed triple helix \qty{300}{nm} long, then packed side by side
into fibrils cross-linked by \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:bonds}{covalent bonds}. Glycine's absence of a
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:aminoacid}{side chain} is what lets the three chains pack at the axis; a single
substitution of glycine by any other residue kinks the helix and gives
brittle bones. Vitamin C is needed to hydroxylate the prolines; without
it the helix is unstable and the fibrils fail: scurvy.
\end{example}

\section{Allostery: haemoglobin}

\begin{definition}[Allostery, cooperativity]\label{def:b1:proteins:allostery}
A \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} is \emph{allosteric}\index{allostery} when the binding of a
ligand at one site changes the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein}'s conformation and thereby its
affinity at another site. When the sites are alike and the change
raises the affinity of the others, binding is \emph{cooperative}\index{cooperativity}:
the saturation curve is sigmoid rather than hyperbolic, and the
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} switches from nearly empty to nearly full over a narrow range
of ligand concentration. The \emph{Hill equation}\index{Hill equation}
describes it:
\[
Y = \frac{P^n}{P_{50}^n + P^n},
\]
where $Y$ is the fraction of sites occupied, $P$ the ligand
concentration (or partial pressure), $P_{50}$ the value at half
saturation, and $n$ the Hill coefficient --- 1 for independent sites,
up to the number of sites for perfect cooperativity.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Haemoglobin and myoglobin]\label{prop:b1:proteins:hemoglobin}
Myoglobin, one chain with one haem, binds $\mathrm{O_2}$ with a
hyperbola ($n = 1$, $P_{50} = \qty{0.37}{kPa}$): it is saturated at any
pressure the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} offer and serves as a store in muscle.
Haemoglobin, four subunits, binds \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{cooperatively} ($n \approx 2.8$,
$P_{50} = \qty{3.5}{kPa}$): the first $\mathrm{O_2}$ bound shifts the
tetramer from a low-affinity \emph{T state} toward a high-affinity
\emph{R state}, so that it loads nearly fully at the lung's
\qty{13}{kPa} and unloads a large fraction at the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}'
\qty{5}{kPa}. Its affinity is further lowered, and unloading favoured,
by acid and $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (the \emph{Bohr effect}) and by
2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPG), which binds the T state: in a working
muscle, or at altitude, more oxygen is released for the same pressure.
The fetus makes a haemoglobin that binds BPG weakly, so its blood draws
oxygen from the mother's across the placenta.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Using the Hill equation]\label{met:b1:proteins:hill}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Read $P_{50}$ from the curve at $Y = 0.5$; estimate $n$ from
        the slope of $\log[Y/(1-Y)]$ against $\log P$ (the Hill plot),
        which is a straight line of slope $n$ near $P_{50}$.
  \item Compute $Y$ at the loading pressure (lungs) and at the
        unloading pressure (\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}); the difference is the fraction
        of the carrying capacity delivered.
  \item Multiply by the capacity: blood holds \qty{150}{g/L} of
        haemoglobin, each gram binding \qty{1.34}{mL} of $\mathrm{O_2}$,
        i.e.\ \qty{200}{mL} of $\mathrm{O_2}$ per litre when saturated.
  \item To include the Bohr effect or BPG, use the shifted $P_{50}$ at
        the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} only, where the acid is.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Seeing and separating proteins}

\begin{method}[Separating proteins]\label{met:b1:proteins:methods}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Electrophoresis in SDS}: the detergent coats every chain
        with negative charge in proportion to its length, so chains
        migrate through a polyacrylamide gel by size alone; a ladder of
        known masses calibrates the gel, and a stained band's position
        gives the mass to a few percent.
  \item \emph{Chromatography}: through a column of beads that retard
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} by size (gel filtration: large ones elute first), by
        charge (ion exchange), or by specific binding (affinity: an
        immobilised ligand holds one \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} and lets the rest through).
        A purification is followed by the rise of specific activity
        (\cref{ch:b1:cell-unit-of-life}).
  \item \emph{Sequencing}: the gene's sequence, translated; or the
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} itself, cut into peptides and read by mass spectrometry.
  \item \emph{Structure}: X-ray diffraction of a crystal, or electron
        microscopy of frozen single particles, gives the position of
        every atom; the ribbon drawings of this chapter are their
        summaries.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{omfigure}
\includegraphics[width=0.36\linewidth]{images/book3/ai/b3-12-protein-gel.jpg}

{\small A stained SDS--polyacrylamide gel. Each lane is a sample; each
band a \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} of one size; the left lane a ladder of markers of known
mass. Small \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{proteins} run farther.}
\end{omfigure}

\begin{example}[Reading a gel]\label{ex:b1:proteins:gel}
A crude extract shows dozens of bands; after affinity chromatography
one band remains at the position of the \qty{33}{kDa} marker. Run
without SDS and without reducing agent, the same \omterm{def:b1:proteins:peptide}{protein} migrates as a
\qty{66}{kDa} species: it is a dimer of two identical chains held by a
\omterm{def:b1:proteins:tertiary}{disulfide bridge}, which the reducing agent breaks and the detergent
separates. Two gels, one line of reasoning, and the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:tertiary}{quaternary
structure} is known.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: Haemoglobin at Work}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- oxygen carried from lung to
muscle, at rest, in a sprint, on a mountain and before birth, ending on
the oxygen delivered per litre of blood}]
\label{pb:b1:proteins:1}
Blood holds \qty{150}{g/L} of haemoglobin; \qty{1}{g} binds
\qty{1.34}{mL} of $\mathrm{O_2}$. Haemoglobin obeys the \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{Hill equation}
with $n = 2.8$ and $P_{50} = \qty{3.5}{kPa}$ at pH 7.4; myoglobin has
$n = 1$ and $P_{50} = \qty{0.37}{kPa}$. Partial pressures of
$\mathrm{O_2}$: \qty{13.3}{kPa} in the lungs at sea level,
\qty{5.3}{kPa} in resting \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}. Take $2.8\ln 3.5 = 3.51$,
$2.8\ln 5.3 = 4.67$, $2.8\ln 13.3 = 7.25$, $2.8\ln 2.7 = 2.78$,
$2.8\ln 4.5 = 4.21$, $2.8\ln 7 = 5.45$, $2.8\ln 4 = 3.88$,
$2.8\ln 2.6 = 2.68$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- At rest.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the oxygen capacity of a litre of blood.
  \item Compute $P^{2.8}$ for $P = 3.5$, $5.3$ and $13.3$ kPa (as
        $e^{2.8\ln P}$).
  \item Compute the saturation $Y$ of haemoglobin in the lungs and in
        the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}.
  \item Compute the fraction of the load delivered and the oxygen
        delivered per litre of blood, in millilitres.
  \item A resting human consumes \qty{250}{mL} of $\mathrm{O_2}$ per
        minute. What cardiac output does this delivery require?
  \item Compute the saturation of myoglobin at the same two pressures
        and the oxygen it would deliver per litre if it replaced
        haemoglobin. Conclude.
  \item Explain in one sentence what \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{cooperativity} buys.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- A sprint.}
In a sprinting muscle the pH falls to 7.2 and the local pressure of
$\mathrm{O_2}$ to \qty{2.7}{kPa}; the Bohr effect raises $P_{50}$ to
\qty{4.5}{kPa} there.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute $Y$ at \qty{2.7}{kPa} with $P_{50} = \qty{3.5}{kPa}$
        (no Bohr effect).
  \item Compute $Y$ at \qty{2.7}{kPa} with $P_{50} = \qty{4.5}{kPa}$.
  \item Compute the oxygen delivered per litre in the two cases
        (loading in the lungs unchanged) and the gain from the Bohr
        effect.
  \item The muscle's myoglobin is at $P = \qty{2.7}{kPa}$. What is its
        saturation, and what happens to it if the pressure falls to
        \qty{0.5}{kPa} during a contraction?
  \item Explain why the Bohr effect is a form of \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{allostery} and where
        the protons act.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- A mountain.}
At \qty{4000}{m} the lungs' $\mathrm{O_2}$ pressure is \qty{7}{kPa}.
Within days the red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} raise their BPG, shifting $P_{50}$ to
\qty{4.0}{kPa}; within weeks the marrow raises haemoglobin to
\qty{180}{g/L}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the saturation in the lungs at \qty{7}{kPa} with
        $P_{50} = \qty{3.5}{kPa}$, and the delivery per litre (\omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}
        at \qty{5.3}{kPa}). Compare with sea level.
  \item Compute the lung and \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue} saturations with
        $P_{50} = \qty{4.0}{kPa}$, and the delivery.
  \item Explain why lowering the affinity helps although it lowers
        the loading in the lungs.
  \item Compute the delivery per litre with $P_{50} = \qty{4.0}{kPa}$
        and \qty{180}{g/L} of haemoglobin.
  \item Would a still lower affinity ($P_{50} = \qty{6}{kPa}$) help at
        \qty{7}{kPa}? Compute the two saturations and decide.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Before birth.}
Fetal haemoglobin has $P_{50} = \qty{2.6}{kPa}$; in the placenta the
pressure of $\mathrm{O_2}$ is \qty{4}{kPa} on both sides.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the saturation of maternal haemoglobin at \qty{4}{kPa}.
  \item Compute the saturation of fetal haemoglobin at \qty{4}{kPa}.
  \item Explain how oxygen moves from mother to fetus although the
        pressure is the same on both sides.
  \item Fetal haemoglobin has $\gamma$ chains instead of $\beta$, which
        bind BPG weakly. Explain how this produces the lower $P_{50}$.
  \item At birth the fetal blood must unload in \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} at
        \qty{5.3}{kPa}. Compute the fetal delivery per litre from lungs
        at \qty{13.3}{kPa}, and say why the switch to adult
        haemoglobin over the first months is useful.
  \item A mutation raises the adult $P_{50}$ to \qty{5}{kPa}. Predict
        the person's arterial saturation at sea level, and the
        consequence for delivery.
  \item Explain why a carrier with $P_{50} = \qty{3.5}{kPa}$ and no
        \omterm{def:b1:proteins:allostery}{cooperativity} ($n = 1$) would be a poor oxygen carrier: compute
        its delivery.
  \item State the result: the oxygen delivered per litre of blood at
        rest and in a sprinting muscle, and the two properties of
        haemoglobin that make the difference.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
