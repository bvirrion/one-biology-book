\chapter{Membrane Traffic and Protein Sorting}\label{ch:b3:membrane-traffic}

A cell of the pancreas makes some ten million molecules of digestive
enzyme a minute, packs them into granules, and releases them into a
duct on a signal from the gut --- without ever letting a molecule of
trypsin loose in its own cytoplasm. A liver cell takes cholesterol out
of the blood by swallowing the particles that carry it, whole, at a
rate of thousands a minute, and returns each receptor to the surface
for the next. A newly made protein destined for the mitochondrion, the
nucleus, the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosome}, the membrane or the outside world reaches its
address among a dozen compartments with an error rate that would
shame a postal service. This chapter is about the sorting: the signals
written into proteins, the machines that read them, the vesicles that
carry membrane and cargo between compartments, the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:coats}{coats} that shape the
vesicles and the proteins that make them fuse with the right target, and
the two-way traffic --- secretion out, endocytosis in --- that a
eukaryotic cell keeps up every second of its life.

\section{Signals and addresses}

\begin{definition}[Sorting signals]\label{def:b3:membrane-traffic:signals}
\index{sorting signal}\index{signal peptide}\index{nuclear localisation signal}\index{presequence}
A \emph{sorting signal} is a segment of a protein, or a modification of
it, read by a receptor that delivers the protein to one compartment.
The \emph{signal peptide}, a stretch of \numrange{15}{30} residues at
the amino terminus with a hydrophobic core, sends a protein into the
endoplasmic reticulum as it is being made; from there the default
route leads through the Golgi to the plasma membrane or the outside,
and further signals divert to the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosome} (a phosphorylated sugar) or
back to the reticulum (the carboxy-terminal sequence KDEL). A
\emph{nuclear localisation signal}, a short basic patch such as
PKKKRKV, is bound by importins that carry the folded protein through
the nuclear pore; a mitochondrial \emph{presequence}, an amphipathic
helix of \numrange{20}{50} residues, is recognised by receptors on the
outer membrane and threaded, unfolded, through translocases of both
membranes; peroxisomal enzymes end in SKL. Proteins with none of these
stay in the cytosol. Every signal was found the same way: deleting it
leaves the protein in the cytosol, grafting it onto a cytosolic protein
sends that protein to the compartment.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Blobel and Dobberstein (1975) translated the messenger RNA of an
antibody light chain in a cell-free system. Without membranes the
product was longer by about twenty residues than the secreted protein;
with fragments of endoplasmic reticulum added \emph{during} translation,
the product had the secreted size and was protected from added
protease --- it was inside the vesicles --- whereas membranes added
after translation did nothing. The extra amino-terminal segment, the
\omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:signals}{signal peptide}, is needed to enter the reticulum, is removed inside it,
and works only while the chain is being made: the \emph{signal
hypothesis}, confirmed in every detail when the receptor (the
signal-recognition particle, Walter and Blobel 1981) and the channel
(Sec61) were purified.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Membrane proteins and topology]\label{prop:b3:membrane-traffic:topology}
\index{stop-transfer sequence}\index{membrane topology}
A hydrophobic stretch of about twenty residues in the middle of a
chain being translocated acts as a \emph{stop-transfer} sequence: the
channel opens sideways and releases it into the bilayer as a
transmembrane helix, with the part already translocated in the lumen
and the rest made into the cytosol. A second such stretch restarts
translocation, and a protein with $n$ alternating signals ends with
$n$ membrane-spanning helices. The orientation set in the reticulum is
never changed: what faces the lumen of the reticulum faces the lumen of
the Golgi and of every vesicle after it, and the \emph{outside} of the
cell after fusion with the plasma membrane. Hence sugars, added only in
the lumen, are always on the extracellular face of a surface protein,
and the topology of a receptor can be read from where its glycosylation
sites lie.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Pulse--chase]\label{met:b3:membrane-traffic:pulse-chase}
\index{pulse--chase}\index{autoradiography}
To follow a population of newly made molecules through the cell: (1)
expose the cells briefly (the \emph{pulse}, a few minutes) to a
radioactive or otherwise tagged precursor --- tritiated leucine for
proteins; (2) replace it with an excess of unlabelled precursor (the
\emph{chase}), so that no new label is incorporated; (3) fix samples of
cells at intervals and locate the label by autoradiography in electron
micrographs, or by isolating the compartments and counting; (4) plot
the fraction of label in each compartment against time. The order in
which compartments fill and empty is the route; the timing gives the
residence time in each. Palade's laboratory (1960s) applied it to the
pancreatic acinar cell and found the label in the rough reticulum at
\qty{3}{min}, in the Golgi at \qty{20}{min}, in condensing vacuoles and
granules at \qty{40}{min}, and in the duct after an hour and on a
signal: the secretory pathway, laid out in time.
\end{method}

\begin{theorem}[Sequential compartments and the pulse--chase curves]\label{thm:b3:membrane-traffic:pulse-chase}
\index{compartment model}
Let a pulse of labelled protein leave compartment $A$ for $B$ with
first-order rate $k_{1}$ and leave $B$ for $C$ with rate $k_{2}$, $C$
retaining it. With all the label in $A$ at $t = 0$,
\[
A(t) = e^{-k_{1}t},\qquad
B(t) = \frac{k_{1}}{k_{2} - k_{1}}\bigl(e^{-k_{1}t} - e^{-k_{2}t}\bigr),
\qquad
C(t) = 1 - A - B,
\]
and the label in $B$ peaks at $t^{*} = \ln(k_{2}/k_{1})/(k_{2} - k_{1})$.
The mean residence time in $A$ is $1/k_{1}$ and in $B$ is $1/k_{2}$,
whatever the order of the two rates.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\mathrm{d}A/\mathrm{d}t = -k_{1}A$ gives the exponential. For $B$,
$\mathrm{d}B/\mathrm{d}t = k_{1}A - k_{2}B = k_{1}e^{-k_{1}t} - k_{2}B$;
try $B = \beta(e^{-k_{1}t} - e^{-k_{2}t})$: the derivative is
$\beta(-k_{1}e^{-k_{1}t} + k_{2}e^{-k_{2}t})$ and $-k_{2}B = \beta(-k_{2}
e^{-k_{1}t} + k_{2}e^{-k_{2}t})$, so the equation requires $\beta(k_{2}
- k_{1})e^{-k_{1}t} = k_{1}e^{-k_{1}t}$, $\beta = k_{1}/(k_{2} -
k_{1})$, and $B(0) = 0$ as required. Setting $\mathrm{d}B/\mathrm{d}t =
0$: $k_{1}e^{-k_{1}t} = k_{2}e^{-k_{2}t}$, whence $t^{*}$. The mean time
a molecule spends in a compartment it leaves at rate $k$ is $\int_{0}^{
\infty} t\,k e^{-kt}\,\mathrm{d}t = 1/k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Palade's cell in numbers]\label{ex:b3:membrane-traffic:palade}
Take $k_{1} = \qty{0.1}{min^{-1}}$ (ten minutes in the reticulum) and
$k_{2} = \qty{0.05}{min^{-1}}$ (twenty in the Golgi). The Golgi peaks at
$t^{*} = \ln 2/0.05 = \qty{13.9}{min}$, holding then $B = 2(e^{-1.39} -
e^{-0.69}) = 2(0.25 - 0.50)\cdot(-1) = 0.50$: half the label. At
\qty{40}{min}, $A = 0.018$, $B = 2(0.018 - 0.135) \to 0.23$, $C = 0.75$:
three quarters of the protein has reached the granules. The observed
curves of Palade's laboratory have this shape, and fitting them was
how the residence times were first measured.
\end{example}

\section{The endoplasmic reticulum: folding, sugars and quality}

\begin{definition}[N-glycosylation and quality control]\label{def:b3:membrane-traffic:glycosylation}
\index{N-glycosylation}\index{calnexin cycle}\index{ER-associated degradation}\index{unfolded protein response}
As the chain enters the lumen a preassembled oligosaccharide of
fourteen sugars (two N-acetylglucosamines, nine mannoses, three
glucoses) is transferred from a lipid carrier onto asparagines in the
sequence Asn-X-Ser/Thr --- \emph{N-glycosylation}. The three glucoses
are then trimmed one by one, and the trimming is a folding clock: a
protein still carrying one glucose is held by the \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:chaperones}{chaperone} calnexin;
a protein whose last glucose has been removed but which is not yet
folded is re-glucosylated by an enzyme that recognises exposed
hydrophobic patches, and returns to calnexin --- the \emph{calnexin
cycle}. Proteins that fail repeatedly are pulled back into the cytosol,
ubiquitinated and destroyed by the proteasome: \emph{ER-associated
degradation} (ERAD). When unfolded proteins accumulate faster than
they can be folded or destroyed, sensors in the reticulum membrane
launch the \emph{unfolded protein response}: translation is slowed,
\omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:chaperones}{chaperone} genes induced, the reticulum expanded, and, if the stress
persists, the cell killed. The commonest mutation of cystic fibrosis
($\Delta$F508 in CFTR) makes a channel that would work but folds too
slowly, is caught by ERAD and never reaches the surface; drugs that
help it fold are now a treatment.
\end{definition}

\section{Vesicles: budding, targeting, fusion}

\begin{definition}[Coats and vesicle budding]\label{def:b3:membrane-traffic:coats}
\index{coated vesicle}\index{COPII}\index{COPI}\index{clathrin}\index{small GTPase}
A transport vesicle is shaped by a \emph{coat} of proteins assembled on
the cytosolic face of a membrane, which curves the membrane into a bud
of \qtyrange{60}{100}{nm}, collects cargo through receptors that span
the membrane, and is shed after the vesicle pinches off. Three coats
serve the main routes: \emph{COPII} for vesicles leaving the reticulum
for the Golgi, \emph{COPI} for the return traffic from the Golgi to the
reticulum and between Golgi cisternae, and \emph{clathrin} --- a
three-legged protein that polymerises into a cage of hexagons and
pentagons --- for vesicles leaving the trans-Golgi for \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{endosomes} and for
endocytosis at the plasma membrane, where adaptor proteins link the
cage to the receptors being internalised. Each coat is recruited by a
\emph{small GTPase} (Sar1 for COPII, Arf1 for COPI and clathrin) that
inserts into the membrane in its GTP form and, on hydrolysing GTP after
budding, lets the coat fall off. Retrieval signals keep the compartments
distinct: escaped reticulum proteins carrying KDEL are bound in the
Golgi by a receptor and carried back in COPI vesicles.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Targeting and fusion]\label{def:b3:membrane-traffic:fusion}
\index{Rab GTPase}\index{SNARE}\index{membrane fusion}\index{synaptotagmin}
A vesicle finds its target by two layers of recognition. \emph{Rab
GTPases} --- some sixty in a human cell, each on the membranes of one
compartment --- recruit long tethering proteins that catch an incoming
vesicle carrying the matching Rab. Then the \emph{SNAREs} act: a
v-SNARE on the vesicle and t-SNAREs on the target, helical proteins
anchored in their membranes, wind around each other from their distal
ends toward the membranes, and the energy of the four-helix bundle they
form pulls the two bilayers together until they fuse. Each SNARE pairing
is specific, a second check on the address. After fusion the bundle
is pried apart by the ATPase NSF for reuse. Fusion can be made to wait
for a signal: in the nerve terminal the SNAREs are held half-zipped by
complexin until \emph{synaptotagmin}, binding calcium that enters when
an action potential arrives, completes the zippering in under a
millisecond --- the release of neurotransmitter treated in the Year~2
volume. The neurotoxins of tetanus and botulism are proteases that
cleave SNAREs.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Novick and Schekman (1980) isolated temperature-sensitive yeast
mutants that stopped secreting at \qty{37}{\celsius} and accumulated
protein in whichever compartment their defect blocked --- reticulum,
Golgi or vesicles piled under the surface; the $23$ \emph{sec} genes
defined the steps and, cloned, turned out to encode the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:coats}{coats}, the
GTPases and the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:fusion}{SNAREs}. Rothman reconstituted transport between Golgi
cisternae in a test tube with purified membranes, cytosol and ATP,
and from it purified NSF and the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:fusion}{SNAREs}; the two approaches converged
on the same proteins, and the yeast and mammalian machines proved to be
the same.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The Golgi]\label{prop:b3:membrane-traffic:golgi}
\index{Golgi apparatus}\index{cisternal maturation}\index{mannose-6-phosphate}
The Golgi is a stack of four to eight flattened cisternae, cis face
toward the reticulum, trans face toward the plasma membrane, each
cisterna housing a different set of enzymes that trim and rebuild the
sugars of passing glycoproteins and add O-linked sugars, sulfate and
phosphate. Cargo advances chiefly by \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:membrane-traffic:golgi}{cisternal maturation}}: a new
cisterna forms at the cis face from arriving vesicles, moves through
the stack as the ones before it do, and matures as \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:coats}{COPI} vesicles carry
each cisterna's enzymes backward to the younger one behind it; at the
trans face it breaks up into vesicles bound for their destinations.
There the sorting is decided: lysosomal hydrolases, tagged in the cis
Golgi with \emph{mannose-6-phosphate}, are bound by their receptor and
packed into \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:coats}{clathrin} vesicles for the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{endosome}; regulated secretory
proteins aggregate at the mildly acid pH into condensing granules;
everything else leaves in the constitutive stream for the surface. In
I-cell disease the phosphorylating enzyme is missing, the hydrolases
are secreted instead of delivered, and the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosomes} fill with
undigested material.
\end{proposition}

\section{Endocytosis and the lysosome}

\begin{definition}[Endocytosis]\label{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}
\index{receptor-mediated endocytosis}\index{endosome}\index{lysosome}\index{autophagy}
\emph{Receptor-mediated endocytosis} concentrates a ligand bound to
surface receptors into clathrin-coated pits, which pinch off (the
GTPase dynamin constricts the neck) as vesicles of about \qty{100}{nm}
--- a thousand per minute in a fibroblast, turning over the whole
surface membrane in an hour. The vesicles fuse into \emph{early
endosomes}, whose lumen is acidified to pH~6 by a proton pump; most
ligands release their receptors there, the receptors return to the
surface in recycling vesicles, and the ligand travels on as the
endosome matures into a late endosome (pH~5.5) and fuses with a
\emph{lysosome}: a compartment at pH~4.5--5 holding some sixty
hydrolases --- proteases, nucleases, lipases, glycosidases --- active
only at that pH, so that a leak into the neutral cytosol does little
harm. The lysosome also digests the cell's own material delivered by
\emph{autophagy}: a double membrane grows around a portion of cytoplasm
or a worn-out organelle, closes into an autophagosome, and fuses with
the lysosome; the products are returned to the cytosol. Autophagy is
induced by starvation (it recycles a cell's own substance for energy)
and clears aggregates and damaged mitochondria; its failure is
implicated in neurodegeneration.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Brown and Goldstein (1970s) followed low-density lipoprotein (LDL),
the particle that carries cholesterol in blood, into cultured
fibroblasts. LDL bound saturably to some \num{20000} surface sites per
cell, was internalised within minutes into coated pits, and its
cholesterol was released in \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosomes} and switched off the cell's own
cholesterol synthesis. Fibroblasts from patients with familial
hypercholesterolaemia bound no LDL (receptor absent) or bound it but
did not internalise it (a mutation in the receptor's cytosolic tail
that prevents its capture by \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:coats}{clathrin} adaptors): the disease was a
defect of endocytosis, the receptor's tail contained the internalisation
signal, and the recycling receptor --- each making hundreds of round
trips --- was established as the general mechanism of uptake.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Uptake through recycling receptors]\label{prop:b3:membrane-traffic:uptake}
\index{receptor recycling}
Let a cell have $R$ receptors in all, of which $S$ are at the surface
and $R - S$ inside on their way back. A surface receptor is occupied
with probability $\theta = [L]/(K_{d} + [L])$, an occupied receptor is
internalised at rate $k_{i}$, and an internalised receptor returns at
rate $k_{r}$. At steady state the flux of ligand into the cell is
\[
J = \frac{R\,k_{i}k_{r}\,\theta}{k_{r} + k_{i}\theta},
\]
which saturates, as the ligand concentration rises, at $J_{\max} =
R\,k_{i}k_{r}/(k_{i} + k_{r})$: the uptake of a cell is limited not by
its receptors alone but by how fast it can bring them back.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Surface receptors are lost at rate $k_{i}\theta S$ and regained at rate
$k_{r}(R - S)$; at steady state these balance, so $S = R\,k_{r}/(k_{r}
+ k_{i}\theta)$. Each internalisation carries one ligand, so $J =
k_{i}\theta S$, which gives the formula. As $\theta \to 1$ the flux
tends to $R\,k_{i}k_{r}/(k_{r} + k_{i})$ --- the receptors cycle as fast
as the two rates allow, with a fraction $k_{r}/(k_{i} + k_{r})$ of them
at the surface at any moment.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Cholesterol by the particle]\label{ex:b3:membrane-traffic:ldl}
A fibroblast with $R = \num{20000}$ LDL receptors, an internalisation
time of \qty{5}{min} ($k_{i} = \qty{0.2}{min^{-1}}$) and a return time
of \qty{10}{min} ($k_{r} = \qty{0.1}{min^{-1}}$) takes up, at saturating
LDL, $J_{\max} = \num{20000}\times 0.2\times 0.1/0.3 \approx 1300$
particles per minute, with a third of its receptors at the surface at
any moment; each particle brings some \num{1500} molecules of
cholesterol, two million a minute, enough to build about a tenth of a
square micrometre of membrane. A heterozygote for familial
hypercholesterolaemia, with half the receptors, takes up half as much;
blood cholesterol doubles, and heart disease comes twenty years early.
The statins raise $R$ by starving the liver cell of its own
cholesterol.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[One membrane, many compartments]\label{rem:b3:membrane-traffic:one-membrane}
Every membrane of the secretory and endocytic system is, topologically,
one membrane: the lumen of the reticulum is continuous, through
vesicles, with the outside of the cell, and a lipid or protein made in
the reticulum can reach the surface without ever crossing a bilayer.
What keeps the compartments distinct is not walls but traffic ---
selective packaging out, selective retrieval back, and a specific pair
of recognition molecules at every fusion. The compartments are steady
states of flows, like the pools of a river, and a cell that stops
trafficking loses its geography within hours.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: A Pancreatic Cell's Day}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a secretory cell's throughput
counted in molecules, vesicles and square micrometres, its
pulse--chase curves solved, a liver cell's cholesterol uptake computed
through recycling receptors, and a lysosome acidified proton by proton,
ending on the time of the Golgi peak, the vesicles budding per minute
and the LDL particles taken up per hour}]
\label{pb:b3:membrane-traffic:1}
Data: an acinar cell makes $10^{7}$ enzyme molecules per minute, each
\qty{25}{kDa}, \qty{4}{nm} across. Transport vesicles are spheres of
\qty{70}{nm} diameter; a granule is a sphere of \qty{1}{\micro m}.
Membrane area per lipid molecule \qty{0.7}{nm^2}, two leaflets. The
cell's reticulum has an area of \qty{6000}{\micro m^2}. Pulse--chase:
$k_{1} = \qty{0.1}{min^{-1}}$, $k_{2} = \qty{0.05}{min^{-1}}$. A liver
cell: $R = \num{50000}$ LDL receptors, $k_{i} = \qty{0.2}{min^{-1}}$,
$k_{r} = \qty{0.1}{min^{-1}}$, $K_{d} = \qty{2}{nmol/L}$, plasma LDL
particle concentration \qty{2}{nmol/L}; \num{1500} cholesterol molecules
per particle.
A \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosome}: sphere of \qty{0.5}{\micro m}, pH from $7.2$ to $4.7$,
buffering capacity such that $50$ protons must be pumped for each one
that stays free; a proton pump moves $200$ protons per second.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Throughput.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What mass of enzyme does the cell make per minute, and per day?
        Compare with the cell's own protein content, about
        \qty{300}{pg}.
  \item How many enzyme molecules fit in a vesicle if they fill
        \qty{30}{\%} of its volume (sphere of \qty{4}{nm})? How many
        vesicles per minute leave the reticulum?
  \item How much membrane area do those vesicles carry per minute, and
        in how long would they consume the whole reticulum if none came
        back?
  \item How many lipid molecules is that per minute? What must the
        cell do to keep its reticulum?
  \item How many enzyme molecules fit in a granule at the same packing,
        and how many granules does the cell fill per hour?
  \item A meal empties $300$ granules in ten minutes by fusion with
        the apical membrane, adding their membrane to a surface of
        \qty{50}{\micro m^2}. By what factor does the apical surface
        grow, and what must follow?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Timing.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item With the given rates, when does the Golgi label peak, and what
        fraction is there?
  \item What fraction of a pulse is in the reticulum, Golgi and
        granules at \qty{30}{min}?
  \item Mean residence times in the two compartments? What is the mean
        total time from synthesis to granule?
  \item A drug slows exit from the Golgi to $k_{2} = \qty{0.01}{min^{-1}}$.
        New peak time and peak fraction; what does the Golgi look like
        in the microscope?
  \item Explain why the Golgi peak fraction is $k_{1}/k_{2}$ raised to a
        power (give the power) and hence always below $1$.
  \item If the reticulum step were much faster than the Golgi step,
        what would $B(t)$ approach? Interpret.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Cholesterol.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute $\theta$ at the plasma LDL concentration, and the
        uptake $J$ in particles per minute and per hour.
  \item How many cholesterol molecules per hour? The cell's membranes
        hold $5\times 10^{9}$ cholesterol molecules; how long to replace
        them all from LDL alone?
  \item Compute the fraction of receptors at the surface, and the
        number of round trips each receptor makes in its \qty{20}{h}
        lifetime.
  \item A heterozygote has $R = \num{25000}$: recompute $J$. Plasma LDL
        rises until uptake matches production: by what factor, if
        $\theta$ is well below saturation?
  \item A statin doubles $R$ in the heterozygote's liver cells. What
        happens to plasma LDL, on the same reasoning?
  \item Explain why the receptor-null homozygote, with plasma LDL five
        times normal, is still worse off than the arithmetic of question
        16 alone suggests.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Acid.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosome}'s volume in litres and the number of free
        protons at pH $7.2$ and at pH $4.7$.
  \item How many protons must be pumped in all, with the buffering?
        How long does that take with $50$ pumps?
  \item The pump moves protons in against the gradient. What
        electrical problem arises, and how is it solved (think of a
        counter-ion)?
  \item Why are the hydrolases made active only at pH~5, and what does
        this protect?
  \item Why does the LDL receptor let go of LDL at pH~6 while the
        mannose-6-phosphate receptor lets go of its cargo at the same
        pH --- what must both proteins have in common?
  \item Chloroquine is a weak base that crosses membranes uncharged
        and is trapped once protonated. By what factor does it
        concentrate in a \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosome} at pH $4.7$ relative to the cytosol at
        pH $7.2$ (the ratio of proton concentrations), and what does
        that do to the \omterm{def:b3:membrane-traffic:endocytosis}{lysosome}?
  \item Summarise: the Golgi peak time (question~7), the vesicles
        leaving the reticulum per minute (question~2), and the LDL
        particles taken up per hour (question~13).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
