\chapter{Cell Differentiation: The Skeletal Muscle Cell}\label{ch:b2:cell-differentiation}

A fibre of your thigh muscle is a single cell thirty centimetres
long, with hundreds of nuclei, packed from end to end with
contractile rods so regular that it looks striped under the
microscope. It began as a cluster of ordinary-looking cells that
crawled out of a somite, multiplied, stopped dividing, lined up,
fused, and switched on several hundred genes they had never used
before. Every cell of the body has the same genome; a \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{muscle fibre}
is what that genome does when one particular \omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factor}
is turned on. This chapter takes the skeletal muscle cell as the
worked example of \emph{\omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:differentiation}{differentiation}} --- how a cell acquires
and keeps a specialised identity --- and the muscle cell is a good
one because the master switch is known, the steps can be watched in
a dish, and the finished cell is a machine whose parts are visible.

\section{What differentiation is}

\begin{definition}[Differentiation, determination, potency]\label{def:b2:cell-differentiation:differentiation}
\index{differentiation}\index{determination}\index{stem cell}\index{potency}
A cell is \emph{differentiated} when it expresses the stable
pattern of genes --- and so the proteins, shape and function --- of
one cell type. It is \emph{determined} (or committed) when its fate
is fixed although its differentiation has not yet begun: a \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblast}
looks like a fibroblast but will make only muscle. \emph{Potency} is
the range of types a cell can still give: \emph{totipotent} (the
zygote and first blastomeres, which make everything including the
\omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:placenta}{placenta}), \emph{pluripotent} (the \omterm{def:b2:mammal-reproduction:implantation}{inner cell mass}, which makes
every tissue of the body), \emph{multipotent} (a stem cell of the
blood or of muscle, which makes the types of one tissue),
\emph{unipotent}. A \emph{stem cell} is a cell that divides to give
one daughter like itself and one that goes on to differentiate,
which keeps the stock while supplying the tissue. Differentiation
is in most cases not a loss of genes but a choice among them: the
genome of a muscle nucleus is complete.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Gurdon (1962) transplanted the nucleus of a differentiated
intestinal cell of a tadpole into a frog egg whose own nucleus had
been destroyed; a fraction of the eggs developed into normal,
fertile frogs. The differentiated nucleus had lost none of the
genes needed to make a whole animal; the egg's cytoplasm had reset
it. The same operation with a mammary cell nucleus in a sheep's egg
gave Dolly (1996), and the reprogramming of a skin fibroblast into
a pluripotent \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:differentiation}{stem cell} by four \omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factors} (Yamanaka,
2006) showed that the reset can be done with a handful of proteins.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Differentiation is transcriptional]\label{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}
\index{transcription factor}\index{master regulator}
The difference between two cell types lies in which genes are
transcribed. A few \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factors}}, expressed in
response to the inductive signals of
\cref{ch:b2:vertebrate-development}, bind the regulatory regions of
hundreds of target genes and switch them on, while the genes of
other fates are switched off; and because such a factor commonly
activates its own gene, the state, once entered, sustains itself.
For some tissues a single factor is a \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{master regulator}}
sufficient to impose the fate on a cell of another type: \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD} for
skeletal muscle, Pax6 for the eye, Sox9 for cartilage. The state is
stabilised further by changes to the chromatin --- methylation of
DNA, modification of histones --- that keep the silenced genes
silent through divisions (the \emph{epigenetics} of the Year 3
volume). A differentiated cell therefore remembers what it is
without any change to its DNA sequence, and the memory is written
in the pattern of factors and chromatin, not in the genes.
\end{proposition}

\section{The making of a muscle fibre}

\begin{definition}[Myogenesis]\label{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}
\index{myoblast}\index{myotube}\index{muscle fibre}\index{MyoD}
In the embryo, cells of the somite's dermomyotome are induced by
signals from the \omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural tube}, notochord and surface ectoderm to
express the myogenic factors \emph{MyoD} and \emph{Myf5}: they are
now \emph{myoblasts}, determined but still dividing and unremarkable
to look at. Myoblasts migrate --- into the \omterm{def:b2:limb-organogenesis:bud}{limb buds}, for instance
--- and multiply as long as growth factors (FGF) are present. When
the factors run out, they withdraw from the cell cycle, express a
second factor, \emph{myogenin}, align end to end, and \emph{fuse}:
their membranes merge into a single long \emph{myotube} with many
nuclei in a row. The myotube switches on the muscle genes ---
actin, myosin, troponin, tropomyosin, creatine kinase, the
acetylcholine receptor --- assembles the contractile apparatus, and
matures into a \emph{muscle fibre} whose nuclei lie at its
periphery. A fibre never divides again; a few myoblasts remain
beside it, quiescent, as \emph{satellite cells}, the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:differentiation}{stem cells}
that repair and grow the muscle for life.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[MyoD: a master switch]\label{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:myod}
\index{master regulator!MyoD}
\omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD} is a \omterm{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:transcriptional}{transcription factor} of the helix-loop-helix family. It
binds, as a dimer with a ubiquitous partner protein, a six-base
sequence (the E-box, CANNTG) in the regulatory regions of the muscle
genes, recruits chromatin-opening enzymes, and switches the genes on
--- including the genes for myogenin and for \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD} itself, which is
how the state holds. Its activity is gated: in dividing \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblasts} an
inhibitor protein (Id), induced by growth factors, sequesters the
partner and keeps \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD} idle; when growth factors are withdrawn Id
disappears and \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD} acts. Because \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD} is sufficient to impose the
muscle programme on a fibroblast, a fat cell or a nerve cell, the
muscle fate is, in a sense, one gene deep: the difficulty of
becoming a muscle is not in knowing how, but in being told to.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Davis, Weintraub and Lassar (1987) transferred a single cDNA,
isolated from \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblasts}, into cultured fibroblasts: the fibroblasts
stopped dividing, fused into \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myotubes} and made muscle proteins.
The gene was named \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD}. Its relatives Myf5, myogenin and MRF4 were
found by homology; a mouse lacking both \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD} and Myf5 has no
\omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblasts} and no skeletal muscle at all, while a mouse lacking
myogenin has \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblasts} that never fuse.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[A self-sustaining switch]\label{thm:b2:cell-differentiation:switch}
\index{bistability}\index{positive feedback!gene}
Let $m$ be the amount of a factor that activates its own gene with a
cooperative (sigmoid) response and is degraded at rate $k$:
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{a\,m^{2}}{K^{2} + m^{2}} + b - k\,m,
\]
where $b$ is a small basal synthesis. For suitable $a$, $b$, $K$ and
$k$ this equation has \emph{two} stable steady states, a low one
near $b/k$ and a high one near $a/k$, separated by an unstable
threshold: the cell is \emph{bistable}. A transient inductive
signal that pushes $m$ above the threshold sends the cell to the
high state, where it stays after the signal is gone; below the
threshold it returns to the low state. This is a memory built from
a feedback loop, and it is why a cell, once committed, stays
committed through divisions and in the absence of the inducer.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Steady states satisfy $k m = a m^{2}/(K^{2} + m^{2}) + b$. The
right-hand side is a sigmoid rising from $b$ to $a + b$; the left is
a line through the origin of slope $k$. For $b/k < K$ and $k$ small
enough that the line cuts the steep part of the sigmoid, the two
curves intersect three times: at $m_{\text{low}} \approx b/k$, at an
intermediate $m_{\text{u}}$, and at $m_{\text{high}} \approx (a +
b)/k$. Stability: below $m_{\text{low}}$ synthesis exceeds decay and
$m$ rises; between $m_{\text{low}}$ and $m_{\text{u}}$ decay exceeds
synthesis and $m$ falls back; between $m_{\text{u}}$ and
$m_{\text{high}}$ synthesis exceeds decay and $m$ climbs to
$m_{\text{high}}$; above it, $m$ falls back to $m_{\text{high}}$.
Hence the outer two are stable, the middle one is unstable and is
the threshold.
\end{proof}

\section{The finished cell}

\begin{definition}[The muscle fibre]\label{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}
\index{sarcomere}\index{myofibril}\index{sarcoplasmic reticulum}\index{T tubule}
A skeletal \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{muscle fibre} is a cylinder \qtyrange{10}{100}{\micro m}
across and up to tens of centimetres long, bounded by the
\emph{sarcolemma}, with hundreds of nuclei just under it. Its
interior is filled with parallel \emph{myofibrils}, each a chain of
\emph{sarcomeres} \qty{2.5}{\micro m} long: between two Z discs, thin
filaments of actin (with tropomyosin and troponin) anchored at the
Z discs interdigitate with thick filaments of myosin held at the
centre, and the overlap pattern gives the striations. Around each
myofibril a lace of \emph{sarcoplasmic reticulum} stores calcium;
\emph{transverse tubules}, invaginations of the sarcolemma, run
between the myofibrils and carry the electrical signal from the
surface to the interior. Mitochondria pack the spaces, and the
cytoplasm holds glycogen, creatine phosphate and, in red fibres,
myoglobin. The cell is built for one thing --- to shorten on command
--- and the machinery of that command is
\cref{ch:b2:muscle-movement}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Fibre types]\label{prop:b2:cell-differentiation:types}
\index{muscle fibre types}
A muscle is a mixture of fibre types, set partly by the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblast}
lineage and partly by the nerve that innervates the fibre.
\emph{Slow oxidative} (type I) fibres have a slow myosin, many
mitochondria and capillaries, and myoglobin --- red, tireless,
built for posture and endurance. \emph{Fast glycolytic} (type IIx)
fibres have a fast myosin, few mitochondria, much glycogen ---
white, powerful, exhausted in a minute. \emph{Fast oxidative}
(type IIa) fibres lie between. A sprinter's calf is mostly fast
fibres, a marathoner's mostly slow, and the proportions are
heritable; but the type is not fixed: cross-innervating a fast
muscle with a slow nerve, or months of endurance training, converts
fibres toward the slow type, because the pattern of nerve impulses
switches the genes for the myosin isoforms and for mitochondrial
biogenesis. A differentiated cell keeps its identity as a \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{muscle
fibre} and yet revises its programme in response to use.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Growth and repair]\label{ex:b2:cell-differentiation:satellite}
A muscle grows in the adult not by adding fibres but by enlarging
them: training adds \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{myofibrils} to each fibre, and \emph{satellite
cells} fuse into it to supply the extra nuclei a larger cytoplasm
needs. After injury the same cells divide, re-express \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD}, fuse
and rebuild the damaged segment within weeks --- the embryonic
programme run again in the adult. In muscular dystrophy the fibres
lack dystrophin, a protein that ties the \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{myofibrils} to the
membrane, and tear during contraction; the satellite cells repair
them again and again until, after years, the stock is exhausted and
fibrous tissue takes the muscle's place.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: A Muscle Built and Rebuilt}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a muscle followed from somite to
fibre and through injury: the numbers of myoblasts, fusions and
nuclei, the bistable switch analysed, a repair timed, and training
quantified, ending on the fibre's nuclear count, the switch
threshold and the repair time}]
\label{pb:b2:cell-differentiation:1}
A thigh muscle of an adult has \num{500000} fibres, each
\qty{20}{cm} long and \qty{60}{\micro m} in diameter, with one
nucleus per \qty{25}{\micro m} of length; \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomeres} are
\qty{2.5}{\micro m}. Embryonic \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblasts} divide every \qty{12}{h}
while FGF is present. Switch model: $a = 1$, $K = 1$, $b = 0.02$, $k
= 0.4$. After an injury destroying \qty{10}{\%} of each fibre's
length, the satellite cells of the injured segment (\num{4} per
millimetre of fibre) divide every \qty{18}{h} for a time, then fuse.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The fibre.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the number of \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:fibre}{sarcomeres} in series in one fibre and
        the number of nuclei.
  \item How many \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblasts} fused to make one fibre? The whole muscle?
  \item Compute the volume of one fibre and of the muscle (in
        litres).
  \item Estimate the cytoplasmic volume served by one nucleus, and
        compare with an ordinary cell of \qty{2000}{\micro m^3}.
  \item Why do the nuclei lie at the periphery of a mature fibre?
  \item The muscle's \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{myoblasts} descended from \num{2000} somite
        cells. How many doublings did they need, and how many days,
        to reach the number of question 2?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The switch.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Write the steady-state condition and show that $m \approx
        0.055$ satisfies it.
  \item Show that $m \approx 2.1$ satisfies it.
  \item Show that there is a third solution near $m = 0.41$, and
        explain from the signs of $\mathrm{d}m/\mathrm{d}t$ on either
        side that it is unstable.
  \item A pulse of inducer raises $m$ to $0.6$; another to $0.3$.
        What is the fate of each cell?
  \item The basal synthesis $b$ is raised to $0.4$ by a \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation}.
        Show that the low state disappears. What does the cell do?
  \item Explain how this model accounts for commitment surviving
        cell division, and what must be true of $m$ at each division.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Repair.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many satellite cells does one fibre carry, and how many
        nuclei must be replaced after the injury?
  \item How many doublings must the satellite cells undergo to
        supply them, and how long does that take?
  \item Compare with the observed two to three weeks for repair, and
        say what else the time includes.
  \item Why must the satellite cells re-express \omterm{def:b2:cell-differentiation:myogenesis}{MyoD} before fusing,
        and why must they stop dividing first?
  \item Each round of repair uses up some satellite cells. If the
        pool falls by \qty{5}{\%} per injury, how many injuries
        before it is halved?
  \item How many satellite cells per fibre remain after twenty such
        injuries?
  \item Explain why a dystrophic muscle, whose fibres tear at every
        contraction, is replaced by fibrous tissue after years.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Training.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Training thickens each fibre to \qty{80}{\micro m}. By what
        factor does the fibre's volume grow, and how many extra
        nuclei must satellite cells supply if the ratio of
        question 4 is kept?
  \item Compute the muscle's new volume.
  \item Endurance training converts \qty{30}{\%} of the fast fibres
        to slow ones. What has changed in those fibres and what has
        not?
  \item Explain why an adult muscle enlarges by growth of fibres
        rather than by adding fibres, using the fact that fibres do
        not divide.
  \item A drug blocks the fusion of satellite cells. Predict the
        response of the muscle to training and to injury.
  \item State the result: the fibre's nuclear count, the switch
        threshold, and the time for satellite cells to supply the
        nuclei of a repair.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
