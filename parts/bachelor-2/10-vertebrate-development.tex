\chapter{Embryonic Development of Vertebrates}\label{ch:b2:vertebrate-development}

A frog's egg is a sphere two millimetres across, dark on top and
pale below. Within a day it has divided into thousands of cells;
within two, a sheet of those cells has rolled inside through a
groove to make a gut and lay a rod along the back; within three, the
sheet above the rod has folded into a tube that will be the brain
and spinal cord. Nothing has been added but water. The information
that turns one cell into a swimming tadpole was in the egg and its
genome, and the way it unfolds --- \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{cleavage}, gastrulation,
neurulation, the induction of each part by its neighbours --- is
recognisably the same in a fish, a chick and a mouse. This chapter
follows a vertebrate embryo through those steps and describes the
experiments that showed how each part of it is told what to become.

\section{From egg to blastula}

\begin{definition}[The egg and its axes]\label{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}
\index{animal pole}\index{vegetal pole}\index{yolk}\index{cleavage}
A vertebrate egg is polarised before fertilisation: the
\emph{animal pole} holds the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm, the
\emph{vegetal pole} the \emph{yolk}, the store of protein and lipid
on which the embryo will live until it feeds. The amount of yolk
sets the pattern of everything that follows: a sea urchin or mammal
egg has almost none and cleaves completely into equal cells; a frog
egg has a moderate amount and cleaves completely but unequally
(small cells above, large yolky cells below); a fish or bird egg is
nearly all yolk and cleaves only in a disc of cytoplasm on top of it.
In the frog, the point where the sperm enters fixes the second axis:
the cortex rotates thirty degrees away from it, exposing a
\emph{grey crescent} opposite the entry point, and that side will
become the back. \emph{Cleavage} is a series of rapid mitoses with
no growth --- the cells halve in size at each --- driven by maternal
mRNAs and proteins stored in the egg, the embryo's own genes staying
silent until the \emph{\omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}{mid-blastula} transition}, some twelve
divisions in.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Cleavage arithmetic]\label{thm:b2:vertebrate-development:cleavage}
After $k$ synchronous \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{cleavages} an egg of volume $V_0$ is $2^{k}$
cells of mean volume $V_0/2^{k}$ and radius $r_0/2^{k/3}$, and the
total surface has grown by $2^{k/3}$; the ratio of nuclear to
cytoplasmic volume, which starts near $10^{-4}$ in a frog egg, rises
by $2^{k}$ and reaches that of an ordinary cell ($\sim 0.1$) after
about ten divisions. It is this rising ratio --- the titration of a
fixed maternal stock of some inhibitor by an exponentially growing
amount of DNA --- that triggers the \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}{mid-blastula} transition:
divisions slow, become asynchronous, and the zygotic genome
switches on.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Volume is conserved in \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{cleavage}, so each of $2^{k}$ cells has $V_0
2^{-k}$ and, as a sphere, radius $r_0 2^{-k/3}$; their total area is
$2^{k}\cdot 4\pi r_0^{2}\, 2^{-2k/3} = 4\pi r_0^{2}\, 2^{k/3}$. Each
cell keeps one nucleus of fixed size, so the ratio scales as $2^{k}$
from $10^{-4}$: $2^{10} = 1024$ brings it to $0.1$. The frog's
transition falls at the twelfth division, consistent with a
threshold reached a little later.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Newport and Kirschner (1982) added extra DNA to frog eggs and
found the transition came earlier by as many divisions as the DNA
was in excess; removing cytoplasm had the same effect. The clock is
not time or a count of divisions but a ratio.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Blastula]\label{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}
\index{blastula}\index{blastocoel}
\omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{Cleavage} ends in a \emph{blastula}: a hollow ball (frog, sea urchin)
or a disc (fish, bird) of a few thousand cells around a cavity, the
\emph{blastocoel}. Its cells are still unspecialised in appearance,
but they are not equivalent: a \emph{fate map}, made by staining
patches of the surface with dyes and following them, shows that the
animal cap will give skin and nervous system (\emph{ectoderm}), the
equatorial belt muscle, skeleton, kidney and blood
(\emph{mesoderm}), and the vegetal mass the gut and its organs
(\emph{endoderm}). These three \emph{germ layers} are the same in
every vertebrate, and the next step is to put them in their places:
the mesoderm and endoderm are outside and must go in.
\end{definition}

\section{Gastrulation and neurulation}

\begin{proposition}[Gastrulation]\label{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:gastrulation}
\index{gastrulation}\index{blastopore}\index{archenteron}\index{notochord}
\emph{Gastrulation} is the set of cell movements that turns the
one-layered \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}{blastula} into a three-layered \emph{gastrula}. In the
frog it begins below the grey crescent: cells there change shape,
contracting their outer ends into bottles, and the surface buckles
into a groove, the \emph{blastopore}. Through it the mesoderm and
endoderm stream inside (\emph{involution}), rolling over the lip
and moving forward along the roof of the cavity; the animal cap
spreads down to cover the outside (\emph{epiboly}); the blastopore
closes to a ring around the last yolky cells. Inside, a new cavity
lined with endoderm, the \emph{archenteron}, is the future gut, and
the mesoderm that rolled in first, along the midline, has become a
stiff rod, the \emph{notochord}, the axis of every vertebrate embryo
and the structure that names the phylum. In birds and mammals the
same movements happen through a slit, the \emph{primitive streak},
in a flat disc; in fish the disc spreads over the \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{yolk}. The
choreography differs with the \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{yolk}; the result --- ectoderm outside,
endoderm lining the gut, mesoderm between, notochord in the midline
--- is the same.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Neurulation and the body plan]\label{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}
\index{neurulation}\index{neural tube}\index{somite}\index{neural crest}
The ectoderm above the notochord thickens into a \emph{neural
plate}, whose edges rise as folds and meet in the midline to form
the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural tube}}, the future brain and spinal cord, which
sinks beneath the skin; cells from the crest of the folds, the
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural crest}}, migrate away to make the peripheral nerves,
the pigment cells, the adrenal medulla and much of the face. On
each side of the notochord the mesoderm segments into blocks, the
\emph{somites}, one pair after another from head to tail, which give
the vertebrae, the trunk muscles and the dermis; lateral mesoderm
splits into the layers that line the body cavity and make the heart
and limbs; the endoderm tube buds off the lungs, liver and pancreas.
By the end of neurulation the embryo has the vertebrate body plan
--- a dorsal nerve cord over a notochord, a ventral gut, segmented
muscle, a head --- at a length of a few millimetres, and the rest
of development is the elaboration of organs from this plan
(\cref{ch:b2:limb-organogenesis}).
\end{proposition}

\section{Induction: how cells learn where they are}

\begin{definition}[Induction and competence]\label{def:b2:vertebrate-development:induction}
\index{induction (embryonic)}\index{competence}\index{organiser}
\emph{Induction} is the process by which one group of cells changes
the fate of a neighbouring group by a signal; the responding cells
must be \emph{competent} --- able to answer, which they are only for
a limited time. Development is a chain of inductions: the vegetal
cells induce mesoderm in the cells above them; the dorsal mesoderm
--- the \emph{organiser} at the blastopore lip --- induces the
neural plate in the ectoderm above it and patterns the mesoderm on
either side; the notochord induces the floor of the \omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural tube}; the
optic vesicle, a bud of the brain, induces a lens in the skin it
touches; the lens induces the cornea. Each induced tissue becomes an
inducer of the next. The signals are a handful of secreted protein
families used over and over --- and the same ones that pattern a
limb (\cref{ch:b2:limb-organogenesis}) and, in the adult, run the
signalling of \cref{ch:b2:cell-signalling}.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Spemann and Mangold (1924) cut the dorsal blastopore lip from an
early gastrula of one newt species and grafted it onto the ventral
side of another, pigmented differently so that host and graft cells
could be told apart. The graft did not simply become what it would
have become in place: it rolled in, formed notochord, and
\emph{induced the host's own ventral ectoderm} to make a second
\omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural tube} and, beside it, host somites --- a complete second
embryo, largely of host cells, joined belly to belly with the first.
No other piece of the gastrula could do this. They called the lip
the \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:induction}{organiser}; its signals (proteins that block the ventralising
factor BMP) were identified seventy years later.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[A morphogen gradient]\label{thm:b2:vertebrate-development:gradient}
\index{morphogen}
Many inductions work by a \emph{morphogen}: a signal secreted by a
source, spreading through the tissue and read by cells according to
its local concentration --- above one threshold one fate, above a
higher one another. A morphogen produced at a plane source at rate
$j$ per unit area, diffusing with coefficient $D$ and degraded at
rate $k$, has at steady state the exponential profile
\[
c(x) = c_0\,e^{-x/\lambda}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{D/k}, \qquad c_0 = \frac{j}{\sqrt{Dk}},
\]
so that the distance at which the concentration falls below a
threshold $T$ is $x_T = \lambda\ln(c_0/T)$: a tissue can be
patterned into bands of fixed width by fixed thresholds. With $D =
\qty{1e-12}{m^2/s}$ (a protein slowed by binding as it moves through
tissue) and $k = \qty{1e-3}{s^{-1}}$ (a half-life of about twelve
minutes), $\lambda = \sqrt{10^{-9}}\,\unit{m} \approx
\qty{32}{\micro m}$: a few cell diameters,
the scale over which embryonic fields are patterned. Doubling the
source doubles $c_0$ and shifts every boundary outward by $\lambda
\ln 2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At steady state diffusion balances degradation: $D\,c'' = k\,c$,
whose decaying solution is $c = c_0 e^{-x/\lambda}$ with $\lambda^2 =
D/k$. The flux leaving the source is $-Dc'(0) = Dc_0/\lambda = j$,
which gives $c_0 = j\lambda/D = j/\sqrt{Dk}$. Setting $c(x_T) = T$
gives $x_T$; changing $c_0$ to $2c_0$ adds $\lambda\ln 2$ to it.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Reading the gradient]\label{ex:b2:vertebrate-development:reading}
In the frog gastrula, cells of the marginal zone exposed to graded
amounts of a signal of the activin family become, from high to low
dose, notochord, muscle, kidney and blood; dissociated animal-cap
cells put in a dish with a threshold dose switch fates within a
factor of two of concentration. In the \omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural tube}, a signal from
the notochord and floor plate spreads upward and specifies, from
the bottom, motor neurons and then successive classes of
interneurons at thresholds a few cell diameters apart. The
organism's coordinates are written in concentrations.
\end{example}

\section{Fate, commitment and the timing of decisions}

\begin{proposition}[When a cell's fate is fixed]\label{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:commitment}
\index{determination}\index{regulation (embryonic)}
A cell's \emph{fate} is what it will normally become; its
\emph{potency} is what it can become if moved; it is
\emph{determined} when its fate no longer changes with its
surroundings. Early embryos \emph{regulate}: each of the first two
frog blastomeres, separated, makes a whole small tadpole, and the
first cells of a mammal embryo are all totipotent --- which is how
identical twins arise. Determination comes gradually and at
different times for different tissues: a piece of early gastrula
ectoderm grafted to the belly becomes belly skin, the same piece
taken from a late gastrula makes a neural plate wherever it is put;
by the tailbud stage a limb field transplanted to the flank makes a
limb there. In most invertebrates, by contrast, fate is fixed early
by the cytoplasm each cell inherits (\emph{mosaic} development) and
a separated blastomere makes only its part. The vertebrate strategy
--- keep cells flexible and tell them what to do by induction ---
is what makes twinning, regeneration and the \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:induction}{organiser} experiment
possible.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Spemann (1901) tied a hair loop around a newt egg at the two-cell
stage: if the loop lay in the plane of the grey crescent, so that
each half received part of it, two complete embryos formed; if it
lay perpendicular, so that one half had the whole crescent, that
half made an embryo and the other a ball of belly tissue. The
crescent --- the future \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:induction}{organiser} --- was the one part that could
not be divided.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The timetable of a frog]\label{ex:b2:vertebrate-development:timetable}
At \qty{18}{\celsius}: fertilisation; first \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{cleavage} at \qty{1.5}{h},
then every half hour; \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}{blastula} of \num{4000} cells at \qty{7}{h};
\omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}{mid-blastula} transition at \qty{8}{h}; gastrulation \qty{10}{h} to
\qty{20}{h}; neural folds close at \qty{30}{h}; heart beats at
\qty{2.5}{d}; hatching and feeding at \qty{4}{d}, as a tadpole of
\qty{6}{mm}. A zebrafish does the same in one day, a chick in three,
a mouse in nine; a human embryo gastrulates in the third week and
closes its \omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural tube} by the fourth --- before most women know they
are pregnant, which is why folate, needed for \omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural tube} closure,
is prescribed before conception.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: A Frog Embryo Timed and Patterned}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a frog embryo followed through
cleavage, the mid-blastula transition, gastrulation and induction,
with the morphogen gradient computed and the classical grafts
predicted, ending on the cell count and time of the transition and
the widths of the fate bands}]
\label{pb:b2:vertebrate-development:1}
A frog egg has radius \qty{0.7}{mm} and a nucleus of radius
\qty{0.03}{mm} whose DNA content is $c$; each \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{cleavage} takes
\qty{30}{min} after a first one of \qty{75}{min}. The \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}{mid-blastula}
transition occurs when total DNA reaches $4000\,c$. Morphogen: $D =
\qty{1e-12}{m^2/s}$, half-life \qty{20}{min}, source concentration
$c_0$, thresholds at $0.4\,c_0$ and $0.08\,c_0$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{Cleavage}.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the volume of the egg and of its nucleus, and the
        initial nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio.
  \item After how many \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{cleavages} does the DNA reach $4000\,c$? How
        many cells?
  \item At what time after fertilisation?
  \item What is the mean cell radius then, and the ratio of nuclear
        to cell volume if nuclei keep their size? Comment.
  \item By what factor has the total cell surface grown?
  \item Each \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{cleavage} needs the genome copied: how much DNA has been
        synthesised in total, in units of $c$, and why does the embryo
        not need to transcribe its genes to do it?
  \item What changes at the transition, and what experiment shows
        that it is triggered by a ratio and not by a count?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Gastrulation.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Where does the blastopore form relative to the sperm entry
        point, and what fixed that position?
  \item List, in order, the tissues that roll in through the
        blastopore and their positions afterward.
  \item Gastrulation takes \qty{10}{h} and the involuting sheet
        travels \qty{2}{mm}. Compute the mean cell speed in
        micrometres per minute and compare with a crawling
        fibroblast (\qty{1}{\micro m/min}).
  \item Why is gastrulation impossible before the \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}{mid-blastula}
        transition?
  \item Explain in one sentence why the notochord, and not the
        \omterm{prop:b2:vertebrate-development:neurulation}{neural tube}, is the defining structure of the chordates.
  \item A drug that blocks cell-shape change is applied at the start
        of gastrulation. Predict the embryo.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The gradient.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute $k$ from the half-life, and $\lambda$.
  \item Compute the positions of the two thresholds.
  \item Give the widths of the three fate bands in micrometres and in
        cells of \qty{15}{\micro m}.
  \item The source is halved. Recompute the two positions and state
        which band shrinks most.
  \item A cell at \qty{60}{\micro m} from the source is moved to
        \qty{10}{\micro m} before the gradient is read; then after.
        What does it become in each case, and why?
  \item Explain why an exponential profile gives boundaries whose
        positions depend on the logarithm of the source strength,
        and why this makes patterning robust.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Grafts.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Predict the result of grafting a dorsal lip to the ventral
        side of an early gastrula, and of grafting ventral marginal
        zone to the dorsal side.
  \item Predict the result of grafting a dorsal lip to the ventral
        side of a \emph{neurula}, and explain the difference.
  \item A frog embryo at the two-cell stage is split along the plane
        of the grey crescent; another perpendicular to it. Predict
        both.
  \item An animal cap is cut from a \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:blastula}{blastula} and cultured alone; the
        same cap is cultured next to vegetal cells. Predict the
        tissues formed.
  \item Why did Spemann and Mangold's result require host and graft
        to be distinguishable, and what would a graft of the wrong
        species have shown?
  \item State the result: the \omterm{def:b2:vertebrate-development:egg}{cleavage} number and time of the
        transition, its cell count, and the three band widths.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
