\chapter{Bacteria and Antibiotic Resistance}\label{ch:g11:antibiotic-resistance}

In 1941 a policeman dying of blood poisoning from a scratch was given
the first doses of penicillin ever used on a patient. He recovered
within days --- and relapsed and died when the supply, painstakingly
extracted from mould, ran out. Within a decade penicillin was
manufactured by the tonne and infections that had killed for
millennia became curable in a week. Within two decades, bacteria that
penicillin no longer touched were common in every hospital. This
chapter is about why: how bacteria vary, how an \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} sorts them,
and how the arithmetic of a population of billions turns a rare
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} into a ward-wide problem.

\section{How bacteria vary}

\begin{proposition}[Bacterial reproduction and variation]\label{prop:g11:antibiotic-resistance:variation}
A bacterium reproduces by dividing in two after copying its single
\omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}: the daughters are, apart from copying errors, genetically
identical to the mother --- a \emph{clone}. In a rich medium a
division every 20 minutes turns one \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} into a billion in ten hours.
Genetic variation in the clone comes from two sources:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}} (\cref{ch:g11:mutations}), rare per division
        but frequent in a population of billions;
  \item \emph{horizontal gene transfer}\index{horizontal gene transfer}:
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} received from another bacterium, of the same species or
        another, most often as a \emph{plasmid} --- a small circle of
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, separate from the \omterm{def:g11:cell-cycle-mitosis:chromosome}{chromosome}, carrying a few \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} and
        able to pass from \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} to \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} through a bridge of membrane.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[The arithmetic of a population]\label{ex:g11:antibiotic-resistance:arithmetic}
A resistance \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} arises about once in $10^8$ divisions. A flask
of $10^9$ bacteria has therefore had, in the divisions that produced
it, some ten such \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}; an infected wound or a gut, with $10^{12}$
bacteria, some ten thousand. Every large population of bacteria
contains, before any \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} is used, \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} resistant to it ---
the replica plates of \cref{ch:g11:mutations} proved it.
\end{example}

\section{Antibiotics}

\begin{definition}[Antibiotic]\label{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}
An \emph{antibiotic}\index{antibiotic} is a molecule that kills
bacteria or stops their growth, at concentrations harmless to the
patient's \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, by acting on a structure or a process that bacteria
have and human \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} lack: penicillin and its relatives block the
building of the bacterial wall; streptomycin and tetracycline jam the
bacterial \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{ribosome}, which differs from ours; others block the
bacterial \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzymes} of \omterm{def:g11:dna-replication:replication}{DNA replication}. Most were first found in
moulds and soil bacteria, which make them against their competitors.
Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, which have none of these
targets.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Mechanisms of resistance]\label{prop:g11:antibiotic-resistance:mechanisms}
A bacterium is \emph{resistant}\index{antibiotic resistance} to an
\omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} when it grows at concentrations that stop its sensitive
relatives. Resistance has a genetic basis of one of a few kinds: a
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} for an \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:enzyme}{enzyme} that destroys the \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} (penicillinases cut
penicillin in two); a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} altering the \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic}'s target so
that the drug no longer binds (a changed ribosomal \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein}); a pump
that expels the drug; a wall that does not let it in. Each is an
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} or a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, and each is inherited --- vertically by the clone
and, when on a plasmid, horizontally by neighbours.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Reading an antibiogram]\label{met:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiogram}
An \emph{antibiogram} tests a patient's bacteria against several
\omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotics} at once.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Spread the bacteria evenly on a plate; lay paper discs each
        soaked with one \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic}; incubate overnight.
  \item The \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} diffuses out of the disc, its concentration
        falling with distance; the bacteria grow into a lawn except
        where the concentration stops them: a clear \emph{zone of
        inhibition} around each disc.
  \item Measure each zone's diameter and compare with the threshold
        established for that \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic}: a diameter above the
        threshold means sensitive, below it resistant.
  \item The largest zones among the sensitive results guide the
        prescription; a disc with no zone at all is an \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic}
        the strain ignores.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{How resistance spreads}

\begin{proposition}[Selection by the antibiotic]\label{prop:g11:antibiotic-resistance:selection}
An \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} does not create resistant bacteria; it \emph{selects}
them. In a population where a few \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} carry a resistance \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele},
the drug kills or stops the sensitive majority and leaves the
resistant few to multiply without competition: within days the
population is resistant. The more an \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} is used --- in
patients, in animals, in the environment --- the more often this
sorting happens, and the more resistant the bacteria of a hospital, a
farm or a country become. An incomplete course, which kills the most
sensitive \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} and spares the least, selects most efficiently of all.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Lederberg's replica plates showed the resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} present before
exposure. Cultures grown for a week in rising concentrations of an
\omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} end up resistant to a hundred times the initial dose, and
their resistance is inherited and mapped to specific \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}.
Countries that use the most \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotics} per person have the highest
proportions of resistant strains; hospital wards that reduce their
use see resistance fall over months. A strain of the skin bacterium
\emph{Staphylococcus aureus} resistant to penicillin appeared within
four years of the drug's introduction and now accounts for most
hospital strains; the strain resistant to the replacement drug
followed the replacement by two years.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[From one cell to a ward]\label{ex:g11:antibiotic-resistance:ward}
A patient treated with penicillin carries, among $10^{10}$ bacteria,
a hundred resistant ones. In three days the sensitive ones are gone
and the resistant clone has taken the place: $10^{10}$ resistant
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. On a nurse's hands, on a bed rail, in the next patient, the
clone continues --- and if its resistance \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is on a plasmid, it
hands it to other species along the way. Nothing in this sequence
required the \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} to act on the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}; it only removed the
competition.
\end{example}

\section{Keeping antibiotics working}

\begin{proposition}[Consequences and responses]\label{prop:g11:antibiotic-resistance:consequences}
Resistant infections are longer, costlier and more often fatal;
some strains now resist every available drug. Since resistance is
driven by use, the response is to use \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotics} less and better:
only for bacterial infections (never for viral colds and flu),
chosen after an antibiogram where possible, at the full dose for the
full course, with the narrowest drug that works; to limit their use
in farming; to prevent infection (hygiene, vaccination) so that fewer
courses are needed; and to isolate carriers of resistant strains in
hospitals. New \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotics} buy time; the arithmetic of selection
guarantees that resistance to each will appear.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{remark}[Evolution in a week]\label{rem:g11:antibiotic-resistance:evolution}
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{Mutation} supplying variation at random, the environment sorting it,
the descendants of the survivors inheriting the difference: the
bacteria of this chapter run through, in a week, the process that the
final year will describe over millions of years for whales and oaks.
\omterm{prop:g11:antibiotic-resistance:mechanisms}{Antibiotic resistance} is evolution by natural selection observed in
real time, with humans as the selecting agent --- and the clearest
demonstration that the process is not a theory about the past but a
fact about any population that varies and reproduces.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The Ward}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- an outbreak on a hospital ward
followed from a single mutant cell: the arithmetic of a colony, an
antibiogram read, a course interrupted, and the policy that stops the
spread}]
\label{pb:g11:antibiotic-resistance:1}
A patient's wound is infected with $10^{10}$ bacteria of one species.
The bacteria divide every 30 minutes; a \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} giving resistance to
the \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} amoxicillin occurs once per $10^8$ divisions. The
\omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} kills 90\% of sensitive \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} every 6 hours and does not
affect resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, which keep dividing.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Before treatment.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many divisions produced the $10^{10}$ bacteria from the
        first \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}? (Count the number of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}.)
  \item Estimate the number of resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} present before any
        \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} is given.
  \item Explain why the patient's infection is nevertheless described
        as "sensitive" on the antibiogram.
  \item The antibiogram shows zones of \qty{24}{mm} for amoxicillin
        (threshold 17), \qty{12}{mm} for erythromycin (threshold 20)
        and none for penicillin. Interpret the three results.
  \item The doctor prescribes amoxicillin for 7 days. Why not
        penicillin, and why not the drug with the largest zone if
        one had been larger?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- During treatment.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many sensitive \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} remain after 1 day? After 3 days?
  \item Starting from your answer to question 2, how many resistant
        \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} are there after 1 day if they double every 30 minutes
        without limit? (Use $2^{48} \approx \num{2.8e14}$.) Why is
        this figure unrealistic, and what limits it?
  \item Suppose the resistant clone is limited to the space the
        sensitive \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} vacate, so that it cannot exceed $10^{10}$.
        Roughly when does it fill that space?
  \item Sketch, or describe, the curves of sensitive, resistant and
        total \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} over the 7 days.
  \item In fact the patient's immune system clears the last few
        million \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} of any kind within days. Explain why the
        outcome depends on whether the resistant clone grows faster
        than the immune system clears it.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The course interrupted.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The patient stops after 2 days, feeling well. How many
        sensitive \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} remain, and what fraction of the surviving
        population is resistant?
  \item The bacteria regrow to $10^{10}$ over the following days.
        What proportion is now resistant, if resistant and sensitive
        \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} grow at the same rate?
  \item The patient relapses and is given amoxicillin again. Predict
        the result.
  \item An antibiogram of the relapse shows no zone for amoxicillin.
        Explain the change from the first antibiogram.
  \item The resistance \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is on a plasmid. What further risk does
        that add for the ward?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The ward's policy.}
The ward treats 400 patients a year with amoxicillin; 30\% of
infections isolated on the ward are now resistant, against 5\% in the
town.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Explain the difference between the ward and the town in terms
        of selection pressure.
  \item The ward decides: antibiograms before every prescription,
        full courses, hand hygiene, and isolation of carriers of
        resistant strains. Say which mechanism of this chapter each
        measure acts on.
  \item After two years resistance on the ward is 12\%. Explain why
        it fell, and why it did not return to 5\%.
  \item Why would a new \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic}, introduced on the ward, not solve
        the problem for more than a few years?
  \item State the result: the number of resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} the wound
        held before any drug, the day on which they inherited it, and
        the single habit of the patient that turned a curable
        infection into an outbreak.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
