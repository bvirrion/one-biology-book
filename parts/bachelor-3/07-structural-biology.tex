\chapter{Structural Biology of Proteins}\label{ch:b3:structural-biology}

Max Perutz began work on the structure of haemoglobin in 1937 and
published it in 1960: twenty-two years to see, at a \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{resolution} of half
a nanometre, how four chains of a hundred and fifty amino acids \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold}
around four haems. A haemoglobin molecule \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{folds} itself in a few
milliseconds. Between these two facts lies the subject of this
chapter: what a protein's shape is, why a chain of amino acids has one
shape rather than another, how it finds that shape so fast among an
astronomical number of alternatives, what goes wrong when it does not,
how the shapes are seen --- by X-rays, by magnetic resonance, by
electrons, and now by computation --- and how a protein moves between
shapes to do its work, with haemoglobin's switch between a tense and a
relaxed form as the model for every allosteric protein since.

\section{What a protein's structure is}

\begin{definition}[Levels of structure]\label{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}
\index{secondary structure}\index{alpha helix}\index{beta sheet}\index{tertiary structure}\index{domain}\index{fold}
The \emph{primary structure} is the sequence of residues. The
\emph{secondary structure} is the local regular conformation of the
backbone, stabilised by hydrogen bonds between backbone carbonyls and
amides: the \emph{$\alpha$-helix}, a right-handed spiral of $3.6$
residues per turn, rising \qty{0.15}{nm} per residue (\qty{0.54}{nm}
per turn), each carbonyl bonded to the amide four residues on; and the
\emph{$\beta$-sheet}, in which extended strands lie side by side,
parallel or antiparallel, bonded to their neighbours, the side chains
alternating above and below the sheet. The \emph{tertiary structure} is
the fold of a whole chain in three dimensions; a compact, independently
folding unit of \numrange{50}{250} residues within it is a \emph{domain},
and the arrangement of a domain's secondary elements is its \emph{fold}.
The \emph{quaternary structure} is the assembly of several chains:
haemoglobin's $\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}$. About \num{1300} folds account for
all known domains, and a few dozen of them --- the Rossmann fold, the
TIM barrel, the immunoglobulin fold --- for a large fraction of all
proteins: nature reuses folds and varies their sequences.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[What holds a fold together]\label{prop:b3:structural-biology:forces}
\index{hydrophobic effect}\index{marginal stability}
A folded protein is stabilised by the \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:structural-biology:forces}{hydrophobic effect}} ---
burying non-polar side chains in a core away from water, which frees
the water molecules that would otherwise be ordered around them --- by
the hydrogen bonds of the \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{secondary structure} and of buried polar
groups, by van der Waals packing of a core as dense as a crystal, by
occasional salt bridges, and, in secreted proteins, by disulfide
bonds. The \omterm{prop:b3:structural-biology:forces}{hydrophobic effect} supplies most of the drive; the hydrogen
bonds mostly pay for themselves (a backbone bonded to water in the
unfolded state is bonded to itself in the folded one) and instead
dictate \emph{which} \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold}. The net free energy of folding is small,
$\Delta G \approx -20$ to \qty{-60}{kJ/mol} --- the strength of a
handful of hydrogen bonds for a molecule with hundreds --- because the
loss of conformational entropy on folding nearly cancels the gain in
enthalpy. Proteins are \emph{marginally stable}: a few degrees, a
change of pH, a single mutation in the core can unfold them, and this
marginality is what lets them move.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Anfinsen (1961) unfolded ribonuclease~A with urea and a reducing agent,
breaking its four disulfides and destroying its activity; on removing
both, the enzyme refolded, re-formed the correct four of the $105$
possible disulfide pairings, and recovered full activity. No template,
no enzyme, no information beyond the sequence was present: the native
structure is the thermodynamically most stable state accessible to the
chain, and the sequence encodes it. Reoxidising the disulfides while
the chain was still in urea gave a scrambled mixture with \qty{1}{\%}
activity, which then unscrambled to the native form when allowed to
\omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold}: the chain finds the minimum by itself.
\end{proof}

\section{How a chain finds its fold}

\begin{theorem}[Levinthal's paradox]\label{thm:b3:structural-biology:levinthal}
\index{Levinthal paradox}\index{folding funnel}
If each of the $n$ residues of a protein could adopt $k$ backbone
conformations independently, the chain would have $k^{n}$
conformations. With $k = 3$ and $n = 100$ that is $3^{100} \approx
5\times 10^{47}$; sampling them at one per picosecond ($10^{12}$ per
second) would take $5\times 10^{35}$ seconds, about $10^{28}$ years.
Proteins of this size \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold} in milliseconds to seconds. Therefore
folding is not a search through conformations at random: the energy
surface is a \emph{funnel}, in which almost every partial conformation
is biased toward the native state, and the chain descends by local
moves that are each downhill on average.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The count is the multiplication principle; the time is the count
divided by the sampling rate, $5\times 10^{47}/10^{12} = 5\times
10^{35}$ s, and a year is $3\times 10^{7}$ s. The conclusion is the
contrapositive: a random search of that size is impossible in the
observed time, so the search is not random. What makes it non-random
is that an interaction formed in one part of the chain persists while
others form (hydrophobic collapse and helix formation happen in
microseconds and reduce the space to be searched), and that partially
correct structures are on average lower in energy than wrong ones, so
that the descent is guided --- a funnel rather than a golf course with a
single hole.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Chaperones]\label{def:b3:structural-biology:chaperones}
\index{chaperone}\index{Hsp70}\index{chaperonin}\index{GroEL}
A \emph{molecular chaperone} is a protein that assists the folding of
others without becoming part of them or supplying information about
the \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold}: it prevents the aggregation that competes with folding in the
crowded cytoplasm. \emph{Hsp70} binds exposed hydrophobic segments of
nascent or unfolded chains, releasing them on ATP hydrolysis for
another attempt; the \emph{chaperonin} GroEL (Hsp60 in mitochondria,
TRiC in the eukaryotic cytosol) is a double barrel of fourteen subunits
whose central cavity, capped by GroES, encloses a single chain of up to
\qty{60}{kDa} in isolation for about ten seconds --- an Anfinsen cage
--- and ejects it whether folded or not, to try again. About a tenth
of newly made bacterial proteins pass through GroEL, and a cell whose
chaperones are overwhelmed --- by heat, which is why most are
\emph{heat-shock proteins} induced by a rise in temperature --- fills
with aggregates.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Amyloid]\label{def:b3:structural-biology:amyloid}
\index{amyloid}\index{cross-beta}\index{prion}\index{misfolding disease}
\emph{Amyloid} is an ordered aggregate of protein in which the chains
stack into unbranched fibrils \qtyrange{5}{10}{nm} wide, the strands of
each chain running perpendicular to the fibril axis and hydrogen-bonded
to the strands of the next --- the \emph{cross-$\beta$} structure, the
same whatever the protein. Almost any protein can form it under
destabilising conditions; in the \emph{misfolding diseases} particular
proteins do so in the body: amyloid-$\beta$ and tau in Alzheimer's
disease, $\alpha$-synuclein in Parkinson's, huntingtin, transthyretin,
islet amyloid in type~2 diabetes. A \emph{prion} is an amyloid that
propagates: the misfolded form of the prion protein PrP converts the
normal form on contact into more of itself, so that the ``infection''
carries no nucleic acid, only a shape --- the mechanism of scrapie,
of bovine spongiform encephalopathy and of Creutzfeldt--Jakob disease,
established by Prusiner from 1982 against long resistance.
\end{definition}

\section{Seeing a protein}

\begin{definition}[Structural methods]\label{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}
\index{X-ray crystallography}\index{NMR spectroscopy}\index{cryo-electron microscopy}\index{resolution}
\emph{X-ray crystallography} grows a crystal of the protein, exposes
it to a beam of X-rays and records the diffraction pattern; the
positions of the spots give the lattice and their intensities, once
the phases of the waves are recovered, give the electron density of the
unit cell, into which the chain is built. \emph{Nuclear magnetic
resonance} (NMR) measures, in solution, the couplings between the
nuclei of atoms close in space, from which distances and hence a
structure are computed; it works for proteins under about \qty{40}{kDa}
and reports their motions. \emph{Cryo-electron microscopy} images
thousands to millions of single molecules frozen in a thin film of
vitreous ice, each in a random orientation, and reconstructs the
three-dimensional density by combining them; since direct electron
detectors (2013) it reaches atomic resolution and needs no crystal, so
that large complexes, membrane proteins and flexible assemblies that
never crystallised are now solved routinely. The \emph{resolution} of a
structure is the smallest spacing at which two features are separated:
at \qty{0.35}{nm} the backbone and large side chains are placed, at
\qty{0.2}{nm} every atom, at \qty{0.1}{nm} hydrogens.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Kendrew (1958) obtained the first structure of a protein, sperm-whale
myoglobin, at \qty{0.6}{nm} --- a sausage of unexpected irregularity,
``more complicated and less symmetrical than anyone had predicted''
--- and at \qty{0.2}{nm} in 1960, when the $\alpha$-helices Pauling had
proposed from model-building in 1951 were seen for the first time.
Perutz (1960) solved haemoglobin at \qty{0.55}{nm} using the
heavy-atom method he had devised to obtain the phases: mercury atoms
attached to the protein change the intensities in a way that reveals
the missing information. Comparing the oxygenated and deoxygenated
forms (1970) he saw the subunits rotate against one another by
\qty{15}{\degree} on binding oxygen, the first allosteric transition
observed at atomic scale.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Solving a structure by crystallography]\label{met:b3:structural-biology:crystallography}
\index{Bragg's law}\index{phase problem}
(1) Purify milligrams of the protein and screen hundreds of conditions
(precipitant, pH, salt) for crystals; (2) flash-freeze a crystal and
collect diffraction images at a synchrotron as it rotates in the beam;
(3) index the spots to obtain the unit cell and symmetry; the highest
angle at which spots are seen sets the \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{resolution} by \omterm{met:b3:structural-biology:crystallography}{Bragg's law}, $d =
\lambda/(2\sin\theta)$; (4) solve the \emph{phase problem} --- the
detector records intensities but not phases --- by heavy atoms, by the
anomalous scattering of selenium in selenomethionine, or, for a protein
similar to a known one, by molecular replacement, placing the known
model in the cell; (5) compute the electron density, build the chain
into it and refine the model against the data until the disagreement
($R$-factor) is small; (6) validate: geometry, Ramachandran outliers,
and the fit to data the model was not refined against.
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[Prediction has joined the methods]\label{rem:b3:structural-biology:prediction}
Since 2020 a protein's structure is predicted from its sequence with
an accuracy that often rivals a \qty{0.3}{nm} experimental structure
(\cref{ch:b3:bioinformatics}). The prediction is a hypothesis about a
static native state; the experimental methods remain the arbiter, and
the only source of information about ligands, conformational changes,
complexes and the dynamics discussed below. Their roles have changed
--- a predicted model solves the phase problem by molecular
replacement, and directs which experiments are worth doing --- rather
than one replacing the other.
\end{remark}

\section{Allostery: haemoglobin as the model}

\begin{definition}[Allostery and cooperativity]\label{def:b3:structural-biology:allostery}
\index{allostery}\index{cooperativity}\index{Hill coefficient}\index{T state}\index{R state}
A protein is \emph{allosteric} when the binding of a ligand at one
site changes the affinity of another site, through a change of
conformation transmitted across the molecule. When the two sites bind
the same ligand and the effect is positive, binding is
\emph{cooperative}: the fractional saturation $Y$ rises with ligand
concentration as a sigmoid rather than a hyperbola, described
empirically by the \emph{Hill equation} $Y = [S]^{n_{H}}/(K^{n_{H}} +
[S]^{n_{H}})$ with a \emph{Hill coefficient} $n_{H} > 1$ (haemoglobin:
$n_{H} \approx 2.8$ for its four sites; myoglobin, one site: $n_{H} =
1$). Haemoglobin exists in two quaternary conformations, the
\emph{T state} (tense, low affinity, favoured when no oxygen is bound)
and the \emph{R state} (relaxed, high affinity), and switches between
them as a whole; protons, carbon dioxide and 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate
stabilise T and so lower the affinity --- the \omterm{prop:b3:structural-biology:mechanism}{Bohr effect}, by which
working tissue, acid and warm, takes more oxygen from the blood.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The Monod--Wyman--Changeux model]\label{thm:b3:structural-biology:mwc}
\index{Monod--Wyman--Changeux model}\index{concerted model}
Let a protein of $n$ identical sites exist in two conformations,
$T$ and $R$, in equilibrium $L = [T_{0}]/[R_{0}]$ in the absence of
ligand, every site of $R$ binding the ligand with dissociation constant
$K_{R}$ and every site of $T$ with $K_{T}$, $c = K_{R}/K_{T} < 1$. All
sites of a molecule switch together (the transition is
\emph{concerted}). Then with $\alpha = [S]/K_{R}$ the fractional
saturation is
\[
Y = \frac{\alpha(1+\alpha)^{n-1} + Lc\alpha(1+c\alpha)^{n-1}}
{(1+\alpha)^{n} + L(1+c\alpha)^{n}},
\]
and the fraction of molecules in the $R$ state is $\bar R =
(1+\alpha)^{n}/\bigl[(1+\alpha)^{n} + L(1+c\alpha)^{n}\bigr]$. For $L
\gg 1$ and $c \ll 1$ the curve is sigmoid: the first ligands bind to the
scarce, high-affinity $R$ molecules and tip the equilibrium, so that
binding recruits more $R$ and the affinity of the remaining sites
rises --- \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:allostery}{cooperativity} without any direct interaction between sites.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The species with $i$ ligands bound in the $R$ form has concentration
$[R_{0}]\binom{n}{i}\alpha^{i}$ (each of the $\binom{n}{i}$ choices of
occupied sites contributes $([S]/K_{R})^{i}$ by the law of mass
action), and in the $T$ form $[T_{0}]\binom{n}{i}(c\alpha)^{i}$, since
$[S]/K_{T} = c\alpha$. Summing over $i$ by the binomial theorem, the
total protein is $[R_{0}]\bigl[(1+\alpha)^{n} + L(1+c\alpha)^{n}\bigr]$
and the total ligand bound is $[R_{0}]\sum_{i} i\binom{n}{i}\bigl[
\alpha^{i} + L(c\alpha)^{i}\bigr] = [R_{0}]\,n\bigl[\alpha(1+\alpha)^{n-1}
+ Lc\alpha(1+c\alpha)^{n-1}\bigr]$, using $\sum_{i} i\binom{n}{i}x^{i} =
nx(1+x)^{n-1}$. Dividing bound ligand by $n$ times total protein gives
$Y$; the $R$ fraction is the $R$ terms over the total. When $\alpha$ is
small the $T$ terms dominate the denominator (because $L \gg 1$) and
$Y \approx c\alpha$, the low $T$ affinity; when $\alpha$ is large enough
that $(1+\alpha)^{n} > L(1+c\alpha)^{n}$ the $R$ terms take over and the
affinity is $K_{R}$: the switch between the two regimes is the sigmoid.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Haemoglobin in numbers]\label{ex:b3:structural-biology:hb-numbers}
Take $n = 4$, $K_{R} = \qty{1}{mmHg}$, $c = 0.01$ (so $K_{T} =
\qty{100}{mmHg}$) and $L = 3\times 10^{5}$. At the partial pressure of
oxygen in arterial blood, \qty{100}{mmHg}, $\alpha = 100$ and $Y =
0.97$; at \qty{40}{mmHg}, the venous value at rest, $Y = 0.78$; at
\qty{26}{mmHg}, $Y = 0.52$ --- the model reproduces the half-saturation
pressure of \qty{26}{mmHg}; at \qty{20}{mmHg}, in an exercising
muscle, $Y = 0.35$. A non-cooperative carrier of the same
half-saturation pressure would give $100/126 = 0.79$ in the lungs and
$20/46 = 0.43$ in the muscle, unloading $0.36$ of its capacity where
haemoglobin unloads $0.62$. \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:allostery}{Cooperativity} is what makes a carrier that
loads fully at one pressure and unloads mostly at a pressure only five
times lower.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Mechanism of the switch]\label{prop:b3:structural-biology:mechanism}
\index{Bohr effect}\index{2,3-bisphosphoglycerate}
In deoxyhaemoglobin the iron sits slightly out of the haem plane,
pulled toward the proximal histidine, and the haem is domed. Oxygen
binding flattens the haem and draws the iron, and with it the
histidine and the helix it belongs to, by about \qty{0.06}{nm} toward
the haem; that shift is amplified at the interface between the
$\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}$ and $\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}$ dimers, which rotate by
\qty{15}{\degree} and break the salt bridges that hold the \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:allostery}{T state}.
The effectors act on those same bridges: protons bind histidines whose
bridges exist only in T (the \omterm{prop:b3:structural-biology:mechanism}{Bohr effect}), carbon dioxide forms
carbamates with the amino termini that stabilise T, and
2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, a strongly negative small molecule, sits in
the central cavity between the $\beta$ chains, which is wide enough
only in T. Fetal haemoglobin, with $\gamma$ chains that bind it less
well, has a higher affinity than the mother's and draws oxygen across
the placenta.
\end{proposition}

\section{Proteins in motion}

\begin{definition}[Disorder and dynamics]\label{def:b3:structural-biology:disorder}
\index{intrinsically disordered protein}\index{conformational ensemble}\index{biomolecular condensate}
An \emph{intrinsically disordered} region has no stable \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold} on its own
and exists as an ensemble of rapidly interconverting conformations,
often folding only when it meets its partner; about a third of human
proteins carry a disordered region of more than thirty residues, and
they are enriched in signalling and regulation, where a flexible
segment can be phosphorylated at many sites, bind several partners in
turn, and act as a linker. Folded proteins move too: side chains
rotate in picoseconds, loops open in nanoseconds to microseconds,
\omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{domains} hinge in microseconds to milliseconds, and an enzyme's
catalytic cycle is a choreography of such motions rather than a static
lock-and-key. A crystal structure is one snapshot of a
\emph{conformational ensemble}; NMR and molecular simulation see the
rest. Multivalent disordered proteins and RNAs can also demix from the
cytoplasm into liquid droplets --- \emph{biomolecular condensates} such
as nucleoli, stress granules and P bodies --- that concentrate reactions
without a membrane, and whose ageing into solid aggregates is one route
to the \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:amyloid}{amyloid} diseases.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Structure and function]\label{rem:b3:structural-biology:sf}
The lesson of sixty years of structures is double. A \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold} is a
solution to a physical problem --- bind this, catalyse that, transmit a
signal over four nanometres --- and knowing the structure often makes
the mechanism plain, as it did for the haem's iron and for the
oxygen-driven rotation of haemoglobin's dimers. And a \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold} is a
historical object: the same few hundred architectures, tinkered with,
duplicated and fused, serve for the whole diversity of protein
function, so that the structure of a protein from a bacterium will tell
one, often, how the human cousin works.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:1}
Define the four levels of protein structure and give the example of
each in haemoglobin.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:2}
An $\alpha$-helix contains $36$ residues. How long is it, how many
turns does it make, and how many backbone hydrogen bonds hold it
(each residue's carbonyl bonds to the amide four residues on)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:3}
What did Anfinsen's refolding of ribonuclease show, and why was it
important to do the experiment without any cellular component present?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:4}
Rank crystallography, NMR and \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{cryo-electron microscopy} by the size of
protein each handles best, and say which gives information about
motion.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:5}
Redo Levinthal's count for a protein of $150$ residues with $k = 2$,
and for a peptide of $20$ residues with $k = 3$. For which of the two
is a random search conceivable within a second at $10^{12}$ trials per
second?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:6}
Using the MWC equation with $n = 2$, $L = 100$, $c = 0.01$, compute
$Y$ at $\alpha = 1$, $10$ and $100$, and compare with a single site of
dissociation constant $K_{R}$ ($Y = \alpha/(1+\alpha)$). Where does the
\omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:allostery}{cooperativity} show?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:7}
Explain, with the MWC model, why 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate lowers the
oxygen affinity of haemoglobin, which parameter of the model it
changes, and why stored blood, which loses the compound, delivers
oxygen poorly when transfused.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:8}
A crystal diffracts to a maximum angle $\theta = \qty{15}{\degree}$
with X-rays of wavelength \qty{0.1}{nm}. What is the \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{resolution}? What
features of the protein will be visible? Which angle would be needed
for \qty{0.12}{nm}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:9}
A mutation replaces a buried leucine by a lysine. Predict its effect
on stability, using \cref{prop:b3:structural-biology:forces}, and
explain why the same substitution on the surface would be harmless.
Then explain why a protein with $\Delta G_{\text{fold}} =
\qty{-30}{kJ/mol}$ is not ``very stable'' although the number looks
large.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:10}
Derive the fraction of molecules in the $R$ state, $\bar R$, from the
species counted in the proof of \cref{thm:b3:structural-biology:mwc},
and show that for $c \to 0$ the ligand concentration at which half the
molecules are in $R$ satisfies $(1+\alpha)^{n} = L$. Compute this
$\alpha$ for haemoglobin's parameters and compare with $P_{50}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:11}
\omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:amyloid}{Prions} transmit a shape. Explain why the ``protein-only'' hypothesis
was resisted, what experiment would refute it, and what evidence
(resistance of infectivity to nucleases and ultraviolet light,
sensitivity to protein denaturants, synthetic \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:amyloid}{prions}) supports it. Why
does a \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:amyloid}{prion} need a seed?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:structural-biology:12}
A protein family has kept its \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold} while its sequences have diverged
to \qty{15}{\%} identity. Explain why structure is more conserved than
sequence, what kind of residues are the ones conserved, and how this
is exploited both by profile methods (\cref{ch:b3:bioinformatics}) and
by the design of new proteins.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Fold of a Globin}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a globin's helices measured, its
folding timed against Levinthal, its oxygen binding computed from the
two-state model, and its structure solved from a crystal, ending on the
saturation difference between lung and muscle, the Levinthal time and
the resolution of the map}]
\label{pb:b3:structural-biology:1}
Data: a globin chain of $150$ residues, mean residue mass \qty{110}{Da},
\qty{75}{\%} of residues in eight $\alpha$-helices; helix rise
\qty{0.15}{nm} per residue, $3.6$ residues per turn. Partial specific
volume of protein \qty{0.73}{cm^3/g}. MWC parameters for haemoglobin:
$n = 4$, $K_{R} = \qty{1}{mmHg}$, $c = 0.01$, $L = 3\times 10^{5}$.
X-ray wavelength \qty{0.1}{nm}; unit cell a cube of edge \qty{6}{nm};
crystal a cube of edge \qty{100}{\micro m}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Anatomy of a chain.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many residues are in helices? What total length of helix
        is that, and how many turns?
  \item How many backbone hydrogen bonds do the helices contain,
        counting one per residue beyond the first four of each helix?
  \item The chain's mass and its volume from the partial specific
        volume; the radius of a sphere of that volume.
  \item The extended chain would be \qty{0.36}{nm} per residue long.
        Compare its length with the sphere's diameter: by what factor
        does folding compact the chain?
  \item The eight helices average $14$ residues. If the residues were
        laid end to end, what fraction of the extended length is
        helical, and how does the helix shorten it?
  \item A globin buries about $40$ non-polar side chains in its core.
        Taking \qty{4}{kJ/mol} of hydrophobic stabilisation per buried
        side chain and a conformational entropy cost of
        \qty{0.9}{kJ/mol} per residue on folding (all $150$), estimate
        the net $\Delta G$ of folding and compare with the range in
        \cref{prop:b3:structural-biology:forces}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Finding the \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the Levinthal count for $150$ residues with $k = 3$
        and the time to sample it at $10^{12}$ per second.
  \item The globin \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{folds} in \qty{10}{ms}. How many conformations, at
        most, can it have visited? What fraction of the count is that?
  \item Suppose instead each helix forms independently in
        \qty{1}{\micro s}, and the eight formed helices then find their
        packing among $8!$ orderings sampled at $10^{6}$ per second.
        Estimate the folding time. Is this a funnel?
  \item A \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:chaperones}{chaperonin} encloses a chain for \qty{10}{s} per cycle and
        releases it folded with probability $0.3$ per cycle. What is
        the expected number of cycles, and the expected time, to \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:levels}{fold} a
        difficult substrate? What fraction is still unfolded after a
        minute?
  \item A destabilising mutation raises the unfolded fraction at
        \qty{37}{\celsius} from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$. With $\Delta G =
        -RT\ln(K)$ and $K = [\text{folded}]/[\text{unfolded}]$, by how
        much did $\Delta G_{\text{fold}}$ change?
  \item Explain why a hundredfold more unfolded protein can be the
        difference between health and \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:amyloid}{amyloid} disease, using the
        need for a seed.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Binding oxygen.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute $Y$ from the MWC equation at $\alpha = 100$ (lung) and
        $\alpha = 20$ (working muscle).
  \item Compute the fraction of molecules in the $R$ state at those two
        pressures.
  \item How much of the loaded oxygen is unloaded between lung and
        muscle? Compare with a single-site carrier of half-saturation
        pressure \qty{26}{mmHg}.
  \item Blood carries \qty{150}{g} of haemoglobin per litre, each gram
        binding \qty{1.34}{mL} of oxygen. How many millilitres of
        oxygen per litre does haemoglobin deliver to the muscle, from
        question 15? A resting body consumes \qty{250}{mL/min}: what
        blood flow does that require at rest (unloading from $0.97$ to
        $0.78$)?
  \item Bisphosphoglycerate raises $L$ to $3\times 10^{6}$. Recompute
        $Y$ at $\alpha = 40$ and say what this does to delivery at
        rest.
  \item Fetal haemoglobin has, in effect, $L = 3\times 10^{4}$. Compute
        its $Y$ at the placental pressure of \qty{30}{mmHg}, compare
        with the mother's, and explain the transfer.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Seeing it.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many unit cells does the crystal contain? If each cell
        holds four globin chains, how many molecules diffract together?
  \item Spots are seen to $\theta = \qty{14.5}{\degree}$. What is the
        \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{resolution}, and will the side chains be placed?
  \item What angle would give \qty{0.15}{nm}? Why does a poorly
        ordered crystal diffract only to low angles?
  \item In \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{cryo-electron microscopy} the signal of the averaged image
        grows as the square root of the number of particles. If
        \num{10000} particles give \qty{0.6}{nm}, and \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{resolution}
        improves as the inverse of the signal, how many particles
        give \qty{0.3}{nm}?
  \item \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{Cryo-electron microscopy} needs no crystal. Name two kinds of
        protein or complex for which this decides whether a structure
        can be obtained at all, and explain why.
  \item A predicted model of the globin differs from the crystal
        structure by \qty{0.1}{nm} on average over the backbone. Name
        two things the prediction cannot supply that the crystal did.
  \item Summarise: the unloading between lung and muscle (question~15),
        the Levinthal time for $150$ residues (question~7), and the
        \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:methods}{resolution} of the map (question~20).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
