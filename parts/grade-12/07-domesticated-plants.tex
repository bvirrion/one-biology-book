\chapter{The Domesticated Plant}\label{ch:g12:domesticated-plants}

Beside a modern ear of maize --- six hundred kernels in tidy rows,
wrapped in husks, held on a cob that will not let them go --- lay the
ear of teosinte, the wild grass it came from: a dozen kernels in a
single row, each cased in a stony shell, dropping off one by one at
maturity. Nine thousand years of choosing, by farmers who kept the
seeds of the plants they preferred, turned the one into the other. The
result cannot survive a season without us: a maize ear left in a field
rots where it lies. This chapter is about what happens to a plant when
people become its selecting environment --- and about the newer ways
of changing a plant's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} on purpose.

\section{Selection by people}

\begin{definition}[Domestication, artificial selection]\label{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}
\emph{Domestication}\index{domestication} is the transformation of a
wild \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} into one that depends on humans and serves them, by
\emph{artificial selection}\index{artificial selection}: at each
generation, people keep and sow the seeds of the individuals with the
traits they want, so that the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} behind those traits rise in
frequency. It is the selection of \cref{ch:g12:selection-drift-speciation}
with a human eye in place of the environment, and it works by the same
arithmetic --- faster, since the selecting is deliberate and severe.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[What domestication selects]\label{prop:g12:domesticated-plants:traits}
Crops share a set of traits that no wild plant would keep, the
\emph{\omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{domestication} syndrome}: seeds that stay on the plant instead
of scattering (a non-shattering ear); larger seeds and fruits;
seeds that germinate at once instead of waiting years in the soil;
the loss of toxins and bitter defences; a compact plant with few
branches and one large ear rather than many small ones; and
simultaneous ripening. Each is useful to the harvester and harmful to
a plant on its own --- a crop is a plant selected for our convenience
and against its independence.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Wild wheat sheds its grains from a brittle stalk; the first cultivated
wheats, found in the oldest farming sites, have a tough stalk that
holds the grain --- the harvester's sickle selected, unintentionally,
the mutants whose grain stayed on the plant to be gathered and sown.
Teosinte and maize differ at only about five major \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes}: one changes
a many-branched plant into a single stalk, another frees the kernel
from its stony case, others multiply the rows. Crossing the two gives
fertile offspring, and the intermediate forms exist: the same \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species},
reshaped.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[What a crop has lost]\label{ex:g12:domesticated-plants:lost}
Maize cannot scatter its seed; cultivated wheat cannot shed its grain;
the banana and the seedless grape make no seeds at all and are
propagated by cuttings, every plant a clone. A crop's survival is
delegated to the farmer, and its \emph{\omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic diversity}} has shrunk
with every stage of selection: a modern variety is nearly uniform,
and a whole region may grow one. The wild relatives, still diverse,
are where the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} a future crop may need --- for a new disease, a
drier climate --- are kept (\cref{ch:g10:biodiversity-scales}).
\end{example}

\section{Breeding by crossing}

\begin{proposition}[Varieties and hybrids]\label{prop:g12:domesticated-plants:hybrids}
Breeders make new varieties by crossing plants that carry different
useful \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} and selecting, among the offspring, the combinations
they want, over several generations. A special case dominates maize
and many vegetables: the \emph{F1 hybrid}\index{F1 hybrid}. Two
inbred lines --- each made uniform and homozygous by generations of
self-pollination --- are crossed; their offspring are all genetically
identical, heterozygous at every \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} where the lines differ, and
markedly more vigorous and productive than either parent. Sown again,
the hybrid's own seed segregates into a mixture of less vigorous
plants: the farmer buys new hybrid seed every year.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Why the hybrid is vigorous, and why it does not last]\label{ex:g12:domesticated-plants:vigour}
Each inbred line, homozygous everywhere, carries some weak \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive}
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} in double dose; crossing two lines masks each line's weak
\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} behind the other's working ones, at every \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} where they
differ. The F1 is therefore heterozygous and vigorous. At the next
generation, \omterm{def:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling:meiosis}{meiosis} and fertilisation deal the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{alleles} out again
(\cref{ch:g12:meiosis-genetic-shuffling}): a quarter of the plants
are homozygous for the weak \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} at each \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, and the field is a
patchwork.
\end{example}

\section{Changing the genes directly}

\begin{proposition}[Transgenic and edited crops]\label{prop:g12:domesticated-plants:transgenic}
Since the 1980s a \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} can be introduced into a plant without
crossing: \emph{transgenesis}\index{transgenesis} (\cref{ch:g10:universal-dna}).
A soil bacterium that naturally injects a piece of its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA} into plant
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} is used as the vehicle: the desired \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} is inserted into that
piece, plant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} are infected, and whole plants are regrown from the
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} that took the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}. The \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} may come from any \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}. More
recently, a plant's own \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} can be altered at a chosen position ---
\emph{\omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene} editing} --- without adding foreign \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}. Traits so far:
resistance to an insect (a bacterial toxin \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}), tolerance of a
herbicide, resistance to a virus, a vitamin precursor in rice, oils of
altered composition.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[A toxin gene in maize]\label{ex:g12:domesticated-plants:bt}
A soil bacterium makes a \omterm{def:g11:gene-expression:protein}{protein} toxic to caterpillars and harmless to
mammals; its \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, inserted into maize, makes every \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} of the plant
produce the toxin, and the borer caterpillars that tunnel the stalks
die at their first bite. Insecticide spraying falls; yields rise where
the borer was severe. Within a decade, borers resistant to the toxin
appeared where the crop was grown without interruption --- the
selection of \cref{ch:g11:antibiotic-resistance} in a field --- and
farmers are now required to sow refuges of ordinary maize beside the
transgenic one, so that sensitive borers survive to dilute the
resistant ones.
\end{example}

\section{Choices}

\begin{proposition}[What is at stake]\label{prop:g12:domesticated-plants:stakes}
Every technique of this chapter raises the same questions in a new
form: the narrowing of diversity (one variety over a region, one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}
in every plant), the dependence of farmers on seed they cannot save,
the selection of pests and weeds resistant to the crop's defences, the
spread of a crop's \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} to wild relatives by pollen, and the safety
and ownership of what is eaten. None is answered by the biology alone;
the biology says what each technique does and does not do, and what it
selects.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Reading a crop's history]\label{met:g12:domesticated-plants:read}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the wild ancestor and what the crop has lost relative
        to it (dispersal, dormancy, defences, diversity).
  \item Identify how the change was made: unconscious selection by
        harvesting, deliberate selection, crossing and selection,
        hybridisation, \omterm{prop:g10:universal-dna:universal}{transgenesis}, editing.
  \item Count the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} involved where known: a few major \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} for
        the \omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{domestication} syndrome, many small ones for yield, one
        for a transgenic trait.
  \item Ask what the crop now depends on (the farmer, bought seed, a
        herbicide, refuges) and what depends on it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[The oldest experiment in evolution]\label{rem:g12:domesticated-plants:oldest}
Darwin opened his book on the origin of \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} with a chapter on
pigeons and cabbages, because breeders had already shown what
selection could do in a few centuries; he only had to remove the
breeder and lengthen the time. Every crop is a demonstration of the
last three chapters --- variation, selection, and a change of
frequencies that alters a \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species} --- run at human speed, with a
record of the results in every field and every market.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Nine Thousand Years, Five Genes}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- maize made from teosinte: the
ears compared, the selection reckoned, a hybrid's yield followed
through the F2, and a toxin gene managed against resistance}]
\label{pb:g12:domesticated-plants:1}
A teosinte spike carries 12 kernels of \qty{0.08}{g}, cased in a hard
shell, on a brittle axis; a maize ear carries 600 kernels of
\qty{0.3}{g}, naked, on a rigid cob. Maize was domesticated about 9000
years ago; one generation is one year.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The ears.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the mass of grain per ear in teosinte and in maize,
        and the factor between them.
  \item List the traits of the maize ear that belong to the
        \omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{domestication} syndrome, and for each say why it would be a
        handicap for a wild plant.
  \item The cased kernel is controlled by one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}, with the naked
        \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} \omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive}. Explain why the first farmers could only
        have found naked-kernel plants as rare individuals in their
        fields.
  \item Suppose the naked \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} had frequency 0.01 in the wild
        \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population}. What fraction of plants showed naked kernels?
  \item A farmer sows only seed from naked-kernel plants. What is the
        frequency of the naked \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} in her next crop?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Nine thousand generations.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The ear's kernel number rose from 12 to 600 over 9000
        generations. Compute the average factor of increase per
        generation. Why is a change so small per generation enough?
  \item The number of kernel rows is controlled by many \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} of
        small effect. Explain why such a trait responds to selection
        gradually and for a long time, while the cased-kernel trait
        changed in one step.
  \item Maize still crosses with teosinte and gives fertile
        offspring. Are they one \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:species}{species}? What has \omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{domestication}
        changed and not changed?
  \item A modern variety is genetically uniform. State the risk that
        follows, and the resource that answers it.
  \item Maize cannot disperse its seeds. What does this imply about
        the relation between the plant and the farmer, in terms of
        selection?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The hybrid.}
Inbred line A yields \qty{5}{t/ha}, line B \qty{5.5}{t/ha}, their \omterm{prop:g12:domesticated-plants:hybrids}{F1
hybrid} \qty{10}{t/ha}; the F2 grown from the hybrid's seed yields
\qty{8.2}{t/ha}. Hybrid seed costs the equivalent of \qty{0.4}{t/ha}
of maize.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item By what percentage does the F1 exceed the better parent? By
        what percentage does the F2 fall below the F1?
  \item Explain the F1's vigour and the F2's loss with the
        \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotypes} of \cref{ex:g12:domesticated-plants:vigour}.
  \item A farmer hesitates between buying hybrid seed each year and
        saving seed from the F1. Compare the net harvests, and say why
        the seed company's business exists.
  \item Why can the inbred lines not simply be sold to farmers to make
        the hybrid themselves?
  \item What has the hybrid system done to the \omterm{def:g10:biodiversity-scales:biodiversity}{genetic diversity} of
        the fields, and to the farmer's independence?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The toxin \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}.}
A borer \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} has a resistance \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} at frequency 0.001,
\omterm{def:g11:genes-and-disease:genetic}{recessive}; only $rr$ borers survive on the toxin maize.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item What fraction of borers survives on a field of toxin maize in
        the first year? Compute the frequency of $r$ among the
        survivors.
  \item Explain why, without refuges, resistance spreads within a few
        years, using the \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotic} case as a model.
  \item A refuge of ordinary maize keeps a large \omterm{def:g12:selection-drift-speciation:population}{population} of
        sensitive borers alive next to the toxin field. Explain how
        this keeps the resistance \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} rare, using the \omterm{def:g11:enzymes-and-phenotype:phenotype}{genotype} of
        the offspring of a resistant survivor and a refuge borer.
  \item Compare the refuge with the "full course" of \omterm{def:g11:antibiotic-resistance:antibiotic}{antibiotics}: what
        is the same, what is different?
  \item State the result: the factor by which \omterm{def:g12:domesticated-plants:domestication}{domestication} multiplied
        the grain of an ear, the number of major \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} it took, and
        the one dependence it created.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
