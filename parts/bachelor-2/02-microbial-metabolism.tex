\chapter{Microbial Metabolism and Biofilms}\label{ch:b2:microbial-metabolism}

Fill a glass cylinder with pond mud, a little shredded paper, an egg
yolk and water, and leave it on a windowsill. Within weeks it
stratifies into coloured bands: green algae at the top, a rusty
layer, a purple band, a green one lower down, and black sulfide mud
at the bottom. Nothing was added but light. Each band is a population
that lives on a different chemistry --- on oxygen, on nitrate, on
sulfate, on sulfide, on hydrogen --- and each feeds the next. The
column is the microbial world in miniature: cells that earn their
energy from almost any pair of substances that can exchange
electrons, and that, together, run the chemical cycles of the
planet. This chapter sets out the logic of that energetics, the
kinetics of microbial growth, and the collective life of microbes on
surfaces, which is how most of them actually live.

\section{Ways of making a living}

\begin{definition}[Trophic types]\label{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:trophic}
\index{chemotroph}\index{phototroph}\index{lithotroph}\index{organotroph}
An organism is classified by three answers. Its \emph{energy source}:
light (\emph{phototroph}) or chemical reactions (\emph{chemotroph}).
Its \emph{electron source}: inorganic substances --- $\mathrm{H_2}$,
$\mathrm{H_2S}$, $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$, $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$, water ---
(\emph{lithotroph}) or organic molecules (\emph{organotroph}). Its
\emph{carbon source}: $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (\emph{autotroph}) or organic
carbon (\emph{heterotroph}). Plants and cyanobacteria are
photolithoautotrophs; animals, fungi and \emph{E.~coli} are
chemoorganoheterotrophs; the nitrifying and sulfur-oxidising
bacteria are chemolithoautotrophs, which make their organic matter
from $\mathrm{CO_2}$ with energy drawn from the oxidation of
minerals, in the dark. Almost every combination exists somewhere
among \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{prokaryotes}; eukaryotes occupy only two.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The energy of a redox pair]\label{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:redox}
\index{redox potential}\index{electron tower}
Every energy-yielding metabolism moves electrons from a
\emph{donor} couple to an \emph{acceptor} couple. Each couple has a
standard potential $E^{\circ\prime}$ (at pH 7): the more negative,
the more readily it gives electrons. Transferring $n$ moles of
electrons from a donor at $E_d$ to an acceptor at $E_a$ releases a
standard free energy
\[
\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = -\,n F\,(E_a - E_d), \qquad F = \qty{96.5}{kJ/V/mol},
\]
so that the yield is proportional to the vertical distance between
the two couples on the \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:redox}{electron tower}}. With
$\mathrm{O_2/H_2O}$ at \qty{+0.82}{V} and $\mathrm{CO_2}$/glucose at
\qty{-0.43}{V}, the 24 electrons of a glucose molecule yield
$24\times 96.5\times 1.25 = \qty{2900}{kJ/mol}$; handed instead to
sulfate ($\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}/H_2S}$, \qty{-0.22}{V}), the same
electrons yield only $24\times 96.5\times 0.21 = \qty{490}{kJ/mol}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For the reaction donor$_{\text{red}}$ + acceptor$_{\text{ox}}$
$\to$ donor$_{\text{ox}}$ + acceptor$_{\text{red}}$, the free-energy
change is the work done by $n$ moles of electron charge $nF$
falling through the potential difference $E_a - E_d$, with the sign
convention that a spontaneous transfer (electrons toward the more
positive couple) has $\Delta G < 0$. This is the Nernst relation of
the Year 1 volume applied to two half-reactions.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Chemolithotrophy]\label{prop:b2:microbial-metabolism:lithotrophy}
\index{chemolithotrophy}\index{nitrification}
Some bacteria draw all their energy from inorganic oxidations, using
oxygen (or nitrate) as acceptor, and fix $\mathrm{CO_2}$ with the
ATP and reducing power so gained. \emph{Nitrifiers}: ammonium
oxidisers (\emph{Nitrosomonas}) turn $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ into nitrite
($\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = \qty{-275}{kJ/mol}$), nitrite oxidisers
(\emph{Nitrobacter}) turn nitrite into nitrate (\qty{-74}{kJ/mol});
together they convert the ammonium of decay into the nitrate that
plants take up (\cref{ch:b2:biogeochemical-cycles}).
\emph{Sulfur oxidisers} (\emph{Thiobacillus}, \emph{Beggiatoa})
oxidise $\mathrm{H_2S}$ to sulfur and sulfate; \emph{iron oxidisers}
oxidise $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ to rust; \emph{hydrogen oxidisers} burn
$\mathrm{H_2}$. Because their electron donors sit high on the tower,
the yield per molecule is small, and because their donors are poorer
reductants than NADH, they must spend energy driving electrons
\emph{uphill} to make the NADH that carbon fixation needs: a
nitrifier oxidises some thirty ammonium ions to fix one
$\mathrm{CO_2}$, and grows slowly.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Winogradsky (1887--1890) grew filamentous sulfur bacteria in a drop
of water into which he let sulfide diffuse from one side and air from
the other: they gathered at the boundary, accumulated sulfur
globules, and consumed them when the sulfide was cut off. He then
isolated nitrifying bacteria on a purely mineral medium containing
ammonium and no organic carbon at all, on which they grew and
produced nitrate --- the first demonstration that life can be built
from $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and the energy of an inorganic reaction,
without light. He called it chemosynthesis.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Anoxygenic photosynthesis]\label{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anoxygenic}
\index{anoxygenic photosynthesis}
The purple and green bacteria photosynthesise with a single
photosystem and a bacteriochlorophyll that absorbs in the near
infrared, using $\mathrm{H_2S}$, $\mathrm{H_2}$ or organic acids as
electron donors instead of water: they release sulfur or sulfate,
never oxygen, and they need light of wavelengths that pass through
the green and cyanobacterial layer above them. Purple sulfur
bacteria therefore grow in the purple band of the Winogradsky
column, just where sulfide from below meets the light from above;
green sulfur bacteria, which tolerate more sulfide and less light,
grow beneath them. Oxygenic photosynthesis, with its two photosystems
in series, is a later invention of one lineage --- the cyanobacteria
--- that learned to take electrons from water.
\end{definition}

\section{Anaerobic respirations and fermentations}

\begin{definition}[Anaerobic respiration]\label{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}
\index{anaerobic respiration}\index{denitrification}\index{sulfate reduction}\index{methanogenesis}
\emph{Anaerobic respiration} is respiration --- an electron-transport
chain that pumps protons and makes ATP by chemiosmosis --- with a
terminal acceptor other than oxygen. \emph{Denitrifiers}
(\emph{Pseudomonas}, \emph{Paracoccus}) reduce nitrate to nitrite,
nitric oxide, nitrous oxide and finally $\mathrm{N_2}$, which escapes
to the air; they use their aerobic chain with a few added enzymes
and switch to nitrate only when oxygen is gone. \emph{Sulfate
reducers} (\emph{Desulfovibrio}) reduce sulfate to sulfide, blacken
the mud with iron sulfide and give it its smell. \emph{Methanogens},
which are archaea, reduce $\mathrm{CO_2}$ with $\mathrm{H_2}$ to
methane ($4\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{CO_2} \to \mathrm{CH_4} +
2\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = \qty{-131}{kJ/mol}$), or
split acetate into methane and $\mathrm{CO_2}$; they are killed by
oxygen and live in cow rumens, rice paddies, marshes and landfills,
from which they release a greenhouse gas that is the subject of
\cref{ch:b2:global-change}. Iron and manganese oxides serve other
groups. The Year 1 volume treated oxygen as \emph{the} acceptor; for
most of Earth's history, and in every sediment today, it is only the
first of a series.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The sequence of acceptors in a sediment]\label{prop:b2:microbial-metabolism:sequence}
Going down through a water-logged sediment, the electron acceptors
are used up in the order of the energy they yield: oxygen in the
first millimetres, then nitrate, then manganese and iron oxides,
then sulfate, and finally $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (\omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{methanogenesis}) in the
deepest anoxic layer. The order is the order of the tower: at each
depth the organisms using the best remaining acceptor grow fastest,
out-compete the rest for the organic donors, and exhaust their
acceptor before the next group takes over. In the sea, where sulfate
is abundant (\qty{28}{mmol/L}), the sulfate zone is thick and
\omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{methanogenesis} begins only metres down; in a freshwater marsh,
sulfate-poor, methane forms just below the surface.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[Fermentation, revisited]\label{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:fermentation}
\index{fermentation!microbial}
A \emph{fermentation} uses no external acceptor: the organic
substrate is split into a more oxidised and a more reduced product,
ATP is made only by substrate-level phosphorylation, and the NADH of
glycolysis is re-oxidised by reducing a metabolite. Yeasts make
ethanol and $\mathrm{CO_2}$; lactic bacteria make lactate (yoghurt,
sauerkraut, sore muscles); \emph{E.~coli} makes a mixture of acids,
ethanol and gases; clostridia make butyrate, butanol and acetone;
propionibacteria make the propionate and $\mathrm{CO_2}$ of Swiss
cheese. The yield is two ATP per glucose against about thirty by
respiration, so a fermenter must process fifteen times more sugar
for the same growth, and its products --- acids, alcohol --- poison
its own medium: fermentations preserve food because the fermenter
makes the food uninhabitable, including for itself.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Syntrophy: living on the rest]\label{ex:b2:microbial-metabolism:syntrophy}
In an anoxic sediment the fermenters leave behind acetate, propionate,
butyrate and $\mathrm{H_2}$. Oxidising propionate to acetate and
$\mathrm{H_2}$ has a \emph{positive} standard free energy
(\qty{+76}{kJ/mol}) and is impossible --- unless a methanogen next
door consumes the hydrogen as fast as it forms, keeping its partial
pressure below \qty{1e-4}{atm}, at which the reaction becomes
exergonic. Neither partner can grow alone on propionate; together
they turn it into methane. This \emph{interspecies hydrogen
transfer} is why anaerobic digestion is always the work of a
consortium, and why the archaeon and its bacterial partner are often
found pressed against each other.
\end{example}

\section{Growth on a substrate}

\begin{theorem}[Monod's law]\label{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:monod}
\index{Monod equation}\index{yield coefficient}
A population growing on a single limiting substrate at concentration
$S$ has a specific growth rate
\[
\mu = \mu_{\max}\,\frac{S}{K_s + S}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mu X,
\]
where $X$ is the biomass concentration, $\mu_{\max}$ the maximal
rate (the reciprocal of the shortest \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:division}{generation time} divided by
$\ln 2$) and $K_s$ the \emph{half-saturation constant}, the
substrate concentration at which growth runs at half speed --- a few
milligrams per litre of glucose for \emph{E.~coli}. Substrate is
consumed in proportion to growth, $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t =
-\mu X/Y$, with $Y$ the \emph{yield}: grams of biomass per gram of
substrate, about \num{0.5} for aerobic growth on sugar and \num{0.1}
for a fermenter.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Monod (1942) grew \emph{E.~coli} in batches on a series of glucose
concentrations and measured the exponential rate of each: the rates
rose with concentration and saturated, as a Michaelis--Menten curve,
which is what one expects if the rate is set by a saturable uptake
system. The law is empirical --- a cell has thousands of enzymes,
not one --- but it fits nearly every substrate-limited culture, and
it is the foundation of \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:fermentation}{fermentation} technology and of wastewater
engineering.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The chemostat]\label{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:chemostat}
\index{chemostat}\index{dilution rate}
A \emph{chemostat} is a vessel of volume $V$ fed with fresh medium
(substrate $S_{\text{in}}$) at a flow $F$ and drained at the same
flow, so that the \emph{\omterm{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:chemostat}{dilution rate}} is $D = F/V$. Biomass and
substrate obey
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} = (\mu - D)\,X, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t} = D\,(S_{\text{in}} - S) - \frac{\mu X}{Y}.
\]
At steady state $\mu = D$: the culture grows exactly as fast as it is
washed out, whatever $D$ below a critical value, and the operator
sets the growth rate by turning the pump. The steady-state
substrate and biomass are
\[
S^* = \frac{K_s\,D}{\mu_{\max} - D}, \qquad X^* = Y\,(S_{\text{in}} - S^*),
\]
and above the \emph{washout} rate $D_c = \mu_{\max}
S_{\text{in}}/(K_s + S_{\text{in}})$ no population can hold on.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Setting $\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ with $X \neq 0$ gives $\mu = D$;
inverting Monod's law, $D(K_s + S^*) = \mu_{\max} S^*$, hence $S^* =
K_s D/(\mu_{\max} - D)$. Setting $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ and
replacing $\mu$ by $D$: $D(S_{\text{in}} - S^*) = DX^*/Y$, hence
$X^*$. The solution exists only while $S^* < S_{\text{in}}$, i.e.\
$D < \mu_{\max} S_{\text{in}}/(K_s + S_{\text{in}})$; beyond that,
$\mu < D$ for every $S \le S_{\text{in}}$ and $X$ decays to zero. The
steady state is stable: if $X$ rises, $S$ falls, $\mu$ drops below
$D$ and $X$ is washed back down.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Reading a chemostat]\label{ex:b2:microbial-metabolism:chemostat}
With $\mu_{\max} = \qty{1.0}{h^{-1}}$, $K_s = \qty{0.02}{g/L}$,
$S_{\text{in}} = \qty{2}{g/L}$ and $Y = 0.5$, at $D =
\qty{0.5}{h^{-1}}$: $S^* = 0.02\times 0.5/0.5 = \qty{0.02}{g/L}$ and
$X^* = 0.5\times 1.98 = \qty{0.99}{g/L}$; the culture leaves
\qty{1}{\%} of the sugar unused. The washout rate is $1.0\times
2/2.02 = \qty{0.99}{h^{-1}}$. A digester or a gut works on the same
principle: the human colon, fed three times a day and emptied once,
holds its bacteria at a \omterm{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:chemostat}{dilution rate} near \qty{0.04}{h^{-1}}, and any
species that cannot double within a day is washed out --- unless it
holds on to the wall.
\end{example}

\section{Biofilms}

\begin{definition}[Biofilm]\label{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:biofilm}
\index{biofilm}\index{extracellular matrix!bacterial}
A \emph{biofilm} is a community of microorganisms attached to a
surface and embedded in a self-produced \emph{matrix} of
polysaccharides, proteins and extracellular DNA. It forms in stages:
reversible \emph{attachment} of single cells (flagella, pili),
irreversible attachment and \emph{microcolony} growth, \emph{maturation}
into a structured film with towers and water channels, and
\emph{dispersal} of cells that swim off to seed new surfaces. The
matrix holds water and nutrients, protects the cells from
desiccation, grazers, antibiotics and immune cells, and sets up
chemical gradients that make the film a landscape of niches. Most
bacteria on Earth live this way: on rocks in streams, on roots, on
teeth (dental plaque), on catheters and implants, in the lungs of
cystic fibrosis patients, on ship hulls and in pipes.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Gradients inside a biofilm]\label{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:gradient}
\index{oxygen penetration depth}
In a film of thickness $L$ whose cells consume oxygen at a constant
volumetric rate $q$, with the concentration held at $c_0$ at the
surface and no flux through the substratum, the steady-state
oxygen profile is a parabola, and if the film is thick enough for
oxygen to run out, it does so at the \emph{penetration depth}
\[
L_p = \sqrt{\frac{2 D_e\, c_0}{q}},
\]
where $D_e$ is the effective diffusion coefficient in the matrix.
With $D_e = \qty{1.5e-9}{m^2/s}$, $c_0 = \qty{0.25}{mol/m^3}$ and $q
= \qty{0.17}{mol/m^3/s}$ (a dense film of respiring cells), $L_p
\approx \qty{66}{\micro m}$: below a tenth of a millimetre a \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:biofilm}{biofilm}
is anoxic, and denitrifiers, fermenters and sulfate reducers live
under a roof of aerobes.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $x$ be the depth below the surface. At steady state the
diffusive flux $-D_e\,\mathrm{d}c/\mathrm{d}x$ changes with depth
only by the consumption: $D_e\,\mathrm{d}^2c/\mathrm{d}x^2 = q$.
Integrating twice, $c = c_0 + a x + q x^2/2D_e$. If oxygen vanishes
at depth $L_p$ with zero flux there (nothing arrives from below),
$c(L_p) = 0$ and $c'(L_p) = 0$: the second gives $a = -qL_p/D_e$,
and the first then gives $c_0 - qL_p^2/D_e + qL_p^2/2D_e = 0$, i.e.\
$L_p^2 = 2D_e c_0/q$. The profile is $c = (q/2D_e)(L_p - x)^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Quorum sensing]\label{prop:b2:microbial-metabolism:quorum}
\index{quorum sensing}\index{autoinducer}
Bacteria count themselves. Each cell secretes a small signal
molecule, an \emph{autoinducer} (acyl-homoserine lactones in
Gram-negatives, short peptides in Gram-positives), at a low constant
rate. In a dilute suspension the signal diffuses away; in a dense
population or a \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:biofilm}{biofilm} it accumulates, and above a threshold
concentration it binds a receptor that switches on a set of genes
--- including the gene for the autoinducer itself, hence the name.
The genes so controlled are those that pay only when many cells act
together: luminescence, the secretion of digestive enzymes and
toxins, matrix production, the virulence of pathogens that must
overwhelm a host at once rather than alert it one cell at a time.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Nealson, Platt and Hastings (1970) found that the marine bacterium
\emph{Vibrio fischeri}, which lights the organ of a squid, is dark in
dilute culture and glows only above about $10^{7}$ cells per
millilitre; cell-free medium taken from a dense, glowing culture
made a dilute one glow at once. The medium contained the signal; its
structure, an acyl-homoserine lactone, was determined in 1981, and
mutants unable to make it never glow however dense they become.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Why a biofilm survives antibiotics]\label{ex:b2:microbial-metabolism:tolerance}
Cells in a \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:biofilm}{biofilm} survive concentrations of antibiotic a hundred to
a thousand times those that kill the same strain in suspension,
without any resistance gene. The matrix slows the drug's diffusion
and binds some of it; the gradients leave the deep cells starved,
anoxic and barely growing, and most antibiotics kill only growing
cells (penicillins need wall synthesis, aminoglycosides need active
transport); a few \emph{persister} cells are dormant altogether and
regrow the film when the treatment stops. A chronic infection on an
implant is therefore usually treated by removing the implant.
\end{example}

\section{Microbial communities}

\begin{example}[The Winogradsky column, explained]\label{ex:b2:microbial-metabolism:column}
Light and air enter from above; organic matter and sulfate from the
mud. Cyanobacteria and algae grow at the top and release oxygen.
Below, heterotrophs use up the oxygen on the organic matter, then
the nitrate; deeper, sulfate reducers turn sulfate into sulfide,
which diffuses upward and blackens the mud with iron sulfide. Where
the rising sulfide meets the descending light, purple sulfur bacteria
oxidise it photosynthetically (the purple band), green sulfur
bacteria below them; where sulfide meets oxygen, in the dark,
colourless sulfur oxidisers such as \emph{Beggiatoa} earn a living
chemolithotrophically (the white veil); where reduced iron meets
oxygen, iron oxidisers leave a rusty band. Sulfur cycles between
sulfide and sulfate within the column; carbon cycles between
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ and organic matter; only light enters and only heat
leaves. It is a closed ecosystem with an open energy budget, and a
scale model of the ocean floor.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Microbial mats and the early Earth]\label{rem:b2:microbial-metabolism:mats}
Around hot springs and on tidal flats, layered microbial mats a few
millimetres thick pack the whole column into a thickness that light
can cross: cyanobacteria on top, purple bacteria beneath, sulfate
reducers at the base, each layer consuming what the one above
produces, and the mat migrating up through the sediment it traps.
Fossil mats --- stromatolites --- are the oldest evidence of life,
\num{3.5} billion years old. For two billion years, before plants or
animals, the biosphere was these mats and the plankton above them,
and it was they that filled the air with oxygen
(\cref{ch:b2:biogeochemical-cycles}).
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: A Wastewater Treatment Plant}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a town's sewage works analysed as
a set of microbial reactors, from the energetics of its bacteria to
the methane that powers it, ending on the plant's energy balance}]
\label{pb:b2:microbial-metabolism:1}
A plant treats $Q = \qty{10000}{m^3}$ of sewage a day carrying
\qty{300}{g/m^3} of organic matter, measured as the oxygen needed to
burn it (its \emph{chemical oxygen demand}, COD), and
\qty{40}{g/m^3} of ammonium nitrogen. It has an aerated tank of
activated sludge, an anoxic tank for \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{denitrification}, and an
anaerobic digester for the sludge. Data: aerobic heterotrophs
convert organic matter with a yield $Y = 0.4$ (kilogram of biomass
COD per kilogram of substrate COD, so the rest is oxidised);
nitrification needs \qty{4.57}{kg} of $\mathrm{O_2}$ per kilogram of
nitrogen; \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{denitrification} needs \qty{2.86}{kg} of substrate COD per
kilogram of nitrate nitrogen; aeration delivers \qty{2}{kg} of
$\mathrm{O_2}$ per kilowatt-hour; the sludge's COD is converted to
methane in the digester at \qty{60}{\%}, each kilogram of COD
converted giving \qty{0.35}{m^3} of methane of energy content
\qty{36}{MJ/m^3}, burnt in a generator of \qty{35}{\%} electrical
efficiency. Nitrifiers: $\mu_{\max} = \qty{0.5}{d^{-1}}$, $K_s =
\qty{1}{g/m^3}$ of ammonium nitrogen.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The energetics.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Using the \omterm{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:redox}{electron tower}, compute $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$
        for the oxidation of one mole of glucose (24 electrons at
        \qty{-0.43}{V}) by oxygen (\qty{+0.82}{V}).
  \item Compute it for nitrate reduced to $\mathrm{N_2}$
        (\qty{+0.74}{V}) and for sulfate reduced to sulfide
        (\qty{-0.22}{V}).
  \item Why does \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{denitrification} only start in the anoxic tank, and
        why is \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{sulfate reduction} (with its smell) a sign of a badly
        run plant?
  \item If cells capture \qty{40}{\%} of the free energy as ATP
        (\qty{50}{kJ/mol}), how many ATP per glucose does each of the
        three metabolisms give?
  \item How much more glucose must a sulfate reducer process than an
        aerobe for the same growth?
  \item Fermenters in the digester get 2 ATP per glucose. Explain why
        the digester nevertheless produces almost no biomass and
        mostly gas.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The aerated tank.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the daily load of organic matter, in kilograms of
        COD.
  \item Compute the biomass produced per day (as COD) and the
        oxygen needed to oxidise the rest.
  \item Compute the aeration energy for the organic matter, in
        kilowatt-hours per day.
  \item Compute the daily nitrogen load and the oxygen needed to
        nitrify it.
  \item The sludge is retained in the tank for a mean time $\theta$
        (the reciprocal of its \omterm{thm:b2:microbial-metabolism:chemostat}{dilution rate}). Below what $\theta$
        are the nitrifiers washed out at any ammonium concentration?
  \item With $\theta = \qty{10}{d}$, compute the residual ammonium
        concentration in the effluent.
  \item Winter lowers $\mu_{\max}$ to \qty{0.25}{d^{-1}}. Recompute
        the residual ammonium and say what the operator must do.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{Denitrification} and the digester.}
The nitrate made in the aerated tank is recycled to the anoxic tank,
where heterotrophs reduce it with the incoming organic matter.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the organic matter (kilograms of COD per day)
        consumed by denitrifying all the nitrogen.
  \item Compute the mass of nitrogen released to the air as
        $\mathrm{N_2}$ per day, and the mass leaving in the effluent
        as ammonium (question 12), as a fraction of the load.
  \item That organic matter no longer needs aeration. Compute the
        oxygen saved, and the total oxygen demand of the plant
        (remaining organic matter plus nitrification).
  \item The sludge produced (question 8, kept as COD) goes to the
        digester. Compute the methane produced per day.
  \item Compute the energy of that methane and the electricity it
        yields.
  \item Compute the aeration electricity for the total oxygen demand
        of question 16, and compare.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:biofilm}{biofilm} reactor.}
A trickling filter grows a \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:biofilm}{biofilm} on stones; the bulk water holds
\qty{0.2}{mol/m^3} of oxygen, $D_e = \qty{1.5e-9}{m^2/s}$, and the
film respires at $q = \qty{0.3}{mol/m^3/s}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Derive the oxygen profile $c(x)$ in a film thick enough to
        become anoxic.
  \item Compute the penetration depth.
  \item The film is \qty{300}{\micro m} thick. What fraction of it is
        aerobic, and what do the cells below do with the nitrate
        diffusing down from the aerobic layer?
  \item Explain why a single \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:biofilm}{biofilm} can nitrify and denitrify at
        once, which the aerated tank cannot.
  \item Chlorine is added to disinfect the effluent. Give two reasons
        why the \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:biofilm}{biofilm} survives a dose that kills the same bacteria
        in suspension.
  \item State the result: the plant's daily oxygen demand, its methane
        output, and the fraction of its aeration electricity that the
        methane supplies.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
