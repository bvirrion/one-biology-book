\chapter{Asexual Reproduction and Cloning in Plants}\label{ch:b2:asexual-reproduction}

In a valley in Utah stands a forest of forty-seven thousand aspen
trunks that is one plant: every trunk has grown from the same root
system, every cell carries the same genome, and the individual is
some fourteen thousand years old and weighs six thousand tonnes.
Every strawberry in a supermarket punnet of one variety is a piece
of one plant multiplied by runners; every Cavendish banana on Earth
is a cutting of a cutting of one plant. Plants, unlike most animals,
can make new individuals without sex --- from a stem, a root, a leaf,
a single cell in a flask. This chapter examines how they do it, how
growers exploit it, and what it costs a lineage to give up \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis}
and fertilisation: the question of why sex exists at all.

\section{Vegetative multiplication}

\begin{definition}[Clone, genet, ramet]\label{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}
\index{clone}\index{genet}\index{ramet}\index{vegetative multiplication}
\emph{Asexual reproduction} produces new individuals from one parent
by mitosis alone, without \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} or fertilisation: the offspring
are a \emph{clone}, genetically identical to the parent and to each
other, apart from the somatic \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations} that accumulate. In plants
the usual form is \emph{vegetative multiplication}, in which part of
the body --- stem, root, leaf --- roots and becomes a separate plant.
A \emph{genet} is the genetic individual, everything that grew from
one zygote; a \emph{ramet} is one physiologically separate unit of
it. A strawberry bed may hold a thousand ramets of one genet; the
aspen forest is one genet. Vegetative multiplication is possible
because plant cells keep the capacity to divide and form any organ
--- they are \emph{totipotent} --- and because plants grow from
meristems that can be reawakened almost anywhere.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The organs of vegetative multiplication]\label{prop:b2:asexual-reproduction:organs}
\index{runner}\index{rhizome}\index{tuber}\index{bulb}
Plants have evolved the same trick from every organ. From stems:
\emph{runners} or \emph{stolons}, horizontal shoots along the ground
that root at their nodes (strawberry, creeping buttercup);
\emph{rhizomes}, horizontal underground stems that branch and
fragment (couch grass, iris, ginger, bracken); \emph{tubers}, swollen
rhizome tips that store starch and carry buds, the ``eyes''
(potato); \emph{corms}, swollen vertical stem bases (crocus);
\emph{bulbils}, small bulbs formed in the axils of leaves or in the
\omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:flower}{flower} head (garlic, some onions). From leaves: \emph{bulbs}, buds
wrapped in fleshy storage leaves that split into daughter bulbs
(onion, tulip); plantlets along the leaf margin, complete with roots,
that drop off (\emph{Kalanchoe}). From roots: \emph{suckers}, shoots
that rise from horizontal roots (aspen, blackthorn, raspberry). By
\emph{fragmentation}: a piece of willow branch that floats away and
roots, a duckweed frond that splits. In every case a storage organ
or a meristem is carried a short distance, and the daughter starts
life with a supply of food no \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} can match.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[The geometry of a spreading clone]\label{thm:b2:asexual-reproduction:spread}
\index{clonal growth}
A \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{genet} whose every \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{ramet} produces $m$ rooted daughters a year, all
surviving, grows in number as $N(t) = N_0 (1 + m)^t$ --- exponential
growth without any sex --- until the \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{ramets} fill the space at their
carrying density $d$ (\omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{ramets} per unit area), after which the \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone}
advances as a front. A \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} that spreads its runners in a straight
line at speed $v$ has, after $t$ years, a radius $vt$, and the number
of \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{ramets} grows as $\pi d (vt)^2$: quadratically, not exponentially.
A strawberry plant that sends runners \qty{0.5}{m} a year has a
radius of \qty{5}{m} after ten years; the aspen \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} of the opening
paragraph, \qty{43}{ha}, has a radius of some \qty{370}{m} and, at a net advance of a few centimetres a year, an
age of order $10^{4}$ years --- consistent with the age estimated
from the retreat of the glaciers.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each year every \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{ramet} is replaced by itself plus $m$ daughters, a
factor $1 + m$; $t$ years give $(1 + m)^t$. Once the interior is
full, only the \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{ramets} on the edge find space, and the edge advances
at the runner speed $v$; the area is $\pi(vt)^2$ and the number of
\omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{ramets} is the area times the density.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The oldest organisms]\label{ex:b2:asexual-reproduction:oldest}
The aspen \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} Pando (Utah) covers \qty{43}{ha} with about
\num{47000} trunks that each live a century or so and are replaced
by suckers from the same roots; the \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{genet} may be over ten thousand
years old. A \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} of the seagrass \emph{Posidonia} in the
Mediterranean stretches \qty{15}{km} and may be a hundred thousand
years old; a creosote bush ring in the Mojave has been dated to
\num{11700} years, the central stems dying as the ring expands. In
every case what is old is the \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{genet}; no cell in it is older than a
few decades, and the genome has been copied thousands of times.
\end{example}

\section{Seeds without sex, and animals without fathers}

\begin{definition}[Apomixis]\label{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:apomixis}
\index{apomixis}\index{parthenogenesis}
\emph{Apomixis} is the production of \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seeds} without fertilisation: an
embryo develops from an unreduced egg cell (the \omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{megaspore} mother cell
skips \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis}) or directly from a cell of the nucellus, and the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed}
carries the mother's \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype}. Dandelions, hawkweeds, blackberries,
Kentucky bluegrass and many citrus do this; the citrus \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} often
holds several embryos, one sexual and the rest nucellar copies of the
mother. Apomixis keeps the \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} --- its dispersal, \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{dormancy} and coat
--- and drops the \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis}, so that a well-adapted \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype} is
broadcast unchanged; most apomicts are \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:polyploidy}{polyploids} of hybrid origin
whose \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} would fail anyway. The animal counterpart is
\emph{parthenogenesis}, development from an unfertilised egg:
obligate in some lizards and in bdelloid rotifers, which have had no
males for tens of millions of years; cyclical in aphids and water
fleas, which multiply parthenogenetically all summer, a female giving
birth to live daughters that already carry their own embryos, and
switch to a sexual generation in autumn that lays overwintering
eggs.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[An aphid summer]\label{ex:b2:asexual-reproduction:aphids}
A parthenogenetic aphid matures in about eight days and produces
some five daughters a day for two weeks; the daughters are born
already pregnant (the embryos begin developing inside the mother's
embryos). With a \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:division}{generation time} near ten days and a net rate of
increase of about \num{50} per generation, a single founder in April
could, unchecked, leave $50^{12} \approx 2\times 10^{20}$ descendants
by October --- a mass exceeding the Earth's. Ladybirds, lacewings,
parasitoid wasps, fungi and rain keep the number to a few thousand
per plant, and the autumn sexual generation resets the \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} into
recombined eggs before winter.
\end{example}

\section{Cloning by the grower}

\begin{proposition}[Cuttings, layering, grafting]\label{prop:b2:asexual-reproduction:horticulture}
\index{cutting}\index{grafting}\index{rootstock}
A \emph{cutting} is a piece of stem, root or leaf induced to root:
the cut end forms a callus of dividing cells from which root
meristems arise, helped by auxin (the rooting hormone) and humidity.
\emph{Layering} roots a branch while it is still attached and cuts
it afterward. \emph{Grafting} joins a shoot of one plant (the
\emph{scion}) to the rooted stem of another (the \emph{rootstock}):
the two cambia are pressed together, callus bridges the wound, and
new xylem and phloem connect across it within weeks. The graft is a
chimera --- the roots keep the rootstock's genome, the fruiting shoot
the scion's --- and it works only between related plants (species of
one genus, sometimes of one family), because the tissues must
recognise each other's cells. Every apple, pear, cherry, citrus and
grape variety is propagated by grafting: a named variety is one
\omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{genet}, kept alive for centuries by scions (the Reinette apples date
from the sixteenth century), and the rootstock is chosen for the
soil, the disease and the size of tree wanted --- dwarfing
rootstocks make the small trees of modern orchards.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Micropropagation and totipotency]\label{prop:b2:asexual-reproduction:tissueculture}
\index{micropropagation}\index{totipotency}\index{callus}
Any living plant cell with a nucleus can, in principle, regenerate a
whole plant. In \emph{tissue culture} a sterilised piece of tissue
(an \emph{explant}: a shoot tip, a leaf disc, an anther) is placed on
an agar medium with sugar, minerals, vitamins and hormones. With
auxin and cytokinin in balance the cells dedifferentiate into a
\emph{callus}, a mass of dividing unspecialised cells; with more
cytokinin than auxin the callus forms shoots, with more auxin than
cytokinin it forms roots, and in some species it forms \emph{somatic
embryos} --- complete embryos without an egg. Shoots are cut and
subcultured every few weeks, multiplying by a factor of three to
ten each time, so that one meristem gives a million plantlets in a
year. Because viruses lag behind the growing tip, a shoot meristem
under a millimetre long is usually virus-free even in an infected
plant: \emph{meristem culture} is how potato, strawberry, banana and
orchid stocks are cleaned. The method is the practical proof of
totipotency, and the reason a plant variety can be sold by the
million within a few years of its creation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Skoog and Miller (1957) grew tobacco pith callus on media with
different ratios of auxin to the newly discovered cytokinin: high
auxin gave roots, high cytokinin gave shoots, a balance gave only
callus --- organ formation was set by a ratio of two hormones, not
by a specific organ-forming substance. Steward (1958) suspended
single cells and small clumps from a carrot root in a rotating
flask of coconut milk: some clumps formed embryo-like structures
that grew into complete flowering carrots, showing that a
differentiated cell of a mature organ still holds, and can use, the
whole programme of development.
\end{proof}

\section{The cost of sex and the cost of doing without}

\begin{theorem}[The twofold cost of sex]\label{thm:b2:asexual-reproduction:twofold}
\index{twofold cost of sex}
In a population where females make $F$ offspring each, of which half
are sons, an asexual mutant female who makes $F$ daughters, all
copies of herself, doubles her share of the next generation's
females. If the asexual lineage has frequency $p$ among females,
then in the next generation
\[
p' = \frac{2p}{1 + p}, \qquad p_t = \frac{2^t p_0}{1 + (2^t - 1)p_0},
\]
so that from one in a thousand it reaches half the population in
ten generations and all of it soon after. Everything else being
equal, sex halves the rate at which a lineage multiplies --- the
price of making males. Since sex is nevertheless nearly universal,
everything else is not equal: sexual lineages must, on average, gain
at least a factor two from recombination.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Asexual females number $pN$ and leave $FpN$ daughters; sexual
females number $(1 - p)N$ and leave $F(1 - p)N/2$ daughters (the
other half are sons, who make no eggs). The asexual share of the
daughters is $Fp/(Fp + F(1 - p)/2) = 2p/(1 + p)$. Writing $q = p/(1 -
p)$, the recursion becomes $q' = 2q$, so $q_t = 2^t q_0$; converting
back gives $p_t$. Setting $p_t = \tfrac12$ gives $2^t q_0 = 1$, i.e.\
$t = \log_2(1/q_0) \approx 10$ for $p_0 = 10^{-3}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[What sex is for]\label{prop:b2:asexual-reproduction:whysex}
\index{Muller's ratchet}\index{Red Queen hypothesis}
Three benefits are large enough to matter. \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:asexual-reproduction:whysex}{Muller's ratchet}}:
in a \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} every harmful \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutation} is inherited by all descendants,
and a mutation-free genome, once lost from a finite population by
chance, can never be remade; the load ratchets upward, one click at
a time, and small asexual populations decay. Recombination rebuilds
clean genomes from two damaged ones. \emph{Adaptation}: two useful
\omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations} arising in different individuals of a \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} compete and
one is lost; in a sexual population they combine (Fisher and Muller,
1930s), so that sexual populations adapt faster when many genes must
change. \emph{The Red Queen}: parasites adapt to the commonest host
\omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype}, and a \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone}, being one \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype}, is the commonest of all;
sexual parents make each offspring a new lock. Snails, fish and
plants that have both sexual and clonal populations show the
sexual ones prevailing where parasites are abundant. The asexual
lineages that flourish --- dandelions, bdelloid rotifers, most crop
\omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clones} --- are either young, or \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:polyploidy}{polyploid} hybrids that carry
variation of their own, or propped up by a grower who kills their
parasites for them.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Monocultures of a clone]\label{ex:b2:asexual-reproduction:monoculture}
In the 1840s most of the potato crop of Ireland was one clonal
variety, the Lumper; the water mould \emph{Phytophthora infestans},
arriving in 1845, met a country of genetically identical, uniformly
susceptible plants and destroyed the crop in three successive years.
The world's export banana until the 1950s was a single \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone}, Gros
Michel, wiped out of Central American plantations by a soil fungus;
its replacement, Cavendish, is likewise a single \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} and is now
being lost, plantation by plantation, to a new strain of the same
fungus, against which it has no variation to offer. A \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} is the
easiest target in nature, and the grower who plants one must supply
the resistance that recombination would have supplied for free.
\end{example}

\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A Strawberry Farm and an Old Clone}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the arithmetic of a clone, from
a strawberry field filled by runners and a laboratory that multiplies
a meristem, to the invasion of a sexual population by an apomict and
the genetic decay of an ancient genet, ending on the time to fill the
field, the plantlets from one meristem, and the equilibrium of
apomicts and sexuals}]
\label{pb:b2:asexual-reproduction:1}
A grower plants \num{100} strawberry plants in a field of
\qty{1}{ha} that can carry \num{4} plants per square metre. Each
plant makes \num{6} runners a year, each rooting \num{2} daughters,
and all plants survive. A laboratory multiplies the same variety by
tissue culture: each subculture, every \num{4} weeks, multiplies the
shoots by \num{5}; \qty{80}{\%} of plantlets survive weaning.
Ordinary cuttings of the variety are \qty{90}{\%} virus-infected;
meristem tips of \qty{0.3}{mm} carry the virus with probability
$0.02$. Aspen genome: \qty{4.8e8}{} base pairs; somatic \omterm{thm:b2:genome-diversification:rate}{mutation rate}
\qty{1e-9}{} per base pair per cell division; \num{20} meristem cell
divisions per year in a growing shoot.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Filling the field.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How many plants can the field hold?
  \item By what factor does the number of plants multiply each year?
  \item Compute the number of plants after 1, 2 and 3 years.
  \item Solve $N(t) = 100\times 13^{t}$ for the time at which the
        field is full.
  \item Once full, only the plants at the edge of a patch find room.
        If a patch advances \qty{0.5}{m} a year, how many years would
        one plant take to cover the field by advance alone?
  \item Explain why the grower plants \num{100} plants spread over the
        field rather than one.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The laboratory.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How many subcultures are needed to go from one meristem to at
        least $10^{6}$ shoots? How many weeks?
  \item How many plants survive weaning?
  \item What is the probability that a meristem-derived line is
        virus-free? That all \num{10} lines started from one plant
        are?
  \item The laboratory starts \num{10} lines and discards infected
        ones after a test. Expected number of clean lines? Compare
        with cuttings.
  \item Explain why the meristem tip escapes the virus.
  \item The field could be planted from the laboratory's output in
        the first year rather than filled by runners in three. Give
        one advantage and one risk of each route.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- An apomict invades.}
A dandelion population is sexual; an apomictic (seed-cloning) mutant
appears at frequency $p_0 = 10^{-3}$ with the same \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} output per
plant. Half of the sexual plants' reproductive effort goes into
\omterm{def:b2:plant-life-cycles:heterospory}{pollen}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Justify the recursion $p' = 2p/(1 + p)$ for the apomict's
        frequency among \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} parents.
  \item Compute $p$ after 5 and 10 generations, and the generation at
        which it passes one half.
  \item A rust fungus specialises on the commonest \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:vocabulary}{genotype}: the
        apomict's \omterm{def:b2:angiosperm-reproduction:seed}{seed} output is reduced by a factor $(1 - cp)$, with
        $c = 0.8$. Write the new recursion.
  \item Find the equilibrium frequency $p^*$ and check its stability
        from the sign of $p' - p$ on either side.
  \item For what values of $c$ does the apomict take over completely?
  \item Compute the equilibrium for $c = 0.6$. Interpret: what must a
        parasite do to protect sex?
  \item The apomict is a triploid. Why can it not go back to sex, and
        why does this matter in the long run?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The old \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the number of somatic \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations} per cell division in
        aspen.
  \item Two trunks of the Pando \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} are separated by \num{14000}
        years of growth on each side of their common ancestor. How
        many cell divisions separate them, and how many \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations}
        distinguish their genomes?
  \item What fraction of the genome is that? If \qty{2}{\%} of the
        genome is coding, how many coding differences?
  \item A \omterm{def:b2:asexual-reproduction:clone}{clone} has no \omterm{def:b2:meiosis-heredity:meiosis}{meiosis} and no selection at the level of the
        organism to remove these \omterm{def:b2:genome-diversification:mutation}{mutations}. What does remove them,
        and why is the sieve weaker than in a sexual lineage?
  \item Is Pando ``genetically uniform''? Discuss in two sentences.
  \item State the result: the time to fill the field by runners, the
        plantlets one meristem gives in a year, and the equilibrium
        frequency of the apomict for $c = 0.8$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
