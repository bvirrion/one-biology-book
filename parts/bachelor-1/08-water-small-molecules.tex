\chapter{Water and Small Biomolecules}\label{ch:b1:water-small-molecules}

A bacterium of one cubic micrometre contains some twenty billion water
molecules and a few hundred million of everything else. Water is not
the background of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} but its medium: it dissolves the ions and the
sugars, it hides the oily parts of proteins and membranes, it sets the
acidity that every enzyme depends on, and it is a reactant or a product
in half the reactions of metabolism. This chapter describes water and
the weak bonds it makes and breaks, the arithmetic of acids, \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{bases} and
\omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffers}, the small organic molecules from which the macromolecules of
the next four chapters are built, and the currency of energy --- \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} ---
in which the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} pays for building them.

\section{Water}

\begin{definition}[The water molecule]\label{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}
\index{water}
Water, $\mathrm{H_2O}$, is a bent molecule (the H--O--H angle is
$104.5^\circ$) in which the oxygen draws the shared electrons toward
itself: it is \emph{polar}\index{polar molecule}, with a partial
negative charge on the oxygen and partial positive charges on the
hydrogens. Each molecule can form up to four \emph{hydrogen
bonds}\index{hydrogen bond} --- an electrostatic attraction between a
hydrogen bound to an electronegative atom (O, N) and a lone pair of
another electronegative atom --- two through its hydrogens, two through
its oxygen. Liquid water is a network of such bonds, each lasting a few
picoseconds.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Properties of water that matter to life]\label{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:properties}
The hydrogen-bond network explains water's exceptional properties:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a high \emph{cohesion} and surface tension (\qty{72}{mN/m}):
        water columns can be pulled up a tree without breaking
        (\cref{ch:b1:plant-transport}); insects walk on ponds;
  \item a high \emph{heat capacity} (\qty{4.18}{kJ.kg^{-1}.K^{-1}})
        and heat of vaporisation (\qty{2.4}{kJ/g}): temperatures of
        \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} and of water bodies change slowly, and evaporating a
        gram of sweat removes \qty{2.4}{kJ};
  \item a \emph{solid less dense than the liquid}: ice floats, and
        lakes freeze from the top down, leaving liquid water below;
  \item an excellent \emph{solvent} for ions and \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{polar molecules}, which
        it surrounds with oriented shells (\emph{hydration}), and a
        poor one for non-polar molecules, which it forces together
        (the \emph{hydrophobic effect}\index{hydrophobic effect}).
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Partial proof]
Cohesion and surface tension measure the work of separating molecules
that are each held by four bonds of about \qty{20}{kJ/mol}. Heating
water must break bonds as well as agitate molecules, hence the heat
capacity; vaporising it must break them all. Ice holds every molecule
in an open tetrahedral lattice of four bonds, which occupies more volume
than the disordered liquid, in which molecules pack more closely. Ions
and polar groups are stabilised by \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds} to water; a non-polar
molecule cannot make them, and the water around it must order itself
into a cage, at a cost in entropy that is reduced when non-polar
molecules cluster and share one cage.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The hydrophobic effect at work]\label{ex:b1:water-small-molecules:hydrophobic}
Shake oil into water and within minutes the droplets have merged: water
squeezes out what it cannot bond to. The same effect folds a protein
(its oily residues hide inside, \cref{ch:b1:proteins}), assembles a
membrane (the tails of the lipids gather away from water,
\cref{ch:b1:lipids}), and drives two matching molecular surfaces
together. Nothing attracts the oil to the oil; water pushes.
\end{example}

\section{Weak bonds}

\begin{definition}[Covalent and non-covalent bonds]\label{def:b1:water-small-molecules:bonds}
A \emph{covalent bond}\index{covalent bond} shares an electron pair
between two atoms and costs \qtyrange{300}{500}{kJ/mol} to break: the
skeletons of molecules are covalent, and only enzymes make or break
them in the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}. \emph{Non-covalent bonds}\index{non-covalent bond}
are the weak, reversible attractions that hold molecules \emph{to each
other} and shape them: the \emph{\omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bond}} (\qtyrange{4}{20}{kJ/mol}
in water); the \emph{ionic bond}\index{ionic bond} between opposite
charges (\qty{12}{kJ/mol} or so in water, whose dielectric constant
screens charges eightyfold); \emph{van der Waals}\index{van der Waals force} attractions between any two atoms in close contact
(\qtyrange{1}{4}{kJ/mol} each, but summed over a whole surface); and
the \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:properties}{hydrophobic effect}}, which is not a bond but the exclusion of
non-polar surfaces by water.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Why weak bonds are the bonds of biology]\label{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:weak}
Thermal energy at \qty{37}{\celsius} is $RT = \qty{2.6}{kJ/mol}$. A
single weak bond is only a few times this and breaks within
microseconds; ten together, on two surfaces that fit, hold for hours.
Molecular \emph{recognition}\index{molecular recognition} --- an enzyme
for its substrate, an antibody for its antigen, one DNA strand for its
complement --- rests on many weak bonds formed at once between
complementary surfaces, which is why it is at once specific (only a
fitting surface makes them all) and reversible (heat, or a competitor,
undoes it). \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:bonds}{Covalent bonds}, a hundredfold stronger, would be permanent.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Melting a double helix]\label{ex:b1:water-small-molecules:melting}
The two strands of DNA are held by two or three \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:water}{hydrogen bonds} per
\omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{base} pair plus the stacking (\omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:bonds}{van der Waals}) of neighbouring pairs; a
single pair would separate at once, but a thousand pairs in a row hold
until the temperature reaches about \qty{90}{\celsius}, and reunite,
in exactly the same register, when it is lowered
(\cref{ch:b1:nucleic-acids}). Specific, strong in number, reversible.
\end{example}

\section{Acids, bases and buffers}

\begin{definition}[pH, acid, base, p$K_a$]\label{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}
Water dissociates slightly, $\mathrm{H_2O} \rightleftharpoons
\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{OH^-}$, with $[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] =
K_w = \qty{e-14}{mol^2/L^2}$ at \qty{25}{\celsius}. The
\emph{pH}\index{pH} is $-\log_{10}[\mathrm{H^+}]$: 7 for pure water,
lower for acids, higher for bases. An \emph{acid}\index{acid} $\mathrm{HA}$
releases a proton, $\mathrm{HA} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+} +
\mathrm{A^-}$, with dissociation constant $K_a =
[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]/[\mathrm{HA}]$ and
\emph{p$K_a$}\index{pKa@p$K_a$} $= -\log_{10}K_a$; its conjugate
\emph{base}\index{base} $\mathrm{A^-}$ accepts one. A strong acid
(HCl) is fully dissociated; the acids of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} (carboxylic acids,
phosphates, ammonium) are weak, with p$K_a$ between 2 and 10.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Henderson--Hasselbalch]\label{thm:b1:water-small-molecules:hh}
\index{Henderson--Hasselbalch equation}
For a weak acid in solution,
\[
\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} .
\]
At $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a$ the acid is half dissociated; one \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}
unit above, it is \qty{91}{\%} dissociated; one below, \qty{9}{\%}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take the logarithm of $K_a = [\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]/[\mathrm{HA}]$:
$\log K_a = \log[\mathrm{H^+}] + \log([\mathrm{A^-}]/[\mathrm{HA}])$,
i.e.\ $-\mathrm{p}K_a = -\mathrm{pH} + \log([\mathrm{A^-}]/[\mathrm{HA}])$.
The ratio is $1$, $10$ and $0.1$ at \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} $= \mathrm{p}K_a$, $\mathrm{p}K_a + 1$
and $\mathrm{p}K_a - 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Buffer]\label{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}
A \emph{buffer}\index{buffer} is a solution containing a weak acid and
its conjugate \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{base} in comparable amounts; it resists a change of \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH},
because added $\mathrm{H^+}$ is taken up by $\mathrm{A^-}$ and added
$\mathrm{OH^-}$ by $\mathrm{HA}$. It works best within one unit of the
p$K_a$, and its \emph{capacity} grows with the total concentration of
the pair. The \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s buffers: phosphate
($\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-}/\mathrm{HPO_4^{2-}}$, p$K_a$ 6.8) and the side chains
of proteins inside \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells}; bicarbonate ($\mathrm{CO_2}/\mathrm{HCO_3^-}$,
effective p$K_a$ 6.1) in the blood, whose acid partner is a gas that
the lungs can remove.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Buffer arithmetic]\label{met:b1:water-small-molecules:bufferarith}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the acid--base pair and its p$K_a$ at the working
        temperature.
  \item Apply Henderson--Hasselbalch to get the ratio \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{base}/acid from
        the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}, or the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} from the concentrations.
  \item To add a strong acid: subtract its amount from the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{base} and
        add it to the acid, then recompute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}. To add a strong
        \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{base}: the reverse.
  \item If one partner can leave the system (carbon dioxide breathed
        out, ammonia excreted), the system is \emph{open}: hold that
        partner at its imposed value rather than letting it accumulate.
        Open \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffers} hold \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} far better than closed ones.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Blood]\label{ex:b1:water-small-molecules:blood}
Arterial blood holds \qty{24}{mmol/L} of bicarbonate and dissolved
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ at \qty{1.2}{mmol/L} (set by the lungs): $\mathrm{pH} =
6.1 + \log(24/1.2) = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.4$. Adding \qty{5}{mmol/L} of acid
turns \qty{5}{mmol/L} of bicarbonate into $\mathrm{CO_2}$; if the lungs
blow that $\mathrm{CO_2}$ off and hold the dissolved value, the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}
becomes $6.1 + \log(19/1.2) = 7.30$; in a closed flask the $\mathrm{CO_2}$
would rise to \qty{6.2}{mmol/L} and the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} fall to $6.1 + \log(19/6.2) =
6.59$. The lungs make the difference.
\end{example}

\section{Small biomolecules}

\begin{definition}[Functional groups]\label{def:b1:water-small-molecules:groups}
The organic molecules of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} are carbon skeletons bearing a small
set of \emph{functional groups}\index{functional group} that determine
their chemistry:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
group & formula & property & found in \\
\hline
hydroxyl & $-\mathrm{OH}$ & polar, H-bonding & sugars, alcohols \\
carbonyl & $>\!\mathrm{C{=}O}$ & polar, reactive & aldoses, ketoses \\
carboxyl & $-\mathrm{COOH}$ & acid (p$K_a \approx 4$) & fatty acids, amino acids \\
amino & $-\mathrm{NH_2}$ & \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{base} (p$K_a \approx 9$) & amino acids \\
phosphate & $-\mathrm{OPO_3^{2-}}$ & charged, energy-rich links & nucleotides, \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} \\
sulfhydryl & $-\mathrm{SH}$ & forms S--S bridges & cysteine \\
methyl & $-\mathrm{CH_3}$ & non-polar & lipids, DNA marks \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
At the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} of the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} carboxyl groups are ionised ($-\mathrm{COO^-}$)
and amino groups protonated ($-\mathrm{NH_3^+}$).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Four families of building blocks]\label{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:families}
Nearly all the organic matter of a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} is built from four kinds of
small molecule, each of a few hundred daltons: \emph{sugars}
(\cref{ch:b1:carbohydrates}), \emph{fatty acids} (\cref{ch:b1:lipids}),
\emph{amino acids} (\cref{ch:b1:proteins}) and \emph{nucleotides}
(\cref{ch:b1:nucleic-acids}). Three of them are joined into
\emph{polymers} --- polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids --- by
\emph{condensation}\index{condensation} (a bond formed with loss of a
water molecule), and taken apart by \emph{hydrolysis}\index{hydrolysis}
(the bond broken by adding water). Condensation is uphill and paid for
by \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP}; \omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:families}{hydrolysis} is downhill and needs only an enzyme.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The cell's inventory]\label{ex:b1:water-small-molecules:inventory}
Of a bacterium's dry mass, proteins are \qty{55}{\%}, RNA \qty{20}{\%},
DNA \qty{3}{\%}, lipids \qty{9}{\%}, polysaccharides \qty{5}{\%}, small
molecules and ions the rest. By number of molecules the picture
inverts: a thousand kinds of small metabolite and ion, at
concentrations from nanomolar to a tenth of a mole per litre, make up
most of the molecules that are not water. Potassium is at
\qty{150}{mmol/L}, glutamate \qty{100}{mmol/L}, \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} \qty{5}{mmol/L}, a
transcription factor at a few molecules per \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}.
\end{example}

\section{Energy: free energy and ATP}

\begin{proposition}[Free energy of a reaction]\label{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:gibbs}
\index{free energy}
A reaction at constant temperature and pressure proceeds spontaneously
in the direction that lowers the Gibbs \omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:gibbs}{free energy} $G$. For
$\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{C} + \mathrm{D}$,
\[
\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ\prime} + RT\ln\frac{[\mathrm{C}][\mathrm{D}]}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]},
\]
where $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ is the standard free-energy change
(all concentrations \qty{1}{mol/L}, \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} 7) and the logarithmic term
corrects for the actual concentrations. At equilibrium $\Delta G = 0$,
so $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = -RT\ln K'_{\text{eq}}$: every
\qty{5.7}{kJ/mol} of $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ shifts the equilibrium
constant tenfold. $\Delta G$ says which way a reaction can go, not how
fast: that is the enzyme's business (\cref{ch:b1:enzymes}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The chemical potential of each species is $\mu = \mu^\circ + RT\ln c$
(the ideal-solution result of the physics course); $\Delta G$ is the
sum of the products' potentials minus the reactants', which gives the
formula; setting it to zero at equilibrium gives the relation to
$K'_{\text{eq}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[ATP, coupling]\label{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}
\emph{ATP}\index{ATP} (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide
(\cref{ch:b1:nucleic-acids}) bearing three phosphates in a row; the two
outer bonds are \emph{phosphoanhydride}\index{phosphoanhydride bond}
bonds whose \omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:families}{hydrolysis}, $\mathrm{ATP} + \mathrm{H_2O} \to \mathrm{ADP} +
\mathrm{P_i}$, has $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = \qty{-30.5}{kJ/mol}$ and,
at the concentrations of a \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, $\Delta G \approx \qty{-50}{kJ/mol}$.
ATP is the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s \emph{energy currency}\index{energy currency}:
catabolism makes it (\cref{ch:b1:respiration-fermentation}), and
biosynthesis, transport and movement spend it. An uphill reaction is
made to go by \emph{coupling}\index{energy coupling} it to ATP
\omterm{prop:b1:water-small-molecules:families}{hydrolysis} through a shared intermediate, so that the sum of the two
$\Delta G$ is negative.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Why ATP hydrolysis releases so much]\label{ex:b1:water-small-molecules:whyatp}
Three reasons: the four negative charges of \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP}'s phosphates repel one
another and are relieved by cleavage; the products $\mathrm{ADP}$ and
$\mathrm{P_i}$ are better stabilised by resonance and by hydration than
the anhydride; and the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} keeps \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} a thousand times above its
equilibrium with ADP and phosphate. The last is the largest term:
with \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} at \qty{5}{mmol/L}, ADP \qty{1}{mmol/L}, $\mathrm{P_i}$
\qty{10}{mmol/L},
$\Delta G = -30.5 + 2.58\ln\frac{10^{-3}\times 10^{-2}}{5\times 10^{-3}}
= -30.5 - 16 = \qty{-46.5}{kJ/mol}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Coupling]\label{ex:b1:water-small-molecules:coupling}
Glucose $+\ \mathrm{P_i} \to$ glucose-6-phosphate $+\ \mathrm{H_2O}$
has $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = \qty{+13.8}{kJ/mol}$: at equilibrium
almost no glucose would be phosphorylated. Hexokinase instead
transfers the phosphate straight from \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP}: glucose $+$ \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:atp}{ATP} $\to$
glucose-6-phosphate $+$ ADP, $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = 13.8 - 30.5 =
\qty{-16.7}{kJ/mol}$, equilibrium constant $800$, reaction essentially
complete. The phosphate never appears free: the two reactions are one.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: Blood as a Buffer}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- five litres of blood at pH 7.40
through a sprint: bicarbonate, carbon dioxide, lungs and kidneys,
ending on the pH shift for a given lactate load}]
\label{pb:b1:water-small-molecules:1}
Arterial blood: \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} 7.40, bicarbonate $[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}] =
\qty{24}{mmol/L}$, dissolved carbon dioxide $[\mathrm{CO_2}] =
\qty{1.2}{mmol/L}$ (proportional to its partial pressure: a partial
pressure of \qty{40}{mmHg} gives \qty{1.2}{mmol/L}). The bicarbonate
system has an effective p$K_a$ of 6.1: $\mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O}
\rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{HCO_3^-}$. Blood volume
\qty{5}{L}. The plasma proteins and haemoglobin add a second \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffer} of
capacity \qty{25}{mmol} of $\mathrm{H^+}$ per litre per \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} unit. A
sprint releases \qty{60}{mmol} of lactic acid (a strong acid at blood
\omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}) into the blood within a minute.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The resting state.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Verify the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} of arterial blood from the bicarbonate and
        carbon dioxide values.
  \item Compute $[\mathrm{H^+}]$ at \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} 7.40, in nanomoles per litre.
  \item How many free protons are there in the whole blood volume?
        Compare with the \qty{120}{mmol} of bicarbonate.
  \item Compute the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved carbon dioxide
        and the fraction of the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffer} pair present as the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{base}.
  \item Venous blood has a partial pressure of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ of
        \qty{46}{mmHg} and \qty{25}{mmol/L} of bicarbonate. Compute its
        \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}.
  \item Why is the p$K_a$ of 6.1, more than one unit below blood \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH},
        not a handicap for this \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffer} in the body, although it would
        be in a flask?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The sprint, closed system.}
Suppose first that no carbon dioxide can leave: every proton taken up
by bicarbonate becomes dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the lactic acid concentration added to the blood.
  \item Compute the new bicarbonate and the new dissolved
        $\mathrm{CO_2}$ if all the acid is buffered by bicarbonate.
  \item Compute the resulting \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}.
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} the same acid would give in \qty{5}{L} of pure
        water.
  \item Is the closed bicarbonate system a good \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffer} for this load?
        Quantify by the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} shift.
  \item Compute the change in $[\mathrm{H^+}]$ between \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} 7.40 and the
        \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} of question 9, and the fraction of the added protons that
        remain free in solution.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The sprint, \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{open system}.}
Now the lungs hold the dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$ at \qty{1.2}{mmol/L}
by blowing off the excess.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} with bicarbonate at its new value and
        $\mathrm{CO_2}$ held at \qty{1.2}{mmol/L}.
  \item How many millimoles of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ must the lungs remove,
        in excess of the resting output, to hold the value?
  \item The resting output is \qty{10}{mmol} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ per
        minute. By what factor must ventilation rise for a minute to
        clear the excess?
  \item The runner hyperventilates further, driving the partial
        pressure of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ to \qty{30}{mmHg}. Compute the new
        dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}.
  \item Now include the protein \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffer}: of the \qty{60}{mmol} of
        protons, a share is taken by proteins in proportion to the two
        \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffers}' capacities. Estimate the capacity of the open
        bicarbonate system near \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} 7.4 (the millimoles of acid per
        litre that shift the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} by one unit, from Henderson--Hasselbalch
        with $\mathrm{CO_2}$ fixed) and the share of the protons each
        \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffer} takes.
  \item Recompute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} after the sprint with both \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffers}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Recovery, and the kidney.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Over the next hour the liver oxidises the lactate (removing
        the acid). What happens to the bicarbonate and the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}, with the
        lungs holding $\mathrm{CO_2}$ constant?
  \item A patient with kidney failure retains \qty{60}{mmol} of acid
        a day and cannot excrete it. Compute the fall of bicarbonate
        per day (\omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{open system}) and the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} after three days if
        bicarbonate is not replaced.
  \item A patient with lung disease retains $\mathrm{CO_2}$ at a
        partial pressure of \qty{60}{mmHg} with bicarbonate at
        \qty{24}{mmol/L}. Compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}. The kidneys respond over days
        by raising bicarbonate to \qty{33}{mmol/L}; compute the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH}
        then.
  \item Explain in two sentences why the lungs correct \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} in minutes
        and the kidneys in days, and what each controls in the
        Henderson--Hasselbalch ratio.
  \item The kidneys excrete the \qty{60}{mmol} of acid of a day in
        \qty{1.5}{L} of urine at \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} 5. Compute the free protons in that
        urine, and conclude how the acid is carried.
  \item Vomiting loses \qty{100}{mmol} of HCl. Predict the direction of
        the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} change and compute it for the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{open system}.
  \item State the result: the \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:ph}{pH} shift of the blood for a
        \qty{60}{mmol} lactate load in the closed system, in the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{open
        system} with bicarbonate alone, and with the protein \omterm{def:b1:water-small-molecules:buffer}{buffers}
        added.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
