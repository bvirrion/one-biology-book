\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis} --- Cell Cycle Control and Programmed Cell Death}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:1}
G1, S, G2, M. G1 progression and the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{restriction point}: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} D--Cdk4/6;
G1/S: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} E--Cdk2; S: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} A--Cdk2; G2/M: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} B--Cdk1. The
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{anaphase-promoting complex} (APC/C) destroys \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} B and securin and
ends mitosis.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:2}
Hartwell: the \emph{cdc} mutants of budding yeast, arrested at defined
stages, and the concept of Start. Nurse: \emph{cdc2} of fission yeast
as the kinase timing mitosis, and its human homologue (Cdk1) rescuing
the yeast --- conservation. Hunt: \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} in sea urchin eggs, a protein
synthesised through the cycle and destroyed at each division.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:3}
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{Restriction point} (late G1): growth factors, nutrients, size; acts on
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} D--Cdk4/6 and Rb. G2/M: DNA intact and replicated; \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:ddr}{ATM}/ATR
inhibit Cdc25, keeping Cdk1 phosphorylated and inactive. Spindle
assembly: every kinetochore attached; unattached kinetochores make
Mad2--Cdc20 complexes that inhibit the APC/C.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:4}
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{Apoptosis}: cell shrinks, \omterm{def:b3:chromatin-epigenetics:nucleosome}{chromatin} condenses, DNA cut into a ladder,
membrane blebs but stays sealed, \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{phosphatidylserine} exposed, corpse
engulfed, no inflammation. \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{Necrosis}: cell swells, membrane ruptures,
contents leak, random DNA degradation, inflammation. Executioners:
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{caspases}, cysteine proteases cutting after aspartate.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:5}
Rise from $5$ to $30$ at $1$ per minute: \qty{25}{min}. Degradation from
$30$ to $5$ with half-life \qty{2}{min}: $\log_{2}(30/5)\times 2 =
\qty{5.2}{min}$. Period about \qty{30}{min}, mitosis \qty{17}{\%} of it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:6}
(a) $C$ can never fall below $C_{1}$: locked in mitosis on the high
branch. (b) No inhibitory phosphorylation: the threshold $C_{2}$ is
lowered, mitosis begins early at a small size (the ``wee'' phenotype).
(c) Cdk1 stays phosphorylated: the high branch is unreachable, arrest in
G2 with elongated cells. (d) As (b), and the G2/M \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint}, which acts
through Wee1/Cdc25, can no longer hold the cell.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:7}
G1 nucleus in S-phase cytoplasm: its origins are licensed and the
cytoplasm supplies active \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} E/A--Cdk2, so it enters S at once. G2
nucleus in S-phase cytoplasm: its origins fired and were not
relicensed (licensing factors destroyed or inhibited by CDK), so it
cannot replicate again; it waits until the partner reaches G2 and both
enter mitosis together.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:8}
The unattached kinetochore is a catalyst: it converts Mad2 into its
active conformation continuously, producing a diffusible inhibitor of
Cdc20 that spreads through the cell and keeps the APC/C off everywhere
--- one kinetochore makes enough. Without Mad2 the APC/C is activated
as soon as Cdk1 has switched it on, whether or not chromosomes are
attached: anaphase begins with unattached chromosomes, daughters are
aneuploid, and the organism (or the cell line) dies of chromosome loss.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:9}
No \emph{ced-9}: cells that should live die --- embryonic lethality
from too much death. No \emph{ced-3}: none of the $131$ deaths occurs,
the cells survive and differentiate, the worm is viable. Both: no death
--- the \emph{ced-3} phenotype. Since removing the executioner cancels
the effect of removing the guardian, CED-9 acts upstream, by holding
CED-4/CED-3 inactive; when it is gone they are active, unless they are
absent too.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:10}
With $A = aC$, $\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = k_{s} - k_{d}aC^{2}$, a
one-variable equation with steady state $C^{*} = \sqrt{k_{s}/(k_{d}a)}$
and slope $-2k_{d}aC^{*} < 0$ there: any deviation decays
monotonically, and a single first-order variable cannot oscillate. The
S-shaped curve gives two stable branches separated by a forbidden
zone, so the state must jump and cannot settle; an explicit delay in
the feedback would give oscillations by a different route --- the brake
arriving too late, overshooting, and so on --- as in many hormonal
rhythms.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:11}
Each arriving sensor is captured by a guardian, so nothing happens
until all $N$ guardians are occupied, at $t \approx N/r$; the next
sensors are free, activate Bax/Bak, whose pore formation rises steeply
with their number, and the mitochondria open within minutes. A stronger
stimulus shortens the time only through $r$; above the threshold the
outcome is the same. Venetoclax occupies guardians, reducing the
effective $N$: the time shrinks, and a primed cell --- already near $N$
--- dies at once.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:12}
Excess Bcl-2 blocks the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:pathways}{intrinsic pathway}: B~cells that should die when
their growth signals end survive, and the population grows by failure
of death, at the slow rate at which such cells are made --- indolent,
until a second lesion adds proliferation. Loss of Rb removes the
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{restriction point}: retinal precursors proceed through the cycle
without growth factors and divide as fast as the engine allows ---
aggressive. The two tumours are the two programmes each failing alone:
a death control and a division control, either of whose loss suffices
for a tumour, the second faster.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:1}
\textbf{1.} $10^{7}\times 3500/5 = 7\times 10^{9}$ cells a day, about
\num{80000} a second.
\textbf{2.} $3500/5 = 700$ per crypt per day; $22\times 2^{5} = 704$.
\textbf{3.} $80\times 365 \approx \num{29000}$ divisions; $\times 0.6
\approx \num{17500}$ mutations.
\textbf{4.} Transit cells are shed within days, taking their mutations
with them; the stem cell persists for life, so every one of its
mutations is kept and each division adds more --- slow cycling
minimises them.
\textbf{5.} The villus empties in about the $5$ days of its normal
transit; rebuilding takes a day of recovery, five divisions at
\qty{12}{h} and the migration up the villus --- four to five days, during
which the barrier is bare: the gut's fast transit divisions make it an
early casualty of radiation.
\textbf{6.} A sealed apoptotic corpse releases no contents into a lumen
full of bacteria and provokes no inflammation; \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{necrosis} would spill
enzymes and signals and inflame the mucosa at every cell shed.
\textbf{7.} $(40 - 8)/2 = \qty{16}{h}$.
\textbf{8.} $\log_{2}(40/8)\times \qty{10}{min} = 2.3\times 10 =
\qty{23}{min}$.
\textbf{9.} Period about \qty{16.4}{h}; Cdk1 active \qty{2.3}{\%} of the
time.
\textbf{10.} Not $k_{s}$ alone but the time held at the thresholds: the
somatic cell waits at the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{restriction point} for growth factors and at
G2/M for its DNA, and the stem cell longer than the transit cell. The
frog egg has no \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoints} and a cytoplasm stocked with everything, so
only the bare oscillator sets its period.
\textbf{11.} On the low branch at a \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} level above the normal
$C_{2}$: the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint} has shifted the S-curve to the right by
inhibiting Cdc25, so the jump does not occur. On repair Cdc25 is
released, the threshold falls below the accumulated \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin}, and the
cell enters mitosis at once --- which is why checkpoint-released cells
enter mitosis synchronously.
\textbf{12.} Neither \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:cdk}{cyclin} B nor securin is degraded: the cell stays in
metaphase with cohesin intact and Cdk1 active, indefinitely; it dies
there or eventually slips out with an unsegregated genome.
\textbf{13.} $10^{11}$ divisions a day.
\textbf{14.} With the \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:checkpoints}{checkpoint} $10^{11}/5\times 10^{4} = 2\times 10^{6}$
aneuploid daughters a day; without, $2\times 10^{9}$.
\textbf{15.} $2\times 10^{4}$ a day; over $80$ years $6\times 10^{8}$.
\textbf{16.} $10^{9}/50 = 2\times 10^{7}$ missegregations a day: every
day the tumour tries twenty million new karyotypes, and selection keeps
the ones that grow faster or resist a drug.
\textbf{17.} With every division missegregating, no embryonic cell
lineage keeps a euploid genome and the embryo dies. Partial loss gives
occasional aneuploidy in cells that survive it (especially without
p53), a chromosomal instability that supplies variation to a tumour
without killing the organism.
\textbf{18.} A meiotic error puts the extra chromosome into the gamete,
so the zygote and every cell derived from it are trisomic; a mitotic
error occurs in one somatic cell and is inherited only by its clone.
\textbf{19.} $10^{11}\times \qty{1}{ng} = \qty{100}{g}$ a day,
\qty{36}{kg} a year --- about half the body's mass every year.
\textbf{20.} \qty{20}{g} of protein a day, about a third of the dietary
intake and a few per cent of the body's total protein turnover.
\textbf{21.} $10^{11}$ corpses at $24$ per macrophage per day: $4\times
10^{9}$ macrophages full time, \qty{4}{\%} of the $10^{11}$
macrophages' time.
\textbf{22.} A lysed corpse releases proteases, DNA, ATP and other
alarm signals that cause inflammation and can provoke autoimmunity
against nuclear antigens; the intact corpse advertises itself with
\omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{phosphatidylserine} on its outer leaflet (and by losing its ``don't eat
me'' signals), and attracts phagocytes with released nucleotides.
\textbf{23.} The \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:pathways}{intrinsic pathway} is blocked at the mitochondrion. The
extrinsic route still works where caspase-8 activates caspase-3
directly, though its amplification through Bid is lost. Cells
accumulate by not dying rather than by dividing faster --- slow growth
--- until a later lesion (often \emph{MYC}) adds proliferation.
\textbf{24.} \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{Apoptosis} takes hours, needs ATP and passes through
steps that can be blocked --- \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{caspase} inhibitors, the mitochondrial
threshold --- so penumbral neurons can be rescued if treated in time;
core neurons die of energy failure within minutes, with no programme to
interrupt.
\textbf{25.} Bare oscillator period about \qty{16}{h}; some $2\times
10^{4}$ aneuploid dividing cells a day in a normal body; \qty{100}{g}
of cells recycled by \omterm{def:b3:cell-cycle-apoptosis:apoptosis}{apoptosis} each day.
\end{solution}
