\chapter{Ecosystems and Trophic Structure}\label{ch:b1:ecosystem-organization}

A spring in a limestone plain pours out water so clear that every
plant, fish and turtle in it can be counted from a boat. Sunlight
falls on the eelgrass at \qty{1.7}{} million kilocalories per square
metre a year; the grass stores one percent of it; the snails and
turtles that eat the grass get a sixth of that; the fish that eat the
snails a tenth again; and the one heron at the top lives on a
hundred-thousandth of what fell on the water. Every \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem} is
built this way: a stream of energy that enters once, passes from
eaters to eaten, and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} as heat, while the matter it moves is
recycled. This chapter defines the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem} and its parts, the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{niche}
each species occupies, the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{trophic levels} and \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:foodweb}{food webs} through which
energy flows, the productivity that sets the size of the whole, and
the ways of counting how many kinds of things live in it.

\section{Ecosystem and niche}

\begin{definition}[Ecosystem, biotope, community]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}
\index{ecosystem}\index{biotope}\index{community}
An \emph{ecosystem} is the set of \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} living in a defined place
together with the physical environment they occupy and exchange with:
the \emph{community} (or biocoenosis) --- all the \omterm{def:b1:populations:population}{populations} of all
the species --- and the \emph{biotope} --- the soil, water, air, light
and climate. Its boundaries are chosen for the question: a rotting
log, a pond, a forest, an ocean basin, the whole biosphere. An
ecosystem is an \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{open system} (\cref{ch:b1:organism-environment}): energy
flows through it, matter cycles within it and across its borders, and
its structure is the pattern of who eats whom.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Habitat, niche]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}
\index{ecological niche}\index{habitat}
A species' \emph{habitat} is where it lives; its \emph{niche} is how it
lives: the full set of conditions it tolerates and resources it uses
--- temperature, moisture, light, food, time of activity, nesting
sites --- each of them a dimension along which the species occupies
a range. The \emph{fundamental niche} is the range it could occupy
alone; the \emph{realised niche} the part it actually occupies in the
presence of competitors and predators (\cref{ch:b1:species-interactions}).
Two species with the same niche cannot coexist in one place for long;
the niches of coexisting species differ, and the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community} is a set
of niches fitted together.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Five warblers in one spruce]\label{ex:b1:ecosystem-organization:warblers}
Five species of small insect-eating warbler nest in the same spruce
forests and eat the same insects; watched closely, each forages in a
different part of the tree --- the treetop, the outer new needles, the
middle branches, the trunk and lower branches, the ground beneath ---
and at different heights and times. Their \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{habitat} is one; their
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{niches} are five, and the forest holds five species where the food
alone would suggest one.
\end{example}

\section{Trophic structure}

\begin{definition}[Producers, consumers, decomposers]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}
\index{trophic level}\index{producer}\index{consumer}\index{decomposer}
The \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} of an \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem} are grouped by what they eat into
\emph{trophic levels}. \emph{Producers} (autotrophs: plants, algae,
cyanobacteria, chemosynthetic bacteria) make organic matter from
inorganic; they are the first level. \emph{Consumers} eat it:
\emph{primary consumers} (herbivores) eat producers, \emph{secondary
consumers} (carnivores) eat herbivores, \emph{tertiary} consumers eat
carnivores; omnivores eat at several levels. \emph{Decomposers}
(bacteria, fungi) and \emph{detritivores} (earthworms, woodlice,
dung beetles) live on dead matter and wastes from every level, and
return its minerals to the soil and water for the producers to take
up again --- the link that closes the cycle of matter.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Food chain, food web]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:foodweb}
\index{food chain}\index{food web}
A \emph{food chain} is a sequence of \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} each eating the one
before: grass, grasshopper, frog, snake, hawk. In a real \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community}
each species eats several and is eaten by several, and the chains
interlock into a \emph{food web}, whose arrows point from the eaten
to the eater --- in the direction of the energy. Chains are short:
rarely more than four or five links, for a reason the next section
gives.
\end{definition}

\section{The flow of energy}

\begin{definition}[Productivity]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:productivity}
\index{primary productivity}\index{gross primary productivity}\index{net primary productivity}
The \emph{gross primary productivity} (GPP) of an \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem} is the
rate at which its \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers} fix energy by photosynthesis, per unit
area and time (\unit{kJ.m^{-2}.yr^{-1}}, or grams of carbon or dry
matter); the \emph{net primary productivity} (NPP) is what remains
after the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers}' own respiration --- the organic matter actually
made available to the rest of the system. \emph{Secondary
productivity} is the rate at which \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{consumers} build their own biomass
from what they eat. \emph{Biomass} is the standing stock, the mass
present at a moment; productivity is a flow, biomass a store, and
the ratio of the two is the turnover time.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The ten-percent rule]\label{thm:b1:ecosystem-organization:lindeman}
\index{trophic efficiency}
Of the energy entering one \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{trophic level} as food, only about a tenth
--- between \qty{5}{} and \qty{20}{\%} --- reaches the next level as
its food. The rest is not eaten, is not assimilated (\omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} as faeces),
or is respired by the level itself. The energy available therefore
falls tenfold per level, which is why \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:foodweb}{food chains} rarely exceed four
or five links, and why the biomass and numbers of top predators are
small.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Partial proof]
The energy assimilated by a level is what it eats minus what it
excretes; of that, respiration takes what the level spends on living
(\cref{ch:b1:organism-environment}), and only the remainder --- its
growth and reproduction --- is available to be eaten. Each of the
three fractions (eaten, assimilated, converted to biomass) is well
below one: for a herbivore on a meadow about \qty{20}{\%} of the
plant growth is eaten, \qty{50}{\%} of that assimilated, and
\qty{10}{\%} of that turned into flesh, giving \qty{1}{\%}; for a
carnivore, which eats and assimilates more of its prey, about
\qty{10}{\%}. The rule is an observed regularity, not a law; its size
is what the measurements give.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Lindeman (1942) measured, in a small lake, the energy fixed by the
plankton and the energy held and respired at each level above it, and
found each level passing on about a tenth. Odum (1957) drew the full
budget of a Florida spring: of \qty{1.7e6}{kcal} per square metre per
year of sunlight, the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers} fixed \num{20800} (gross), respired
\num{12000} and left \num{8800} net; the herbivores took \num{3400}
and passed \num{380} to the carnivores; the carnivores passed
\num{20} to the top carnivores. The efficiencies were \qty{1.2}{\%},
\qty{16}{\%}, \qty{11}{\%} and \qty{5}{\%} --- the ten-percent rule
within a factor of two at each step.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Pyramids]\label{prop:b1:ecosystem-organization:pyramids}
\index{ecological pyramid}
Stacking the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{trophic levels} gives a \emph{pyramid}. A pyramid of
\emph{energy} (flow per level) is always upright: each level can only
pass on less than it received. A pyramid of \emph{biomass} (standing
stock) is usually upright but can be inverted: in the open sea the
phytoplankton, eaten as fast as it grows, weighs less at any moment
than the zooplankton living on it, because it turns over in days while
the animals last months. A pyramid of \emph{numbers} can be any shape:
one oak feeds thousands of caterpillars. Energy is the honest measure.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Drawing up an energy budget]\label{met:b1:ecosystem-organization:budget}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Measure GPP: by gas exchange (oxygen released or
        $\mathrm{CO_2}$ taken up by a plot or a bottle in light and
        dark: light minus dark gives gross, light alone gives net), or
        by harvest (biomass gained per year plus what was eaten and
        shed).
  \item Measure, for each \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{consumer} level, the intake (what it eats),
        the assimilation (intake minus faeces), the respiration (its
        oxygen consumption) and the production (growth plus offspring
        $=$ assimilation minus respiration).
  \item \omterm{thm:b1:ecosystem-organization:lindeman}{Trophic efficiency} between levels $=$ production of the upper
        level $/$ production of the lower. Check that respiration plus
        production plus decomposition of each level sums to its
        assimilation.
  \item Convert to common units (\unit{kJ}, or grams of carbon at
        about \qty{40}{kJ/g}) and draw the pyramid; the sum of all
        respiration should approach the GPP over a year in a steady
        \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Why the top is thin]\label{ex:b1:ecosystem-organization:top}
A square kilometre of savanna makes some \qty{5000}{t} of grass a
year; the grazers turn it into \qty{50}{t} of antelope and buffalo;
the lions into \qty{2}{t} of lion --- one or two animals. To feed a
tiger, a forest must be large enough that its tenth of a tenth of a
tenth of the sunlight is a whole deer every week: a hundred square
kilometres a tiger, which is why large carnivores are rare, roam far,
and vanish first when a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:niche}{habitat} shrinks.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Productivity of the biomes]\label{prop:b1:ecosystem-organization:biomes}
\index{biome}
\omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:productivity}{Net primary productivity} per unit area is set by light, temperature,
water and nutrients: tropical rain forest \qtyrange{2000}{3500}{} g of
dry matter per square metre per year; temperate forest and cultivated
land \qtyrange{600}{1500}{}; grassland \qtyrange{200}{1500}{}; tundra
and desert below \qty{200}{}; the open ocean about \qty{125}{} (light
plentiful, nutrients scarce), upwelling coasts and reefs \qtyrange{500}{2500}{}.
The ocean, two thirds of the planet, contributes about half of global
production; the forests, a tenth of the land, contribute a third of
the land's. Where water and nutrients are ample, productivity tracks
light and warmth; where they are not, it tracks them.
\end{proposition}

\section{Counting diversity}

\begin{definition}[Species richness, diversity index]\label{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:diversity}
\index{species richness}\index{Shannon index}
The \emph{species richness} $S$ of a \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{community} is the number of
species in it. Richness alone ignores abundance: a wood with a
hundred oaks and one of each of nine other trees is less diverse than
one with ten of each. The \emph{Shannon index}
\[
H = -\sum_{i=1}^{S} p_i \ln p_i ,
\]
where $p_i$ is the fraction of individuals belonging to species $i$,
rises with both richness and \emph{evenness}; it is $\ln S$ when all
species are equally abundant and near zero when one dominates. The
\emph{evenness} is $H/\ln S$. Diversity generally rises from the poles
to the tropics, with area, with time since the last disturbance, and
with the productivity of the site up to a point.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Sampling a community]\label{met:b1:ecosystem-organization:sampling}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sample with a method suited to the \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organisms} (quadrats, nets,
        traps, transects) and record the number of individuals of each
        species.
  \item Plot the number of species found against the number of
        samples (or individuals): the curve rises steeply, then
        flattens; where it flattens, the richness is nearly complete.
        Compare \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{communities} only at equal sampling effort.
  \item Compute $H$ and the evenness; compare with the rank--abundance
        plot (species ranked by abundance, on a log scale), whose
        slope shows dominance.
  \item Interpret: low evenness points to a dominant species or a
        stress; a fall of $H$ over time points to a change in the
        \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{biotope}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Two woods]\label{ex:b1:ecosystem-organization:twowoods}
Wood A: 100 oaks and one each of nine other trees; $H = -(0.917\ln
0.917 + 9\times 0.0092\ln 0.0092) = 0.08 + 0.39 = 0.47$, evenness
$0.47/2.30 = 0.20$. Wood B: ten each of the same ten species; $H =
\ln 10 = 2.30$, evenness 1. Same richness, five times the diversity.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Budget of a Spring}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- Odum's spring drawn up level by
level: sunlight to grass, grass to snail, snail to fish, fish to
heron, with the respiration and the decomposers, ending on the trophic
efficiencies at each level}]
\label{pb:b1:ecosystem-organization:1}
A spring receives \qty{1.7e6}{kcal} of sunlight per square metre per
year. Its \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers} fix \qty{20800}{kcal} (gross) and respire
\qty{12000}{}. Herbivores eat \qty{3400}{kcal} of plant matter, of
which they assimilate \qty{1500}{}, respire \qty{1100}{} and turn the
rest into growth and offspring. Carnivores eat \qty{380}{kcal}, respire
\qty{300}{} and produce the rest; top carnivores eat \qty{20}{kcal},
respire \qty{14}{} and produce \qty{6}{}. Everything not eaten or
respired goes to the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers}, who respire it. Take
\qty{1}{kcal} $= \qty{4.18}{kJ}$ and \qty{10}{kcal} per gram of dry
biomass.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers}.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:productivity}{net primary productivity}.
  \item Compute the efficiency of photosynthesis: gross production
        over sunlight. Compare with the \qty{34}{\%} of
        \cref{ch:b1:photosynthesis} and list three losses that
        separate them.
  \item What fraction of the gross production do the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers}
        respire?
  \item How much of the net production is eaten by herbivores, and
        how much goes directly to the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers}?
  \item Express the NPP in grams of dry matter per square metre per
        year and place the spring among the biomes of the chapter.
  \item How much of the sunlight, per square metre and year, \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} the
        spring as heat without ever having passed through a living
        \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{consumers}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the herbivores' production and their assimilation
        efficiency (assimilated/eaten).
  \item Compute the herbivores' production efficiency
        (production/assimilated).
  \item Compute the \omterm{thm:b1:ecosystem-organization:lindeman}{trophic efficiency} from \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers} to herbivores:
        herbivore production over net primary production.
  \item Compute the carnivores' production and the \omterm{thm:b1:ecosystem-organization:lindeman}{trophic efficiency}
        herbivores-to-carnivores.
  \item Compute the \omterm{thm:b1:ecosystem-organization:lindeman}{trophic efficiency} carnivores-to-top carnivores.
  \item How much energy per square metre per year reaches the top
        carnivore's own \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}? What fraction of the sunlight is
        that?
  \item Why does the assimilation efficiency rise from herbivores to
        carnivores?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers} and the balance.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item List every flow to the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers} (uneaten net production,
        herbivore faeces and uneaten herbivore production, and so on)
        and sum them.
  \item Compute the total respiration of the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:ecosystem}{ecosystem}: \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers},
        herbivores, carnivores, top carnivores and \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers}.
  \item Compare it with the gross production. What does the comparison
        mean for the spring's biomass over a year?
  \item What fraction of the gross production is respired by
        \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers}?
  \item Draw the pyramid of energy with the four levels' production
        and label the efficiencies.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Stocks, turnover and change.}
The standing biomass is \qty{800}{g/m^2} of \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{producers}, \qty{40}{g/m^2}
of herbivores, \qty{10}{g/m^2} of carnivores and \qty{1.5}{g/m^2} of top
carnivores.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Convert each biomass to kilocalories and draw the pyramid of
        biomass. Is it upright?
  \item Compute the turnover time (biomass/production) of each level.
        Which turns over fastest, and why?
  \item A heron of \qty{2}{kg} lives on the spring's top-carnivore
        production. How many square metres does it need?
  \item The spring is enriched with fertiliser and its gross
        production doubles, but the herbivores cannot eat faster.
        What happens to the extra production, to the \omterm{def:b1:ecosystem-organization:trophic}{decomposers}'
        respiration, and to the oxygen of the water at night?
  \item A new fish is introduced that eats the herbivores' eggs and
        halves their production. Predict the carnivores' production
        and the herons' numbers, and the fate of the eelgrass.
  \item Explain why removing the top carnivores would change the
        eelgrass far more than removing the same energy's worth of
        herbivores.
  \item State the result: the trophic efficiencies at each of the
        three transfers, the efficiency of sunlight to gross
        production, and the fraction of the sun's energy that reaches
        the top carnivores.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
