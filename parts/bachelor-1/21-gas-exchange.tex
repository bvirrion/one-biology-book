\chapter{Respiratory Gas Exchange}\label{ch:b1:gas-exchange}

A litre of air holds thirty times more oxygen than a litre of water,
weighs eight hundred times less, and lets oxygen diffuse through it
ten thousand times faster. A trout must pass a hundred and fifty
grams of water over its \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gills} for every milligram of oxygen it takes,
and it extracts four fifths of what the water carries; a human moves
a few grams of air per milligram and wastes three quarters of it.
The two \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:organ}{organs} are answers to the same equation --- the flux of a gas
across a surface --- in two media. This chapter states the physics,
derives what an \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface} must be, describes \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gills}, \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lungs} and
tracheae, follows oxygen and carbon dioxide through the blood, and
looks at how ventilation is controlled and what changes at altitude
and under water.

\section{The physics of a gas in two media}

\begin{definition}[Partial pressure, solubility]\label{def:b1:gas-exchange:partial}
\index{partial pressure}
In a mixture of gases each gas exerts a \emph{partial pressure}
proportional to its fraction: in air at \qty{101}{kPa}, oxygen
(\qty{20.9}{\%}) has $P_{\mathrm{O_2}} = \qty{21.2}{kPa}$, carbon
dioxide (\qty{0.04}{\%}) \qty{0.04}{kPa}. A gas dissolves in a liquid
in proportion to its partial pressure (Henry's law): water in
equilibrium with air at \qty{15}{\celsius} holds \qty{0.32}{mmol/L} of
oxygen (\qty{10}{mg/L}, \qty{7}{mL/L} of gas), against \qty{8.7}{mmol/L}
(\qty{210}{mL/L}) in the air itself. The partial pressure --- the same
in the water and the air at equilibrium --- is what drives diffusion
between media; the content is what a breather can extract. Carbon
dioxide is twenty-five times more soluble than oxygen, so it is never
the harder gas to exchange.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Fick's law for an exchange surface]\label{thm:b1:gas-exchange:fick}
\index{Fick's law}
The rate at which a gas crosses a membrane of area $A$ and thickness
$x$ separating two media at \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:partial}{partial pressures} $P_1$ and $P_2$ is
\[
\dot V = K\,\frac{A}{x}\,(P_1 - P_2),
\]
where $K$, the diffusion (Krogh) constant of the membrane, combines
the gas's diffusivity and solubility in the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissue}. The flux is
raised by a larger area, a thinner barrier and a larger difference of
\omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:partial}{partial pressure}; nothing else.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fick's first law for the dissolved gas in the membrane
(\cref{ch:b1:membranes-transport}) gives a flux per unit area
proportional to the concentration gradient; by Henry's law the
concentration at each face is proportional to the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:partial}{partial pressure} in
the medium there, so the gradient is $(P_1 - P_2)/x$ times the
solubility, and the constant absorbs solubility and diffusivity.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[What an exchange surface must be]\label{prop:b1:gas-exchange:requirements}
\index{exchange surface}
An \omterm{def:b1:organism-environment:organism}{organism} of more than a millimetre cannot be supplied by
diffusion through its outer surface (\cref{ch:b1:organism-environment}).
It needs a \emph{respiratory surface} that is large (to raise $A$),
thin (to lower $x$), moist (gases cross membranes only in solution),
\emph{ventilated} --- the outer medium renewed, so that $P_1$ stays
high --- and \emph{perfused} --- the blood behind it renewed, so that
$P_2$ stays low; and a circulation to carry the gas from the surface
to the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}. \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{Gills}, \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lungs} and tracheae are three ways of folding
such a surface into a body and moving the two fluids across it.
\end{proposition}

\section{Gills and the counter-current}

\begin{definition}[Gill]\label{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}
\index{gill}
A \emph{gill} is an evagination of the body surface into the water,
folded into filaments and \emph{lamellae} a few micrometres thick
through which blood flows in capillaries. A fish's gills hang from
four bony arches on each side; water drawn in at the mouth and pushed
out under the gill covers by a two-pump system flows between the
lamellae in one direction, and the blood in the lamellae flows the
other way: a \emph{counter-current}\index{counter-current exchange}.
A trout of a kilogram has some \qty{2000}{cm^2} of lamellar surface,
\qty{2}{\micro m} thick.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Counter-current exchange]\label{thm:b1:gas-exchange:countercurrent}
When water and blood flow in opposite directions along an \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange
surface}, the blood leaving the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gill} can approach the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:partial}{partial pressure}
of the water \emph{entering} it, and the water can be stripped of most
of its oxygen: extractions of \qty{80}{\%} or more. When they flow in
the same direction (co-current), both fluids converge to a common
intermediate value and the extraction cannot exceed \qty{50}{\%}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Along a co-current exchanger the difference $P_{\text{water}} -
P_{\text{blood}}$ falls from its inlet value to zero as the two
approach the same pressure; once they are equal no more gas moves,
and for equal flows the meeting point is halfway. In \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current}
flow the blood, as it moves toward its outlet, meets water that is
ever fresher, so a positive difference is maintained along the whole
length; blood at its outlet faces the incoming water at \qty{21}{kPa},
and water at its outlet faces the incoming venous blood at a few
kilopascals. The limit is set by the surface, not by an equilibrium.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The price of water]\label{ex:b1:gas-exchange:priceofwater}
A resting trout of \qty{1}{kg} takes about \qty{50}{mL} of oxygen an
hour. From water at \qty{7}{mL/L}, with \qty{80}{\%} extraction, it must
pump \qty{9}{L} of water an hour across its \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gills} --- nine kilograms of
a medium fifty times more viscous than air, through \omterm{def:b1:membranes-transport:transporters}{channels} a fraction
of a millimetre wide. A tenth of its metabolism goes to breathing; a
human at rest spends a fiftieth. Warm the pond to \qty{30}{\celsius}
and the same fish, needing more oxygen from water holding less, must
pump three times as much.
\end{example}

\section{Breathing air: lungs and tracheae}

\begin{definition}[The mammalian lung]\label{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}
\index{lung}\index{alveolus}
A \emph{lung} is an invagination of the body surface into a moist
internal chamber, which keeps the surface wet in dry air. The
mammalian lung is a tree of airways ending in some three hundred
million \emph{alveoli}, sacs \qty{0.2}{mm} across whose walls --- one
epithelial \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} and one capillary endothelium, \qty{0.5}{\micro m} in
all --- add up to \qty{100}{m^2} in a human, wrapped in a capillary
net through which the whole cardiac output passes. It is ventilated
\emph{tidally}: the \omterm{def:b1:mammal-organization:bodyplan}{diaphragm} and rib muscles enlarge the chest, air
flows in; they relax, air flows out by the same path. A breath moves
\qty{500}{mL}, of which \qty{150}{mL} only fills the airways
(\emph{dead space}) and never reaches an alveolus; the alveolar gas,
renewed by a seventh at each breath, stays at $P_{\mathrm{O_2}} =
\qty{13.3}{kPa}$ and $P_{\mathrm{CO_2}} = \qty{5.3}{kPa}$ --- the
pressures the blood equilibrates with.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Three designs for air]\label{prop:b1:gas-exchange:designs}
\index{tracheae}
\emph{Mammals} ventilate tidally: simple, but the fresh air mixes
with stale, the alveolar oxygen is two thirds of the atmosphere's,
and extraction is a quarter. \emph{Birds} pass air in one direction
through fine tubes (parabronchi) between two sets of air sacs that
act as bellows, so that fresh air flows through the \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface}
during both inspiration and expiration, in a cross-current with the
blood: their extraction is higher, and a bar-headed goose flies over
the Himalaya where a mammal would faint. \emph{Insects} have no
respiratory blood at all: air enters through valves (\emph{spiracles})
into \emph{tracheae}, tubes stiffened by spiral thickenings that
branch to a micrometre and deliver oxygen by diffusion directly to
every \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, aided by pumping of the abdomen in large or active
insects. Diffusion along tubes limits the design to small bodies ---
which is one reason no insect is larger than a mouse.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Reading a ventilation budget]\label{met:b1:gas-exchange:budget}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Minute ventilation $=$ tidal volume $\times$ breaths per
        minute; alveolar ventilation $=$ (tidal volume $-$ dead space)
        $\times$ breaths per minute. Only the second exchanges gas.
  \item Oxygen uptake $=$ alveolar ventilation $\times$ (inspired
        fraction $-$ alveolar fraction); at rest,
        $\qty{4.2}{L/min}\times(0.21 - 0.14) \approx \qty{0.3}{L/min}$.
  \item Extraction $=$ uptake $/$ (ventilation $\times$ inspired
        fraction): a quarter in a tidal \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung}, four fifths in a
        \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current} \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gill}.
  \item On the blood side: uptake $=$ cardiac output $\times$
        (arterial $-$ venous content); \qty{5}{L/min}$\times$
        \qty{50}{mL/L} at rest, the same \qty{250}{mL/min}. The two
        budgets must match.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Gases in the blood}

\begin{proposition}[Oxygen and carbon dioxide transport]\label{prop:b1:gas-exchange:transport}
\index{carbonic anhydrase}\index{Bohr effect}\index{Haldane effect}
Plasma dissolves only \qty{3}{mL} of oxygen per litre at arterial
pressure; haemoglobin (\cref{ch:b1:proteins}) carries \qty{200}{mL},
loading in the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung} at \qty{13.3}{kPa} and unloading a fifth to a
half in the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues}. Carbon dioxide travels three ways: \qty{7}{\%}
dissolved, \qty{23}{\%} bound to the amino groups of haemoglobin, and
\qty{70}{\%} as bicarbonate: in the red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:gas-exchange:transport}{carbonic
anhydrase}} turns $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and water into carbonic acid within
milliseconds, the acid dissociates, the bicarbonate \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} in
exchange for chloride (the \emph{chloride shift}), and the proton is
taken up by haemoglobin. The two gases help each other: acid and
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ lower haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen (\emph{\omterm{prop:b1:gas-exchange:transport}{Bohr
effect}}), so oxygen is released where $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is made; and
deoxygenated haemoglobin binds protons and $\mathrm{CO_2}$ better
(\emph{\omterm{prop:b1:gas-exchange:transport}{Haldane effect}}), so $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is taken up where oxygen is
released, and given up in the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung} as oxygen loads.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[A litre of blood through a working muscle]\label{ex:b1:gas-exchange:litre}
Arterial blood brings \qty{200}{mL} of oxygen per litre and
\qty{480}{mL} of carbon dioxide (mostly as bicarbonate). In a resting
muscle it \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} \qty{50}{mL} of oxygen and takes up \qty{40}{mL} of
$\mathrm{CO_2}$; the venous pH falls by only \qty{0.04}{}, because
haemoglobin has absorbed the protons. In a sprinting muscle, at pH
7.2 and $P_{\mathrm{O_2}}$ of \qty{2.7}{kPa}, the same litre \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves}
\qty{150}{mL} of oxygen (\cref{ch:b1:proteins}) and carries away the
lactate as well.
\end{example}

\section{Control, altitude and diving}

\begin{proposition}[Ventilation is controlled by carbon dioxide]\label{prop:b1:gas-exchange:control}
\index{chemoreceptor}
The rhythm of breathing is generated in the brainstem and adjusted
by \emph{chemoreceptors}: central ones, bathed in the fluid around the
brain, respond to the pH change that $\mathrm{CO_2}$ produces;
peripheral ones in the carotid arteries respond to a fall of arterial
oxygen below about \qty{8}{kPa}. In ordinary life it is
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ that rules: a rise of \qty{1}{kPa} in arterial
$P_{\mathrm{CO_2}}$ roughly doubles ventilation, while oxygen must fall
a third before ventilation responds. Holding one's breath ends when
$\mathrm{CO_2}$, not oxygen, reaches the limit; hyperventilating first
lowers $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and lets a diver stay under until oxygen runs
out --- unconscious before the urge to breathe returns.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Altitude and depth]\label{ex:b1:gas-exchange:altitudediving}
At \qty{4000}{m} the air holds \qty{12}{kPa} of oxygen and the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{alveoli}
\qty{7}{}: the carotid bodies drive ventilation up, which blows off
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ and makes the blood alkaline --- a brake the kidneys
release over days by excreting bicarbonate; the red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cells} raise their
BPG within days and the marrow raises haemoglobin within weeks
(\cref{ch:b1:proteins}). A seal diving for twenty minutes does the
opposite: it exhales before diving, slows its heart to a tenth, shuts
the blood off from everything but brain and heart, and runs its
muscles on the oxygen bound to their own myoglobin, ten times more
concentrated than ours, and then on \omterm{def:b1:respiration-fermentation:fermentation}{fermentation}, tolerating a lactate
the body clears at the surface. Its \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lungs} collapse below \qty{50}{m},
which keeps nitrogen out of its blood.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:1}
State \omterm{thm:b1:membranes-transport:fick}{Fick's law} for an \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface} and list the four features
that raise the flux.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:2}
Explain why water is a harder medium to breathe than air, with three
numbers.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:3}
From the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current} figure, read the oxygen pressure of the water
leaving and of the blood leaving in each arrangement, and the
extraction.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:4}
In what three forms is carbon dioxide carried in the blood, and in
what proportions?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:5}
A person breathes \qty{15}{} times a minute with a tidal volume of
\qty{450}{mL} and a dead space of \qty{150}{mL}. Compute the minute and
alveolar ventilations and the oxygen uptake if the alveolar fraction
is \qty{14}{\%}. Repeat for \qty{30}{} shallow breaths of \qty{225}{mL}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:6}
A goldfish bowl at \qty{25}{\celsius} holds \qty{6}{mL} of oxygen per
litre. A \qty{20}{g} fish uses \qty{2}{mL} of oxygen an hour. How much
water must cross its \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gills} per hour at \qty{75}{\%} extraction, and
how many times its own volume is that?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:7}
A \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung} of \qty{100}{m^2} and \qty{0.5}{\micro m} transfers
\qty{250}{mL/min} at a mean pressure difference of \qty{8}{kPa}. A
disease thickens the wall to \qty{2}{\micro m} and halves the area.
Compute the transfer at the same difference, and the difference needed
to restore \qty{250}{mL/min}. Why can the body not simply raise the
difference?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:8}
Explain the chloride shift: why bicarbonate \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} the red \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, why
chloride enters, and what would happen to the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s volume without
the exchange.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:9}
Why does a diver who hyperventilates before a breath-hold dive risk
drowning, while one who does not surfaces safely, uncomfortable but
conscious?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:10}
An insect trachea \qty{1}{mm} long and \qty{20}{\micro m} across
supplies a muscle that uses \qty{1e-9}{mol} of oxygen per second.
With $D = \qty{2e-5}{m^2/s}$ in air and \qty{8.7}{mol/m^3} of oxygen
at the spiracle, compute the concentration at the muscle end (Fick:
$J = DA\,\Delta c/L$). Repeat for a trachea \qty{10}{mm} long. What
limits the size of insects?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:11}
Birds and fish both use flows that are not tidal. Compare their
exchangers (direction of the medium, relation to the blood flow,
extraction) and explain why a tidal \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung} cannot reach their
extraction whatever its surface.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b1:gas-exchange:12}
``The \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung} is regulated by the gas it excretes, not by the gas it
takes in.'' Discuss in a paragraph: why $\mathrm{CO_2}$ makes the better
signal at sea level, when the arrangement fails, and how altitude
exposes it.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A Trout and a Human}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a gill and a lung set side by
side: oxygen per litre of medium, ventilation and extraction,
counter-current and tidal flow, and the blood behind each, ending on
the extraction efficiency of the counter-current gill}]
\label{pb:b1:gas-exchange:1}
A resting human takes \qty{250}{mL} of oxygen per minute, breathing
\qty{12}{} times a minute with a tidal volume of \qty{500}{mL} and a
dead space of \qty{150}{mL}; inspired air is \qty{21}{\%} oxygen,
alveolar gas \qty{14}{\%}; cardiac output \qty{5}{L/min}, arterial
oxygen content \qty{200}{mL/L}. A resting trout of \qty{1}{kg} takes
\qty{50}{mL} of oxygen per hour in water at \qty{15}{\celsius} holding
\qty{7}{mL/L}; its haemoglobin carries \qty{100}{mL/L} when saturated;
its blood \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gills} \qty{95}{\%} saturated and returns
\qty{15}{\%} saturated. Water is \qty{800}{} times denser than air.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Two media.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Express the oxygen content of air and of the trout's water in
        millimoles per litre (\qty{22.4}{L/mol}).
  \item Compute the ratio of the two contents.
  \item Compute the mass of medium (air \qty{1.2}{g/L}; water
        \qty{1000}{g/L}) that holds \qty{1}{mmol} of oxygen in each
        case.
  \item Compute the volume of the trout's water that holds as much
        oxygen as one litre of air.
  \item Explain in one sentence why fish \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gills} must be ventilated in
        one direction and \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lungs} may be tidal.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the minute ventilation and the alveolar ventilation.
  \item What fraction of each breath is dead space, and what fraction
        of the minute ventilation is therefore wasted?
  \item Compute the oxygen delivered to the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{alveoli} per minute and
        the oxygen taken up (alveolar ventilation $\times$ the
        difference of fractions). Check against \qty{250}{mL/min}.
  \item Compute the extraction efficiency: uptake divided by the
        oxygen in the inspired minute ventilation.
  \item Compute the alveolar $P_{\mathrm{O_2}}$ from the alveolar
        fraction (total pressure \qty{101}{kPa}, water vapour
        \qty{6.3}{kPa} to subtract first).
  \item On the blood side, compute the arteriovenous difference
        needed for \qty{250}{mL/min} at \qty{5}{L/min}, and the venous
        content and saturation.
  \item During exercise the uptake rises to \qty{3}{L/min} with a
        cardiac output of \qty{20}{L/min}. Compute the arteriovenous
        difference and the venous saturation.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gill}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the trout's oxygen uptake in millilitres per minute.
  \item With \qty{80}{\%} extraction (\omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current}), compute the
        water ventilated per minute and per hour.
  \item With the extraction a co-current exchanger of equal surface
        would give (\qty{43}{\%}), compute the water needed. What
        does the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current} save?
  \item Compute the mass of water pumped per hour, and compare it with
        the mass of air a human moves per hour.
  \item Compute the trout's arteriovenous difference in oxygen content
        and the cardiac output its uptake requires.
  \item Compute the ratio of water flow to blood flow at the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gill}, and
        the ratio of air flow to blood flow at the human \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung}
        (alveolar ventilation over cardiac output). Comment.
  \item Pumping water costs the trout about \qty{10}{\%} of its
        oxygen uptake. If the water warms to \qty{25}{\celsius}
        (\qty{5.5}{mL/L}) and its uptake rises by half, by what factor
        must ventilation rise, and what happens to the fraction spent
        on breathing?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The exchanger itself.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item In the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current} \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gill} the blood \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} at \qty{18}{kPa}
        facing water entering at \qty{21}{kPa}, and water \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} at
        \qty{4}{kPa} facing blood entering at \qty{3}{kPa}. Compute
        the pressure difference at each end.
  \item In a co-current exchanger both fluids leave at \qty{12}{kPa}.
        Compute the difference at each end and explain why exchange
        stops before the water is used up.
  \item The human \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung}: alveolar \qty{13.3}{kPa}, venous blood
        arriving at \qty{5.3}{kPa}, arterial leaving at \qty{13.3}{kPa}.
        Which arrangement does a tidal \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung} resemble, and why can its
        arterial blood not exceed the alveolar value?
  \item A trout's lamellae total \qty{2000}{cm^2} at \qty{2}{\micro m}
        thick; a human's \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{alveoli} \qty{100}{m^2} at \qty{0.5}{\micro m}.
        Compute the ratio $A/x$ of the two, and the ratio of their
        oxygen uptakes. What does the comparison suggest about the
        mean pressure difference across a lamella relative to an
        alveolar wall?
  \item Explain why carbon dioxide is never limiting for a fish
        although water holds so little oxygen.
  \item State the result: the extraction efficiency of the trout's
        \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gill} and of the human's \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung}, and the two features of the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gill}
        that make the difference.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
