\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:genetic-engineering} --- Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:1}
An origin of replication, so that the host copies the \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid} and
passes it to daughter cells; a \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{selectable marker}, usually antibiotic
resistance, so that only cells carrying the \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid} grow; and a unique
restriction site (or cluster of sites) at which the insert is ligated
without cutting the \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid} elsewhere.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:2}
A given six-base sequence occurs once every $4^{6} = 4096$ bp in random
DNA: about $4.6\times 10^{6}/4096 \approx 1100$ fragments averaging
\qty{4.1}{kb}. Real genomes are not random --- base composition,
codon usage and the avoidance of some sequences shift the count (the
actual number of EcoRI sites in \emph{E.~coli} is several hundred).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:3}
Denaturation at \qty{95}{\celsius} separates the strands; annealing at
\qtyrange{50}{65}{\celsius} lets the \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:pcr}{primers} pair with their sites;
extension at \qty{72}{\celsius} lets the polymerase copy from each
\omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:pcr}{primer}. An ordinary polymerase would be destroyed at \qty{95}{\celsius}
in the first cycle and would have to be added afresh thirty times; Taq
survives all the cycles.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:4}
A \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:crispr}{guide RNA} whose twenty bases match the target, and a \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:crispr}{PAM} (NGG)
immediately 3$'$ of the target on the non-target strand. After the
\omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:lesions}{double-strand break}: end joining, which leaves a small insertion or
deletion and usually destroys the gene's reading frame; or \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{homologous
recombination} with a template carrying the desired sequence, which
writes it in.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:5}
$50\times 1.9^{35} = 50\times 5.7\times 10^{9} \approx 3\times 10^{11}$
copies. $\Delta C_{T} = 7.3$; ratio $1.95^{7.3} = e^{7.3\ln 1.95}
\approx 130$: the first sample had about $130$ times more template.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:6}
Bacteria cannot splice: a genomic copy with introns would be
transcribed into an unusable message, so the intron-free cDNA, copied
from the mature mRNA, is used. Other obstacles: the human promoter is
not recognised by bacterial polymerase (supply a bacterial promoter and
ribosome-binding site); human codon usage differs (synthesise the gene
with the host's preferred codons); the protein may not fold or may
aggregate (lower the temperature, fuse to a soluble partner, or refold
from inclusion bodies); glycosylation is absent (use yeast or mammalian
cells if it matters); disulfides need the oxidising periplasm.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:7}
The targeted stem cells are injected into a host blastocyst and mix
with its own cells, so the mouse is built from two genotypes --- a
chimaera --- and only if some of its germ cells derive from the
targeted cells does it transmit the allele. Breeding the chimaera to a
wild-type mouse gives heterozygotes (from half its gametes if half the
germ line is targeted); intercrossing heterozygotes gives one quarter
homozygous \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:transgenic}{knockouts}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:8}
Exact: $6.4\times 10^{9}\times 4^{-20}\times (1/16) \approx 4\times
10^{-4}$ sites (the GG of the \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:crispr}{PAM} costs a factor $16$) --- none, in
practice. Within two mismatches there are $1 + 60 + 1710 = 1771$
sequences: $1771\times 4\times 10^{-4} \approx 0.7$ chance sites. The
real genome is not random, and a guide is checked against it directly.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:9}
$p_{1} = 0.05 + 0.8\times 0.05\times 0.95 = 0.088$; $p_{2} = 0.152$;
$p_{3} = 0.255$; then $0.41$, $0.60$: about five generations to pass
$0.5$, against the estimate $\ln(1/(2\times 0.05))/\ln 1.8 \approx 4$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:10}
End joining acts in every cell, including quiescent stem cells in
which \omterm{def:b3:dna-repair:dsb}{homologous recombination}, an S/G2 process, is inefficient; it
needs no template to be delivered and its product --- any disruption of
the enhancer --- does the job, whereas correction requires the exact
sequence and yields a minority of cells. Also, the same edit works for
every $\beta$-globin mutation, sickle or thalassaemic. Drawback: it
removes a regulatory element (with whatever other roles it has), it
leaves the sickle allele in place, and the mixture of indels is
uncontrolled.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:11}
With homozygote fitness $1 - s$: $p' = \bigl[p^{2}(1-s) + p(1-p)(1+c)
\bigr]/(1 - sp^{2})$. From rarity homozygotes are negligible and the
drive grows as $(1+c)p$ whatever $s$; whether it goes to fixation is
decided near $p = 1$, where a rare wild-type allele of frequency $q$
returns as $q' \approx q(1-c)/(1-s)$: fixation if $c > s$, an
intermediate equilibrium otherwise. Resistance: each failed conversion
(end joining instead of recombination) leaves an allele that the guide
no longer recognises; if such alleles are fit --- an in-frame indel in
a non-essential region --- they carry no cost while the drive does, so
selection favours them and they replace the drive. The remedies are to
target essential sequences where indels are lethal, and to use several
guides at once.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:genetic-engineering:12}
\omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:crispr}{Cas9} injected into a zygote may act after the first divisions, so
different cells receive different repairs --- mosaicism; the repair of
each break is chosen by the cell, end joining giving unpredictable
indels and large deletions or loss of heterozygosity at the cut; the
homologous-recombination outcome is a minority; off-target breaks
occur at sites that cannot all be predicted; and the embryo can only
be checked by sequencing a few biopsied cells, which cannot speak for
the rest. Evidence needed: methods that edit every cell identically
(before the first S phase) with efficiencies near \qty{100}{\%},
whole-genome sequencing of every cell of edited animal embryos showing
no unintended change, and long-term follow-up of edited animals over
generations --- none of which exists.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:genetic-engineering:1}
\textbf{1.} Once per $4096$ bp. $P(\text{no site}) = (1 - 1/4096)^{1500}
= e^{-0.37} = 0.69$.
\textbf{2.} Choose an enzyme with no site in the gene; or amplify the
gene by PCR with \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:pcr}{primers} that carry new restriction sites at their 5$'$
ends (or use blunt or ligation-independent cloning).
\textbf{3.} \qty{0.1}{\micro g}\,$\times 2\times 10^{7} = 2\times
10^{6}$ colonies; \qty{10}{\%}, $2\times 10^{5}$, carry the insert.
\textbf{4.} White: \qty{10}{\%}. Half the inserts are in the right
orientation, so $P(\text{none in } n \text{ whites}) = 0.5^{n} \le 0.01$
gives $n \ge 6.6$: pick seven.
\textbf{5.} $32$ doublings, $2^{32} = 4.3\times 10^{9}$ cells, $2.1\times
10^{11}$ \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmids}; each $5500\times 650 = 3.6\times 10^{6}$ Da $=
5.9\times 10^{-18}$ g: \qty{1.3}{\micro g} of \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid}.
\textbf{6.} Cloning needs only replication, supplied by the \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid}'s
origin; the human promoter is not read by bacterial RNA polymerase, so
for expression a bacterial promoter and ribosome-binding site must be
put in front of the (intron-free) coding sequence.
\textbf{7.} $N_{0} = N_{T}/2^{C_{T}}$: $10^{12}/2^{28} \approx 3700$
copies; at $C_{T} = 35$, $10^{12}/2^{35} \approx 29$ copies.
\textbf{8.} $2^{n} = 10^{12}$: $n = 39.9$, forty cycles.
\textbf{9.} $10^{12}/2^{38} \approx 3.6$ copies. Beyond about $40$
cycles a single contaminating molecule, or \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:pcr}{primer} artefacts, reach the
threshold, and a sample of fewer than one copy is meaningless.
\textbf{10.} One molecule crosses at cycle $40$: a positive. Prevent it
with separate rooms and equipment for setting up and for handling
products, one-way workflow, negative controls in every run, and
enzymatic destruction of carried-over amplicons.
\textbf{11.} $3700/0.4 \approx 9000$ RNA copies.
\textbf{12.} Reported $2^{3.3} \approx 10$-fold; true $1.9^{3.3} =
e^{3.3\ln 1.9} \approx 8.3$-fold.
\textbf{13.} $40\times \qty{35}{\micro g} = \qty{1.4}{mg}$ a day,
\qty{0.51}{g} a year; $\times 10^{7}$: \qty{5100}{kg} a year.
\textbf{14.} $5.1\times 10^{6}/5800 \approx 880$ mol; $5.3\times 10^{26}$
molecules.
\textbf{15.} $5.1\times 10^{6}$ g $/ (20$ g/L$) = 2.6\times 10^{5}$ L
$= \qty{260}{m^3}$ --- a tenth of the pool.
\textbf{16.} $5100\times 8000 = 4.1\times 10^{7}$ kg of pancreas, at
\qty{0.1}{kg} each: $4\times 10^{8}$ pigs a year.
\textbf{17.} The recombinant hormone is identical to the human one, so it
raises no antibodies and causes no allergic reactions; it carries no
animal pathogens (viruses, prions); supply does not depend on slaughter;
and the sequence can be improved.
\textbf{18.} The residues that touch the receptor are unchanged, so
binding and signalling are those of insulin; the altered residues lie
at the surface where insulin molecules associate into dimers and
hexamers, and changing them changes how fast the injected depot falls
apart into absorbable monomers.
\textbf{19.} $6.4\times 10^{9}\times 4^{-20}/16 \approx 3.6\times
10^{-4}$: no exact chance match.
\textbf{20.} $1 + 60 + 1710 + 27\times 1140 = \num{32551}$ sequences;
$\num{32551}\times 3.6\times 10^{-4} \approx 12$ chance off-target
sites.
\textbf{21.} $10^{7}\times 10^{-4} = 1000$ cells. A stem cell lives and
divides for the patient's lifetime; one that has lost a tumour
suppressor, or acquired a translocation, can found a clone that becomes
a leukaemia.
\textbf{22.} $p_{1} = 0.0019$, $p_{2} = 0.0036$, $p_{3} = 0.0069$;
geometric growth by $1.9$ per generation gives $\ln 500/\ln 1.9
\approx 10$; iterating the recursion, the frequency passes $0.5$ at
generation $11$ ($0.45 \to 0.68$).
\textbf{23.} About a year. A \qty{20}{\%} cost in homozygotes is less
than $c = 0.9$, so the drive still goes to fixation, more slowly, while
the population's mean fitness falls --- which is the purpose of a
suppression drive; a cost above $c$ would stall it at an intermediate
frequency.
\textbf{24.} $10^{6}\times 0.1 = 10^{5}$ resistance alleles in one
generation. Being uncuttable and, if fit, cost-free while the drive is
costly, they are favoured and spread, and the drive is eliminated --- so
drives target sites where end-joining products are lethal, with several
guides at once.
\textbf{25.} About $3700$ copies in the first sample; some \qty{260}{m^3}
of fermenter a year for the world's insulin; about $11$ generations, a
mosquito year, for the drive.
\end{solution}
