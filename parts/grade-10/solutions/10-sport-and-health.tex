\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g10:sport-and-health} --- Physical Activity and Health}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:1}
A larger heart with a bigger stroke volume and a lower resting rate;
more capillaries and \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} in the \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{muscle fibres}; more red blood
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}; larger \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:glycogen}{glycogen} stores (and better regulation of blood sugar
and pressure).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:2}
The stroke volume has grown, so the same \qty{5}{L/min} is delivered
in fewer beats: $Q = f \times V_s$ with a larger $V_s$ needs a smaller
$f$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:3}
About an hour a day of moderate activity. No: walking, cycling to
school, stairs and housework all count --- what matters is movement
that raises \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:expenditure}{energy expenditure}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:4}
The use of substances or methods that raise performance by overriding
the body's regulation instead of building an adaptation. EPO overrides
the control of \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{red-cell} number; stimulants override fatigue, the
warning that protects the heart.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:5}
Persistent fatigue, a rising resting heart rate, falling performance
(also disturbed sleep, frequent infections, \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{tendon} or bone pain).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:6}
After 12 weeks: $72 - 60 = 12$ beats for four sessions, $72 - 64 = 8$
for two. Adaptation is proportional to the demand --- more sessions,
more change --- and in both groups it slows as the new state is
approached.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:7}
$V_s = Q/f$: from $5000/72 \approx \qty{69}{mL}$ to $5000/54 \approx
\qty{93}{mL}$, a factor of $72/54 \approx 1.33$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:8}
$1 - 0.55 = 45\%$ lower. The first step, from least active to low
(1.00 to 0.80), brings the largest gain.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:9}
\qty{2.0}{kg}, i.e. $2.0/55 \approx 3.6\%$ of her mass. Her blood
volume falls, her heart rate rises to keep the output, her body
temperature climbs, her performance drops; cramps become likely.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:10}
$10 \times 1.1^7 \approx \qty{19.5}{km}$, so \qty{20}{km} is reached
in the eighth week: about two months.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:11}
Each fibre thickens by adding myofibrils, and the nervous system learns
to recruit more fibres at once and in better synchrony; both raise the
force. Fibre number is fixed in adulthood.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:12}
Oxygen per litre rises by $56/45 \approx 1.24$, about 24\%; with the
same \omterm{def:g10:heart-lungs-effort:output}{cardiac output} and extraction, $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max rises by
up to the same fraction. But blood with 56\% \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} is much thicker;
slowed by a sleeping heart it can clot in a coronary or cerebral
vessel.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:13}
At altitude the body itself raises its EPO in response to a real lack
of oxygen, by a controlled amount, and lowers it again when the lack
ends: the regulation is at work. Injected EPO imposes a number the
regulation would never choose, with no lack to justify it and no
brake.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:14}
Working muscle takes up glucose without insulin, and trained muscle
stays more sensitive to it; walking therefore lowers the blood sugar
directly and reduces the insulin needed at every meal. A drug lowers
the sugar but leaves the cause --- insensitive, unused muscle --- in
place, and does nothing for the heart, bones or mass that walking
also improves.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g10:sport-and-health:15}
Adaptation is built during rest between repeated, gradually increased
demands: a month of intensive load is more likely to produce
overtraining and injury than fitness, since \omterm{def:g10:muscles-and-joints:muscle}{tendon} and bone adapt over
months, not weeks. And the gains dismantle within weeks of stopping:
nothing is "set up" for a year by a single burst. Regular moderate
activity through the year does what the month cannot.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g10:sport-and-health:1}
\textbf{1.} $4800/72 \approx \qty{67}{mL}$ before; $4800/56 \approx
\qty{86}{mL}$ after.

\textbf{2.} $42 \times 62 \approx \qty{2.6}{L/min}$; $50 \times 62 =
\qty{3.1}{L/min}$: a gain of 19\%.

\textbf{3.} Maximal output $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}/0.150$: from
\num{17.4} to \qty{20.7}{L/min}; maximal stroke volume at 200 beats:
from \num{87} to \qty{103}{mL}.

\textbf{4.} The larger, stronger heart explains the stroke volume;
more capillaries and \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:organelle}{mitochondria} in the fibres would explain a gain
in extraction.

\textbf{5.} A factor of 4 in 26 weeks is an average of $4^{1/26}
\approx 1.055$, about 5.5\% a week --- within the 10\% rule ($1.1^{26}
\approx 12$ would have allowed far more).

\textbf{6.} Overtraining: rising resting heart rate, disturbed sleep,
infection, falling performance, pain in a slow-repairing tissue.

\textbf{7.} A stress injury of the bone of the shin (a stress
fracture or inflammation of its surface): bone adapts slowest of all
tissues, and the load was doubled in a fortnight.

\textbf{8.} Recovery is incomplete from one session to the next; the
body is in a state of persistent strain, and the adaptation that
lowered the rate is being reversed.

\textbf{9.} A week of near rest and sleep (adaptation is built during
rest); then two weeks at half the previous distance, no speed work,
rebuilding by a tenth a week; the shin seen by a doctor and rested
until painless (listen to pain); drink and eat for repair.

\textbf{10.} A stimulant: it masks fatigue, the warning that protects
the heart from exhaustion and overheating.

\textbf{11.} Sam $32 \times 80 \approx \qty{2.6}{L/min}$; Alex $48
\times 64 \approx \qty{3.1}{L/min}$. Alex's is larger even in absolute
terms; and daily life --- stairs, walking, carrying oneself --- moves
the body's own mass, so the figure per kilogram is the one that
decides how it feels.

\textbf{12.} Sam $12/32 \approx 38\%$ of his maximum, Alex $12/48 =
25\%$. Sam, working above a third of his ceiling, arrives out of
breath.

\textbf{13.} Unused muscle takes little glucose from the blood and
loses its sensitivity to insulin; the same meals need more insulin
each year and the blood sugar begins to rise.

\textbf{14.} From 1.00 to 0.70: about 30\% less risk.

\textbf{15.} \omterm{def:g10:sport-and-health:training}{Physical activity} is any movement raising \omterm{def:g10:exercise-and-energy:expenditure}{energy
expenditure}: walking to work, stairs instead of the lift, housework
--- half an hour a day of it needs no sport and no equipment.

\textbf{16.} At 1\% a year, a 10\% margin is ten years of decline.

\textbf{17.} Sam's peak was lower and he has been losing bone for
twenty-five years; his wrist has crossed the threshold at which a fall
breaks it, while Alex's bone, built under load in adolescence and
maintained since, has ten years in hand.

\textbf{18.} An adaptation is a structure maintained by use, not a
permanent acquisition: it is dismantled when the demand stops and
rebuilt when it resumes.

\textbf{19.} Heart and artery disease, type 2 diabetes, osteoporosis
(also some cancers and depression). Diabetes: inactive muscle loses
its sensitivity to insulin, the blood sugar rises and the pancreas is
overworked.

\textbf{20.} Resting heart rate 54 against 78; $\dot V\!\mathrm{O_2}$max
\num{48} against \qty{32}{mL/min} per kilogram; about three hours a
week of running, kept up for fourteen years.
\end{solution}
