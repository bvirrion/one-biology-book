\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b1:gas-exchange} --- Respiratory Gas Exchange}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:1}
$\dot V = K(A/x)(P_1 - P_2)$: a larger area, a thinner barrier, a
higher outer \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:partial}{partial pressure} (ventilation) and a lower inner one
(perfusion).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:2}
Thirty times less oxygen per litre (\qty{7}{} against \qty{210}{mL/L});
eight hundred times denser and fifty times more viscous, so costly to
pump; oxygen diffuses through it ten thousand times more slowly, so
the medium must be moved right across the surface.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:3}
Co-current: water and blood both leave at \qty{12}{kPa}; extraction
$(21 - 12)/21 = \qty{43}{\%}$. \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{Counter-current}: water \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} at
\qty{4}{kPa}, blood at \qty{18}{kPa}; extraction $(21 - 4)/21 =
\qty{81}{\%}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:4}
Dissolved (\qty{7}{\%}), bound to haemoglobin's amino groups
(\qty{23}{\%}), as bicarbonate in the plasma (\qty{70}{\%}).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:5}
Minute \qty{6.75}{L/min}; alveolar $15\times 0.3 = \qty{4.5}{L/min}$;
uptake $4.5\times 0.07 = \qty{0.32}{L/min}$. Shallow: minute
\qty{6.75}{L/min} still, but alveolar $30\times 0.075 =
\qty{2.25}{L/min}$ and uptake \qty{0.16}{L/min} --- half, for the same
work of breathing; the dead space is paid at every breath.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:6}
$2/(6\times 0.75) = \qty{0.44}{L}$ per hour, twenty-two times its
volume of \qty{20}{mL}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:7}
$A/x$ falls by $2\times 4 = 8$: \qty{31}{mL/min}. To restore
\qty{250}{}, the difference must be \qty{64}{kPa} --- impossible, since
the alveolar pressure cannot exceed \qty{13}{kPa} in air and venous
blood cannot fall below zero; the patient can raise it only by
breathing oxygen, and even then not eightfold.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:8}
\omterm{prop:b1:gas-exchange:transport}{Carbonic anhydrase} is inside the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}, so bicarbonate is made there
and accumulates; it diffuses out down its gradient through an
exchanger that admits a chloride for each bicarbonate, keeping the
charge balanced. Without the exchange, bicarbonate would stay inside
with its proton's partner, raising the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell}'s osmolarity, and water
would enter: the \omterm{def:b1:cell-unit-of-life:cell}{cell} would swell in the \omterm{def:b1:body-plans-tissues:tissue}{tissues} (it swells slightly
as it is).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:9}
The urge to breathe comes from rising $\mathrm{CO_2}$. Hyperventilation
lowers $\mathrm{CO_2}$ far below normal without adding much oxygen
(haemoglobin was already saturated); during the dive oxygen falls to
the level of unconsciousness before $\mathrm{CO_2}$ has climbed back to
the level that forces a breath. Without hyperventilation
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ reaches that level while oxygen is still ample, and the
diver surfaces.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:10}
$A = \pi(10^{-5})^2 = \qty{3.1e-10}{m^2}$; $\Delta c = JL/DA =
10^{-9}\times 10^{-3}/(2\times 10^{-5}\times 3.1\times 10^{-10}) =
\qty{160}{mol/m^3}$ --- far more than the \qty{8.7}{} available, so a
tube this narrow cannot supply the muscle by diffusion at \qty{1}{mm};
a wider tube (\qty{100}{\micro m}: $\Delta c = \qty{6.4}{mol/m^3}$, just
possible) or active ventilation is needed. At \qty{10}{mm} the
requirement is tenfold again. Since the tube length grows with body
size and the demand with volume, diffusion through tracheae caps the
size of insects at a few centimetres, more only with pumping.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:11}
Fish: water in one direction, blood opposed (\omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current}),
extraction \qty{80}{\%}. Birds: air in one direction through
parabronchi, blood at right angles (cross-current), extraction
intermediate but higher than mammals', with the \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface}
never receiving stale air. A tidal \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung} mixes fresh air with the
residual gas, so the \omterm{prop:b1:organism-environment:surfaces}{exchange surface} sees at best the alveolar
mixture, and the blood can only equilibrate with that: whatever the
surface, arterial blood cannot exceed the alveolar pressure, and the
alveolar pressure cannot approach the inspired one while a residual
volume remains.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:gas-exchange:12}
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ is the better signal because its arterial level
responds to ventilation directly and steeply (halve ventilation and it
doubles), it is measured with precision through pH by the central
receptors, and oxygen, sitting on the flat top of haemoglobin's
curve, changes little over the normal range of ventilation: a
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ thermostat keeps oxygen right as a by-product. It fails
when oxygen falls independently of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ --- at altitude,
where the inspired oxygen is low but $\mathrm{CO_2}$ production is not,
so the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ signal says ``breathe less'' while oxygen says
``breathe more''; only the carotid bodies, when oxygen is severely low,
override it, and the compromise (hyperventilation with alkalosis) takes
days of renal adjustment to settle.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b1:gas-exchange:1}
\textbf{1.} Air $210/22.4 = \qty{9.4}{mmol/L}$; water $7/22.4 =
\qty{0.31}{mmol/L}$.
\textbf{2.} 30.
\textbf{3.} Air: $1.2/9.4 = \qty{0.13}{g}$; water: $1000/0.31 =
\qty{3200}{g}$ --- \num{25000} times more mass.
\textbf{4.} $210/7 = \qty{30}{L}$ of water.
\textbf{5.} Water is too heavy and too poor in oxygen to be pushed
in and out; it must be passed through once, which also makes the
\omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current} possible; air is cheap enough to move twice.
\textbf{6.} Minute $12\times 0.5 = \qty{6}{L/min}$; alveolar
$12\times 0.35 = \qty{4.2}{L/min}$.
\textbf{7.} $150/500 = \qty{30}{\%}$ of each breath, and of the minute ventilation.
\textbf{8.} Delivered $4.2\times 0.21 = \qty{0.88}{L/min}$; uptake
$4.2\times 0.07 = \qty{0.29}{L/min}$, close to \qty{250}{mL/min}.
\textbf{9.} $0.25/(6\times 0.21) = \qty{20}{\%}$ (\qty{28}{\%} of what
reaches the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{alveoli}).
\textbf{10.} $0.14\times(101 - 6.3) = \qty{13.3}{kPa}$.
\textbf{11.} $250/5 = \qty{50}{mL/L}$; venous \qty{150}{mL/L},
\qty{75}{\%} saturated.
\textbf{12.} $3000/20 = \qty{150}{mL/L}$; venous \qty{50}{mL/L},
\qty{25}{\%} saturated.
\textbf{13.} $50/60 = \qty{0.83}{mL/min}$.
\textbf{14.} $0.83/(7\times 0.8) = \qty{0.149}{L/min}$, \qty{8.9}{L}
per hour.
\textbf{15.} $0.83/(7\times 0.43) = \qty{0.28}{L/min}$: the
\omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current} halves the water to be pumped, and the work.
\textbf{16.} \qty{8.9}{kg} of water per hour; the human moves $6\times
60\times 1.2 = \qty{430}{g}$ of air per hour --- a twentieth of the
mass, for three hundred times the oxygen ($15\,000$ against
\qty{50}{mL} an hour).
\textbf{17.} $100\times(0.95 - 0.15) = \qty{80}{mL/L}$; cardiac output
$0.83/0.080 = \qty{10.4}{mL/min}$.
\textbf{18.} Water/blood $149/10.4 = 14$; air/blood $4.2/5 = 0.84$.
The fish must pass fourteen volumes of water per volume of blood
because each holds so little oxygen; the human, about one.
\textbf{19.} Uptake $\times 1.5$ from water holding $5.5/7 = 0.79$ as
much: ventilation $\times 1.9$; the cost of breathing, roughly
proportional to the flow (or more, since it rises faster than
linearly), goes from \qty{10}{\%} to at least \qty{13}{\%} of a larger
uptake --- the fish works harder to breathe in warm water.
\textbf{20.} Water inlet end: $21 - 18 = \qty{3}{kPa}$; water outlet
end: $4 - 3 = \qty{1}{kPa}$: positive at both ends.
\textbf{21.} Inlet end $21 - 3 = \qty{18}{kPa}$; outlet end $12 - 12 =
0$: the two fluids have equilibrated and no gradient is left, though
the water still holds \qty{57}{\%} of its oxygen.
\textbf{22.} The co-current: the blood equilibrates with a single,
well-mixed alveolar gas and \omterm{def:b1:flowering-plant-organization:organs}{leaves} at its pressure; it cannot exceed
\qty{13.3}{kPa} because there is no fresher gas downstream to meet, as
there is in the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current}.
\textbf{23.} Trout $2000/(2\times 10^{-4}) = \num{1e7}$ (cm$^2$ per cm);
human $10^6/(5\times 10^{-5}) = \num{2e10}$: ratio \num{2000}. Uptakes
$250/0.83 = 300$. The trout takes seven times more oxygen per unit of
$A/x$: its mean pressure difference across the lamella must be larger
--- which the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current} provides, keeping a gradient along the
whole surface where the tidal \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung}'s is small near the end of
equilibration.
\textbf{24.} $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is twenty-five times more soluble in
water than oxygen: the water carries it away as fast as it is
produced, and a fish's blood $\mathrm{CO_2}$ stays very low.
\textbf{25.} \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{Gill} about \qty{80}{\%}, \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:lung}{lung} about \qty{20}{\%} of the
oxygen in the inspired medium; the \omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{gill}'s one-way flow and its
\omterm{def:b1:gas-exchange:gill}{counter-current} arrangement of water and blood make the difference.
\end{solution}
