\chapter{Neural Plasticity, Learning and Memory}\label{ch:b3:learning-memory}

In 1953 a surgeon removed the inner part of both temporal lobes of a
young man with intractable epilepsy. The seizures abated; the patient,
known for fifty years as H.M., never formed another lasting memory of
an event. He could hold a conversation, learn to trace a star in a
mirror as well as anyone --- and each day deny ever having seen the
task --- and he remembered his childhood. Memory, it turned out, was not
one thing, and the making of new memories of events depended on a
structure the size of a thumb. What changes in a brain when it learns
is the oldest question of neuroscience, and its answer, worked out in
a sea slug with twenty thousand neurons and in slices of rat
\omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus}, is that synapses change their strength according to what
passes through them --- a rule Donald Hebb guessed in 1949 and a
molecule, the \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{NMDA receptor}, turned out to implement. This chapter
treats that rule and its machinery, the circuits that use it to store
places and events, the mathematics that says what such a system can
learn and how much it can hold, and the reward signal that tells it
what is worth learning.

\section{Kinds of memory and where they live}

\begin{definition}[Forms of learning and memory]\label{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}
\index{habituation}\index{sensitisation}\index{conditioning}\index{declarative memory}\index{procedural memory}\index{consolidation}\index{hippocampus}
The simplest learning is \emph{non-associative}: \emph{habituation},
a declining response to a repeated harmless stimulus, and
\emph{sensitisation}, an enhanced response after a harmful one.
\emph{Associative} learning links two events: in \emph{classical
conditioning} (Pavlov) a neutral stimulus that predicts a reward or
punishment comes to elicit the response; in \emph{operant
conditioning} an action that is rewarded is repeated. Human memory
divides into \emph{declarative} memory --- facts and events, consciously
recalled, dependent on the \emph{hippocampus} and the medial temporal
lobe --- and \emph{non-declarative} memory --- skills and habits
(striatum, \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:plan}{cerebellum}), conditioned fear (amygdala), priming (cortex)
--- expressed in performance without recollection. A memory is held
first in a labile short-term form, lasting seconds to hours, and is
\emph{consolidated} over hours to years into a stable long-term form
that no longer needs the hippocampus and resides in the cortex.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Scoville and Milner (1957) reported H.M.: after removal of both
hippocampi and the neighbouring cortex, his intelligence and his
memories from before the operation were largely intact, his working
memory normal for as long as he attended to something, but he could
form no new memory of any event or fact. He learned motor skills at a
normal rate over days while denying having practised --- so skill
memory did not need the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus} --- and he remembered the remote
past better than the years just before surgery, so the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus} was
needed to lay down and for a time to hold declarative memories, not to
store them for ever. Monkeys and rats with matching lesions showed the
same dissociation, and the study of a single patient reorganised the
field.
\end{proof}

\section{Hebb's rule and its molecule}

\begin{definition}[Hebbian plasticity, LTP and LTD]\label{def:b3:learning-memory:ltp}
\index{Hebb's rule}\index{long-term potentiation}\index{long-term depression}\index{spike-timing-dependent plasticity}
Hebb (1949) proposed that when a presynaptic neuron repeatedly takes
part in firing a postsynaptic one, the connection between them is
strengthened --- ``cells that fire together wire together''.
\emph{Long-term potentiation} (LTP) is its experimental form: a brief
burst of high-frequency stimulation of a pathway leaves the synapses
it activated stronger for hours in a slice and for weeks in an
animal. LTP is \emph{input-specific} (only the stimulated synapses
change), \emph{associative} (a weak input paired with a strong one to
the same cell is potentiated) and needs \emph{\omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:allostery}{cooperativity}} among
inputs to depolarise the cell. \emph{Long-term depression} (LTD) is its
opposite, produced by prolonged low-frequency activity. In
\emph{spike-timing-dependent plasticity} the sign is set by order: a
presynaptic spike a few milliseconds \emph{before} the postsynaptic
one strengthens the synapse, one just \emph{after} weakens it, within
a window of about \qty{20}{ms} on either side --- so that a synapse
learns to predict, not merely to correlate.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The NMDA receptor as coincidence detector]\label{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}
\index{NMDA receptor}\index{AMPA receptor}\index{CaMKII}\index{CREB}\index{dendritic spine}
At excitatory synapses glutamate acts on two receptors. The
\emph{AMPA} receptor opens on binding and carries the ordinary
synaptic current. The \emph{NMDA} receptor binds glutamate but is
blocked at resting potential by a magnesium ion in its pore; only when
the postsynaptic membrane is depolarised --- by many inputs at once, or
by a back-propagating action potential --- is the magnesium expelled
and the channel conducts, letting in calcium. The \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{NMDA receptor} thus
opens only when presynaptic release (glutamate) coincides with
postsynaptic activity (depolarisation): it is Hebb's ``and'' gate in a
single protein. The calcium that enters a \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{dendritic spine}}
activates the kinase \emph{CaMKII}, which phosphorylates \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{AMPA receptors}
and drives more of them into the synapse from a reserve --- the synapse
is stronger within minutes (early LTP). A large, slow calcium rise
instead activates the phosphatase calcineurin, which removes \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{AMPA
receptors}: LTD. Potentiation lasting more than a few hours (late LTP)
needs new protein: kinases reach the nucleus, the transcription factor
\emph{CREB} switches on genes, and the spine grows and may split ---
the same requirement for gene expression that separates short-term
from long-term memory in every animal studied.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Bliss and L\o mo (1973) stimulated the perforant path into the
rabbit \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus} with a train of pulses and found the evoked
response enlarged for hours afterwards --- the first LTP. Collingridge
(1983) showed that a blocker of the \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{NMDA receptor} (AP5) prevented the
induction of LTP but not the ordinary synaptic response; Morris (1986)
infused AP5 into the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus} of rats and found that they could no
longer learn the position of a hidden platform in a pool of water while
swimming and seeing normally; and mice in which the \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{NMDA receptor} was
deleted only in the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{CA1} region of the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus} lacked both LTP there
and spatial memory. The same receptor, in the same place, was necessary
for the synaptic change and the learning.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[What a Hebbian synapse learns]\label{thm:b3:learning-memory:hebb}
\index{Hebbian learning!principal component}\index{Oja's rule}
Let a neuron receive inputs $\mathbf{x} = (x_{1},\dots,x_{n})$ through
weights $\mathbf{w}$ and respond linearly, $y = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}$.
\omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:ltp}{Hebb's rule}, $\Delta\mathbf{w} = \eta\, y\,\mathbf{x}$, gives on average
over the inputs
\[
\langle\Delta\mathbf{w}\rangle = \eta\,C\,\mathbf{w}, \qquad C =
\langle\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle ,
\]
where $C$ is the correlation matrix of the inputs. The weights grow
without bound, and grow fastest along the eigenvector of $C$ with the
largest eigenvalue: the synapse comes to detect the pattern of input
that is most often present --- the \emph{principal component} of its
inputs. With a term that penalises large weights, \emph{Oja's rule}
$\Delta\mathbf{w} = \eta\, y\,(\mathbf{x} - y\,\mathbf{w})$, the weight
vector converges to that eigenvector normalised to unit length, and the
neuron becomes a stable detector of the dominant correlation in what it
sees.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Substituting $y = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
\mathbf{w}$ into \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:ltp}{Hebb's rule} gives $\Delta\mathbf{w} = \eta\,\mathbf{x}
\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}$, whose average over inputs (with
$\mathbf{w}$ changing slowly) is $\eta C\mathbf{w}$. $C$ is symmetric and
positive semi-definite, so it has orthogonal eigenvectors $\mathbf{e}_{k}$
with eigenvalues $\lambda_{k} \ge 0$; writing $\mathbf{w} = \sum_{k}
a_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}$, each component obeys $\Delta a_{k} = \eta\lambda_{k}
a_{k}$ and grows as $(1 + \eta\lambda_{k})^{t}$, so the component along
the largest eigenvalue comes to dominate the direction of $\mathbf{w}$
while its length diverges. Oja's rule averages to $\eta(C\mathbf{w} -
(\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}C\mathbf{w})\mathbf{w})$; at a fixed point
$C\mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}C\mathbf{w})\mathbf{w}$, so
$\mathbf{w}$ is an eigenvector with $\lambda = \mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}
C\mathbf{w} = \lambda|\mathbf{w}|^{2}$, hence $|\mathbf{w}| = 1$; the
fixed point along the largest eigenvalue is the stable one (a
perturbation toward another eigenvector shrinks, since that
eigenvector's eigenvalue is smaller), which is admitted here.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Two inputs]\label{ex:b3:learning-memory:two-inputs}
A neuron receives two inputs that are usually active together, so $C =
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.8 \\ 0.8 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. The eigenvectors are
$(1,1)/\sqrt{2}$ with eigenvalue $1.8$ and $(1,-1)/\sqrt{2}$ with
eigenvalue $0.2$. Starting from $\mathbf{w} = (1, 0)$, \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:ltp}{Hebb's rule} after
many small steps turns $\mathbf{w}$ toward $(1,1)$: the neuron learns to
respond to the two inputs \emph{together}, and to ignore the rare
occasions on which only one fires --- it has extracted the correlation.
With Oja's rule the weights settle at $(0.71, 0.71)$. This is the sense
in which Hebbian synapses learn what goes together, and it is why the
associative property of LTP, pairing a weak input with a strong one,
follows from the rule.
\end{example}

\section{A slug that learns}

\begin{proposition}[Sensitisation in \emph{Aplysia}]\label{prop:b3:learning-memory:aplysia}
\index{Aplysia}\index{presynaptic facilitation}\index{cAMP}\index{PKA}
The sea slug \emph{Aplysia} withdraws its gill when its siphon is
touched, a reflex of a few dozen identifiable neurons. A shock to the
tail \emph{sensitises} it: the withdrawal to a light touch is enhanced,
for minutes after one shock and for weeks after several. Kandel and
colleagues (1970s--1990s) traced the change to the synapse between the
siphon's sensory neuron and the gill's motor neuron. The tail shock
excites an \omterm{def:b3:nervous-systems:cells}{interneuron} that releases serotonin onto the sensory
neuron's terminal; serotonin raises cyclic AMP, which activates
protein kinase A, which phosphorylates and closes a potassium channel;
the terminal's action potentials broaden, more calcium enters, and more
transmitter is released per spike --- \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:aplysia}{presynaptic facilitation}},
a covalent change lasting minutes. Repeated shocks send the kinase to
the nucleus, where it activates CREB, which switches on genes that
build new synaptic terminals: the long-term memory is more synapses,
and it is blocked by inhibitors of protein synthesis given during
training but not afterwards. \emph{\omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{Habituation}} is the opposite,
a depression of the same synapse; and \emph{classical \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{conditioning}},
pairing the touch with the shock, enhances facilitation at the
synapses that were active just before the serotonin arrived --- a
Hebbian coincidence implemented presynaptically, by an adenylyl
cyclase that is stimulated by calcium and serotonin together.
\end{proposition}

\section{Circuits for places and events}

\begin{definition}[The hippocampal circuit]\label{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}
\index{dentate gyrus}\index{CA3}\index{CA1}\index{place cell}\index{grid cell}\index{pattern completion}\index{replay}
Cortical input reaches the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus} through the entorhinal cortex
and passes through three stages: the \emph{dentate gyrus}, whose
granule cells outnumber their inputs and fire sparsely (separating
similar patterns); \emph{CA3}, whose pyramidal cells connect
recurrently to one another, each receiving thousands of synapses from
others --- an \emph{autoassociative} network that can complete a stored
pattern from a fragment; and \emph{CA1}, which compares the CA3 output
with the direct input and returns the result to the cortex. In a rat
exploring an arena, a hippocampal pyramidal cell fires only when the
animal is in one place --- a \emph{place cell} (O'Keefe, 1971) --- and a
few hundred of them map the arena; in the entorhinal cortex upstream,
\emph{grid cells} (Moser, 2005) fire at the vertices of a hexagonal
lattice covering the space, a coordinate system from which place fields
are built. During sleep and rest the sequences of place cells that
fired during a run are \emph{replayed} at high speed, and blocking the
replay impairs the memory of the run: the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus} rehearses the
day's paths to the cortex.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[How much an autoassociative network holds]\label{prop:b3:learning-memory:capacity}
\index{Hopfield network}\index{memory capacity}
A network of $N$ neurons, each connected to all the others with
Hebbian weights $w_{ij} \propto \sum_{\mu}\xi_{i}^{\mu}\xi_{j}^{\mu}$
summed over the stored binary patterns $\xi^{\mu}$, retrieves a stored
pattern from a corrupted or partial cue by settling into it as an
attractor (Hopfield, 1982). It does so reliably for up to about $P
\approx 0.14N$ random patterns; beyond that the patterns interfere and
retrieval collapses. A rat's \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{CA3}, with some $3\times 10^{5}$ pyramidal
cells, could on this estimate hold some $4\times 10^{4}$ patterns ---
the order of the distinct places or episodes an animal meets in a
season --- and its sparse firing (a few per cent of cells active in any
pattern) raises the capacity further. \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{Pattern completion} from a cue is
what lets a smell or a corner recall a whole scene; the price is that
similar memories merge, which the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{dentate gyrus}'s separation resists.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Testing memory and its mechanism]\label{met:b3:learning-memory:tests}
\index{Morris water maze}\index{fear conditioning}\index{engram}
(1) A spatial task: the \omterm{met:b3:learning-memory:tests}{Morris water maze} --- a rat swims in opaque
water to a platform hidden at a fixed place, learns its position from
the room's cues over days, and is tested by the time spent searching
the right quadrant when the platform is removed; hippocampal lesions,
NMDA blockers and the loss of LTP each abolish the learning. (2) An
associative task: \omterm{met:b3:learning-memory:tests}{fear conditioning} --- a tone paired with a foot shock
comes to elicit freezing; the memory depends on the amygdala, and a
context-specific version on the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus}. (3) A mechanism test:
block the candidate (a drug, a \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:transgenic}{knockout} restricted to one region and
time) and show that learning fails while perception and movement do
not. (4) The engram: label the neurons active during learning with a
light-sensitive channel (\cref{ch:b3:nervous-systems}) and later
reactivate them with light --- the animal freezes in a safe context, as
if remembering; silencing them blocks recall. Memory is in identifiable
cells and their synapses, and can be switched on and off.
\end{method}

\section{Reward, prediction and the plasticity of a lifetime}

\begin{proposition}[Learning by prediction error]\label{prop:b3:learning-memory:rescorla}
\index{Rescorla--Wagner rule}\index{prediction error}\index{dopamine}
Let $V$ be the value an animal attaches to a cue and $\lambda$ the
reward that follows it. The Rescorla--Wagner rule (1972) says that on
each trial the value changes by a fraction $\alpha$ of the
\emph{\omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:rescorla}{prediction error}}:
\[
\Delta V = \alpha\,(\lambda - V), \qquad
V_{n} = \lambda\bigl(1 - (1-\alpha)^{n}\bigr) ,
\]
so learning is fast at first and slows as the prediction improves,
stops when the reward is fully predicted, and reverses (extinction)
when the reward is withheld. When two cues are presented together they
share one prediction, so a cue that adds nothing to a prediction
already made is not learned (\emph{blocking}). Schultz (1997) recorded
dopamine neurons of the midbrain in monkeys and found that they fire
to an unexpected reward, fall silent to a fully predicted one, and dip
below baseline when a predicted reward fails to arrive: they broadcast
$\lambda - V$, the \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:rescorla}{prediction error} itself, to the striatum and cortex,
where dopamine gates plasticity at the synapses that were recently
active. Learning what predicts reward is Hebbian plasticity with a
third factor that says whether the outcome was better or worse than
expected --- and drugs of abuse, by driving dopamine directly,
counterfeit a permanent ``better than expected''.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The recursion $V_{n+1} = V_{n} + \alpha(\lambda - V_{n})$ gives
$\lambda - V_{n+1} = (1-\alpha)(\lambda - V_{n})$, so the error shrinks
geometrically, $\lambda - V_{n} = (1-\alpha)^{n}\lambda$ from $V_{0} =
0$, whence the formula. For two cues $A$ and $B$ presented together
the prediction is $V_{A} + V_{B}$; if $A$ was trained first to
$V_{A} = \lambda$, the error on compound trials is zero and $B$
acquires nothing.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Critical periods and the diseases of plasticity]\label{def:b3:learning-memory:critical}
\index{critical period}\index{ocular dominance}\index{addiction}\index{Alzheimer's disease}
Plasticity is greatest in windows of development. Hubel and Wiesel
(1960s) covered one eye of a kitten for a few weeks after birth: the
visual cortex, normally driven by both eyes in alternating columns,
became driven almost entirely by the open eye, and the deprived eye
stayed functionally blind for life --- while the same deprivation in an
adult cat changed nothing. Such \emph{critical periods}, for vision,
for language, for the songs of birds, close when inhibitory circuits
mature and the extracellular matrix stiffens, and they can be reopened
a little by drugs and training. Plasticity's pathologies are of dose
and target: \emph{addiction} is learning driven by a counterfeit
\omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:rescorla}{prediction error}, which builds habits that outlast the pleasure;
post-traumatic stress is a fear memory over-consolidated by stress
hormones; and \emph{Alzheimer's disease} begins where \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{declarative memory}
begins, in the entorhinal cortex and \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus}, with the loss of
synapses --- which tracks the dementia better than any plaque count ---
before the loss of neurons.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[What memory is made of]\label{rem:b3:learning-memory:what}
A memory is not a recording but a change in the strengths and numbers
of synapses, distributed over the cells that were active when it was
made, laid down by a rule that strengthens what fires together, gated
by a signal that says whether the outcome mattered, and rehearsed in
sleep until the cortex holds it on its own. The rule is Hebb's, the
gate is the \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{NMDA receptor}, the signal is dopamine, the rehearsal is
\omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{replay}, and the mathematics says what such a system can hold and why
it confuses the similar. Every experiment that has switched a memory
on or off --- by a blocker, a gene, a light --- has found it in those
synapses, in those cells. H.M. died in 2008; his brain, sectioned and
imaged, showed the lesion that had taught the field, and no more.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: A Synapse That Remembers}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a memory followed from calcium in a
spine to a rat in a maze: the calcium arithmetic of induction, a
Hebbian neuron converging on what its inputs share, a learning curve
timed by prediction error, and the capacity of a hippocampus, ending
on the calcium concentration that induces LTP, the trials a rat needs
and the patterns its CA3 can hold}]
\label{pb:b3:learning-memory:1}
Data: a spine of volume \qty{0.08}{fL}; each NMDA channel carries
$10^{3}$ Ca$^{2+}$ per opening of \qty{50}{ms}; a synapse has $10$ \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{NMDA
receptors}; \qty{98}{\%} of entering calcium is buffered; resting free
calcium \qty{0.1}{\micro mol/L}; CaMKII is activated above
\qty{2}{\micro mol/L} free calcium, calcineurin between $0.3$ and
\qty{1}{\micro mol/L}. Hebb: two inputs with $C = \begin{pmatrix}1 &
0.6\\ 0.6 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $\eta = 0.1$. Rescorla--Wagner: $\alpha =
0.15$. Capacity: rat \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{CA3} $3\times 10^{5}$ cells, $P = 0.14N$; a rat
explores $20$ new places a day.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Calcium.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item All ten \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{NMDA receptors} open once. How many calcium ions enter,
        how many stay free, and what concentration rise is that in the
        spine?
  \item Is CaMKII activated? What if only two receptors open
        (glutamate released but the spine barely depolarised)?
  \item With two receptors open, which enzyme is in its range, and
        what happens to the synapse? Explain how one ion can produce
        both LTP and LTD.
  \item Calcium is pumped out with a time constant of \qty{20}{ms}.
        Why must inputs arrive within tens of milliseconds to sum
        their calcium, and how does this set the spike-timing window?
  \item A spine is \qty{0.5}{\micro m} from its neighbour on the
        dendrite. Explain why buffering and the spine's narrow neck
        keep the calcium signal in one spine, and why that matters for
        input specificity.
  \item The magnesium block is relieved at about \qty{-30}{mV}. A
        single AMPA synapse depolarises the spine by \qty{5}{mV} from
        \qty{-70}{mV}. How many synchronous inputs (or what else) are
        needed to unblock \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:nmda}{NMDA receptors}? What does this say about
        \omterm{def:b3:structural-biology:allostery}{cooperativity}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- A Hebbian neuron.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $C$.
  \item Starting from $\mathbf{w} = (1, 0)$, apply the averaged Hebb
        rule $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta C\mathbf{w}$ for
        three steps. What is the angle of $\mathbf{w}$ to the $(1,1)$
        direction after each?
  \item After many steps, what is the ratio $w_{2}/w_{1}$, and what
        happens to $|\mathbf{w}|$?
  \item Apply Oja's averaged rule $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} +
        \eta\,(C\mathbf{w} - (\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}C\mathbf{w})
        \mathbf{w})$ for one step from $(0.8, 0.8)$. Toward what does
        it move?
  \item The neuron now responds to the two inputs together. If input 2
        alone fires (rare), what is $y$ relative to the joint case,
        with $\mathbf{w} = (0.71, 0.71)$?
  \item Explain in one sentence how the associativity of LTP (a weak
        input paired with a strong one is strengthened) is this
        theorem in action.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Learning curve.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item With $\alpha = 0.15$, compute $V_{n}/\lambda$ after $5$, $10$
        and $20$ trials.
  \item How many trials to reach \qty{95}{\%} of $\lambda$?
  \item A rat learns a maze in about this many trials. If the reward is
        doubled, does the model predict faster learning in trials? What
        changes?
  \item Cue A is trained to asymptote, then A and B are presented
        together with the same reward for $20$ trials. What is $V_{B}$?
        What do the dopamine neurons do on the compound trials?
  \item The reward now follows B alone. Describe the first trial's
        \omterm{prop:b3:learning-memory:rescorla}{prediction error} and dopamine response, and the course of
        $V_{B}$.
  \item A drug raises dopamine regardless of outcome. Using the rule,
        explain why the actions preceding the drug are learned as if
        rewarded, whatever their consequences.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus}.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Hopfield capacity of the rat's \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{CA3}.
  \item At $20$ new places a day, how long before the capacity is
        reached? What must happen for the animal to go on learning?
  \item \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{Consolidation} transfers memories to the cortex over weeks.
        Explain why a slow transfer protects old cortical memories
        (catastrophic interference) and why the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:kinds}{hippocampus} is a
        temporary store.
  \item If \qty{3}{\%} of \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{CA3} cells are active in any pattern, how many
        cells encode one place, and how many patterns could be
        distinguished if patterns were required to share fewer than
        half their cells? (Argue qualitatively why sparse coding raises
        capacity.)
  \item \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{Replay} during sleep compresses a \qty{10}{s} run into
        \qty{0.5}{s}. If the spike-timing window is \qty{20}{ms}, what
        separation between \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{place cells}' firing in the run becomes
        Hebbian in the \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{replay}? Why might this be the point of
        compression?
  \item The maze memory survives a hippocampal lesion made a month
        after training but not one made a day after. Interpret, with
        H.M.'s pattern of loss.
  \item Summarise: the free calcium concentration after ten NMDA
        openings (question~1), the trials to \qty{95}{\%} learning
        (question~14), and the rat's \omterm{def:b3:learning-memory:hippocampus}{CA3} capacity (question~19).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
