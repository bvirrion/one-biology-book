\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:1}
\omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-positive}: cytoplasmic membrane wrapped in a thick, many-layered
\omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{peptidoglycan} wall threaded with teichoic acids. \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negative}: a thin
\omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{peptidoglycan} layer in a periplasm, then an \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{outer membrane} whose outer
leaflet is \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{lipopolysaccharide}, with porins. The crystal violet--iodine
complex is trapped in the thick, alcohol-dehydrated wall of the
\omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-positive} (purple) but washed out of the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{Gram-negative} through its
thin wall and disrupted \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{outer membrane}, which then takes the pink
counter-stain.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:3}
\omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:hgt}{Transformation} --- Griffith (1928) and Avery, MacLeod and McCarty
(1944): heat-killed virulent pneumococci transform live avirulent
ones, and the agent is DNA. \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:hgt}{Transduction} --- Zinder and Lederberg
(1952): phage P22 carries \emph{Salmonella} genes from one strain to
another through a filter that stops cells. \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:hgt}{Conjugation} --- Lederberg
and Tatum (1946): two auxotrophic \emph{E.~coli} strains give
prototrophic recombinants only when mixed, requiring cell contact
(Davis's U-tube).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:4}
Penicillin: the transpeptidases that cross-link \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:envelope}{peptidoglycan}, which
animal cells do not have. Streptomycin: the small ribosomal subunit,
whose bacterial form differs from the eukaryotic. Ciprofloxacin: DNA
gyrase, a bacterial topoisomerase absent from humans. Rifampicin: the
$\beta$ subunit of bacterial RNA polymerase, which the eukaryotic
enzyme does not share.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:5}
$S^{*} = K_{S}D/(\mu_{\max} - D)$, $X^{*} = Y(S_{0} - S^{*})$. $D = 0.2$:
$S^{*} = \qty{0.0067}{g/L}$, $X^{*} = \qty{0.80}{g/L}$. $D = 0.6$:
$S^{*} = 0.06$, $X^{*} = 0.78$. $D = 0.79$: $S^{*} = 1.58$, $X^{*} = 0.17$.
Washout when $D = \mu(S_{0}) = 0.8\times 2/2.02 = \qty{0.79}{h^{-1}}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:6}
$(N/V)_{\text{thr}} = kA_{\text{thr}}/p$. Light organ: $0.01\times
6\times 10^{12}/100 = 6\times 10^{8}$ per litre $= 6\times 10^{5}$ per
mL. Sea water: $10\times 6\times 10^{12}/100 = 6\times 10^{11}$ per litre
$= 6\times 10^{8}$ per mL --- a thousand times higher, never reached in
the sea.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:7}
\qty{25}{mm}: sensitive, low MIC. \qty{12}{mm}: reduced susceptibility,
intermediate --- may fail at ordinary doses. \qty{0}{mm}: resistant,
growth up to the disc. The drug diffuses from the disc so that its
concentration falls with distance in a reproducible way; growth stops
where the concentration equals the MIC, so zone radius and MIC are
related by a calibration curve established with strains of known MIC.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:8}
The conjugative \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid} carries an integron whose cassette array holds
several resistance genes, plus transposons carrying more; the \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid}
crosses in one mating and expresses them all. They cluster because
selection for any one of the drugs maintains the whole \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid}, so
genes that hitch-hike on it persist (co-selection); integrons capture
cassettes; transposons hop onto \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmids}; and the \omterm{def:b3:genetic-engineering:tools}{plasmid}, not the
gene, is the unit of transfer.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:9}
Drug alone: $10^{-7}\times 10^{9} = 100$ resistant cells expected. Two
independent drugs: $10^{-14}\times 10^{9} = 10^{-5}$. Persisters are
not mutants: a small fraction of dormant cells survives any drug and
regrows into a sensitive population when the drug is gone, so a second
drug does not help; only a treatment long enough to catch them as they
wake, or a drug active on dormant cells, does.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:10}
$X > X^{*}$: consumption $\mu X/Y$ exceeds supply, so $S$ falls below
$S^{*}$; then $\mu(S) < D$ and $\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t < 0$, $X$ falls
back; as $X$ falls, $S$ rises again. Both deviations are corrected: the
steady state is stable. Two species: each needs $\mu_{i}(S) = D$ to
persist, which fixes its own $S_{i}^{*}$; the nutrient can take only
one value, so at most one species persists. The one with the lower
$S^{*}(D)$ drives $S$ below what the other needs, and the other is
washed out; which species that is can change with $D$ if their Monod
curves cross.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:11}
With density $\rho = N/V$, the off state $A = p_{0}\rho/k$ exists while
it stays below $A_{\text{thr}}$, i.e. $\rho < kA_{\text{thr}}/p_{0}$;
the on state $A = p_{1}\rho/k$ exists while it stays above, i.e. $\rho
> kA_{\text{thr}}/p_{1}$. For $kA_{\text{thr}}/p_{1} < \rho <
kA_{\text{thr}}/p_{0}$ both are self-consistent: a population that has
switched on keeps itself on with its higher output, one that has not
stays off. Use: commitment --- once a colony has decided to build a
biofilm or release toxin, a dip in density does not undo the decision,
and the switch is sharp and synchronous rather than graded.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:bacteriology:12}
(a) High doses for short courses: the level stays above the mutant MIC
and kills wild type and first-step mutants alike --- favoured, limited
by toxicity. (b) Low doses for long courses: the level sits in the
window for days and breeds resistance --- against. (c) Stopping when
symptoms resolve: the survivors are the least sensitive cells and the
falling level passes through the window --- against. (d) Combination:
a cell must be resistant to both, which a billion cells do not contain
--- for.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:bacteriology:1}
\textbf{1.} $18$ generations: $2^{18} = 2.6\times 10^{5}$ cells,
$2.6\times 10^{3}$ per mL.
\textbf{2.} $2\times 10^{9}\times 100 = 2\times 10^{11}$ cells $=
2^{37.5}$: after \qty{12.5}{h}; mass \qty{0.2}{g}.
\textbf{3.} $6\times 10^{27}/10^{-12} = 6\times 10^{39} = 2^{132}$ cells:
$132$ generations, \qty{44}{h}. Nutrients, oxygen and waste stop it
within a day; exponential growth is always brief.
\textbf{4.} Stationary cells have dismantled ribosomes and repressed
their metabolic enzymes; they must rebuild them before dividing.
Exponential cells arrive fully equipped.
\textbf{5.} $\mu = \ln 2/\qty{0.333}{h} = \qty{2.08}{h^{-1}}$; at $T =
\qty{60}{min}$, \qty{0.69}{h^{-1}}; after \qty{6}{h} $2^{6} = 64$
instead of $2^{18}$: a factor $2^{12} \approx 4000$ fewer.
\textbf{6.} $200/(0.1\times 10^{-6}) = 2\times 10^{9}$ viable cells per
mL. The microscope also counts dead cells and cells that will not grow
on the plate, and a clump gives one colony.
\textbf{7.} $D = 0.5/1 = \qty{0.5}{h^{-1}}$; $T = \ln 2/0.5 =
\qty{1.39}{h} = \qty{83}{min}$.
\textbf{8.} $S^{*} = 0.01\times 0.5/0.5 = \qty{0.01}{g/L}$; $X^{*} =
0.5\times 1.99 \approx \qty{1}{g/L} = 10^{12}$ cells per litre, $10^{9}$
per mL.
\textbf{9.} $0.5\times 10^{12} = 5\times 10^{11}$ cells per hour; $720/1.39
\approx 520$ generations a month.
\textbf{10.} $D = 0.95$: $S^{*} = 0.01\times 0.95/0.05 = \qty{0.19}{g/L}$,
$X^{*} = 0.5\times 1.81 = \qty{0.9}{g/L}$. At $F = \qty{1.1}{L/h}$, $D
> \mu_{\max}$: washout.
\textbf{11.} The mutant holds $S^{*} = 0.01\times 0.5/0.7 = \qty{0.0071}{g/L}$;
at that glucose the original grows at $1.0\times 0.0071/0.0171 =
\qty{0.42}{h^{-1}} < D$ and declines as $e^{-0.08t}$: washed out over a
few weeks. The mutant takes over.
\textbf{12.} $10^{-9}\times 10^{12} = 1000$ beneficial mutations per
generation: adaptation is continuous, with a new advantageous clone
sweeping every few hundred generations --- the chemostat is an
evolution machine.
\textbf{13.} $kA_{\text{thr}}/p = 0.05\times 6\times 10^{12}/500 =
6\times 10^{8}$ per litre $= 6\times 10^{5}$ cells per mL.
\textbf{14.} $10^{10}/6\times 10^{5} \approx 1.7\times 10^{4}$-fold.
\textbf{15.} $20\times 6\times 10^{12}/500 = 2.4\times 10^{11}$ per litre,
$2.4\times 10^{8}$ per mL --- six orders of magnitude above the
$10^{2}$ per mL of the sea: dark.
\textbf{16.} Tenfold is $3.3$ doublings, \qty{6.6}{h}. At $10^{9}$ per mL
the population is still $1700$ times above threshold: the light stays
on.
\textbf{17.} In the sea no one sees the light and it feeds no host: a
dark mutant saves \qty{20}{\%} and outgrows the glowers. In the organ
the squid selects for light --- it expels or fails to feed dark
cheaters --- so the cost buys a home.
\textbf{18.} Block the receptor or destroy the \omterm{def:b3:bacteriology:quorum}{autoinducer} (quorum
quenching): the bacteria live but never deploy their toxins, and the
immune system clears them. Since sensitive and ``resistant'' bacteria
grow equally --- nothing is killed --- the selective advantage of
escaping the drug is small, and resistance should spread more slowly
than to a killing drug.
\textbf{19.} A: $10^{-8}\times 10^{9} = 10$; B: $100$; both: $10^{-15}\times
10^{9} = 10^{-6}$.
\textbf{20.} $P_{0}(\text{A}) = e^{-10} = 5\times 10^{-5}$;
$P_{0}(\text{both}) = e^{-10^{-6}} = 0.999999$.
\textbf{21.} $16 \to 8$: one half-life, \qty{4}{h}; $16 \to 1$: four,
\qty{16}{h}; in the window from \qty{4}{h} to \qty{16}{h}, \qty{12}{h}
per dose.
\textbf{22.} Dosing every \qty{12}{h}: in the window from hour $4$ to
hour $12$, two thirds of the time; over ten days the first-step
mutants of A --- ten of them at the start --- are selected and A alone
fails.
\textbf{23.} Above \qty{8}{mg/L} throughout: dose every half-life
(\qty{4}{h}), or a dose peaking at \qty{64}{mg/L} every \qty{12}{h}
($64 \to 8$ in three half-lives), or a continuous infusion. Costs:
toxicity of high peaks, or six doses a day that patients will not
keep.
\textbf{24.} $10^{-4}\times 10^{9} = 10^{5}$ persisters survive whatever
the drugs; when treatment stops they wake and regrow a fully sensitive
population --- a relapse. Treatment must last long enough to catch
them as they leave dormancy, which is why tuberculosis takes six months.
\textbf{25.} $10^{9}$ cells per mL in the chemostat; the light comes on at
$6\times 10^{5}$ cells per mL; $P_{0} = 0.999999$ that the two-drug
course meets no resistant cell.
\end{solution}
