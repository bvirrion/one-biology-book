\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g11:mutations} --- Mutations and Genetic Variation}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:1}
A change in the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide} sequence of \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:information}{DNA}, transmitted to the
descendants of the \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}. Substitution, insertion, deletion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:2}
\texttt{ATGCCAGAAT}: substitution (T to A at position 6).
\texttt{ATGCCTGAT}: deletion of one A. \texttt{ATGCCTTGAAT}: insertion
of one T.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:3}
Somatic: in a body \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}, transmitted only to that \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}'s descendants
within the individual. Germline: in a gamete or its precursor,
transmitted to the offspring and all their \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. Only germline
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} enter heredity.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:4}
Ultraviolet light, X-rays, benzopyrene of tobacco smoke (also
radioactivity, aflatoxin). Ultraviolet welds two neighbouring thymines
of one strand together, distorting the helix.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:5}
The intact complementary strand: because each strand determines the
other, the correct sequence of the damaged segment can be rebuilt
from its partner.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:6}
Normal: about 6\%; repair-deficient: 0.01\%. Repair improves survival
about 600-fold at that dose.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:8}
Each replica receives \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} from the same original colonies. If the
antibiotic caused resistance at contact, the affected \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} would be
a random few on each replica, at unrelated positions. Finding
resistance always at the same positions means those original
colonies, grown without the drug, already contained resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:9}
Errors per copy of the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}: $2 \times 3000 \times 10^{-9} =
\num{6e-6}$ (both strands). Among $10^{12}$ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} produced in the last
division, about $\num{6e6}$ carry a new \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} in that \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{gene}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:10}
A somatic \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} in a bud \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} gave the branch its colour; cuttings
are that tissue, so they keep it. The flowers' germ \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} derive from
the same branch, but a change in one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is passed to only half
the gametes, and the ovules were fertilised by pollen carrying the
ordinary \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}; the new \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}, if recessive, is hidden in the
seedlings.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:11}
The missing \omterm{def:g10:cell-metabolism:metabolism}{enzyme} repairs ultraviolet damage; ultraviolet penetrates
only the skin. The gut receives no ultraviolet, suffers no such
lesions, and does not need that repair.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:12}
Lesions are proportional to the dose: a tenth of the ultraviolet means
a tenth of the lesions per \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} per hour --- still thousands. Sunburn
is the death of many \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}; below that dose \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} survive, but the
lesions that escape repair become \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations}. No burn means the damage
was survivable, not absent.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:13}
Neither in itself. In a region with malaria the single copy raises
survival, and the \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele} is kept common despite the disease of double
carriers; where malaria is absent it only causes disease and is rare.
The environment, and the number of copies, decide the value of a
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:14}
The sensitive \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} die; the resistant ones, already present, divide
freely and within a day the culture is entirely resistant. A course
stopped early leaves the last survivors --- the most resistant ---
alive to regrow, in a body now enriched in resistance; taking the full
course kills them before they multiply.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g11:mutations:15}
Most \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} are neutral (outside \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{genes} or without effect on the
\omterm{def:g10:chemistry-of-life:families}{protein}), a minority harmful, a very few useful; being random, they
occur regardless of use, but selection keeps the useful ones and
removes the harmful. Usefulness depends on the environment, so a
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} harmful today can be the one that matters when conditions
change. Over thousands of generations, with billions of individuals,
even rare useful \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} arise repeatedly: evolution is built on the
sorted remainder, not on the average \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g11:mutations:1}
\textbf{1.} So that each colony is a clone with a known origin, and so
that colonies are separate enough to be told apart on the replicas.

\textbf{2.} A few \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} from the top of every colony, in the same
geometric arrangement as on the plate, because the pad is pressed flat
without sliding.

\textbf{3.} That the resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} were present in those two
original colonies: three independent replicas do not agree by chance.

\textbf{4.} Resistant colonies at random, unrelated positions on the
three plates, since which \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} were induced would differ from replica
to replica.

\textbf{5.} \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{Mutation} before contact: the resistance maps to the
original colonies, which never met the drug.

\textbf{6.} \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{Cells} from the two resistant positions grow on
streptomycin; \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} from the other positions do not.

\textbf{7.} Only some, probably: the \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} happened at some division
during the colony's growth, and only the descendants of that \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} are
resistant. To decide, one would plate a known number of the colony's
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} on streptomycin and count survivors.

\textbf{8.} At the 20th of 23 divisions: $1/2^{20-1}$\dots more
simply, the mutant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell} then divides 3 more times: $2^3 = 8$ resistant
\omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} out of $2^{23}$, about one in a million. At the 3rd division:
one of the 8 \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} then present, so an eighth of the colony.

\textbf{9.} Even a few resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells} among the $10^4$ or so the
velvet lifts are enough to found a colony on the antibiotic plate; the
test detects the presence of resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}, not their proportion.

\textbf{10.} The argument rests on position: only if the geometry is
preserved can a colony on a replica be identified with a colony on
the original plate.

\textbf{11.} $30/\num{10000} = \num{3e-3}$.

\textbf{12.} About $\num{3e-3}$ \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} per $10^7$ divisions:
$\num{3e-10}$ per division.

\textbf{13.} Of the same order as the error rate at one \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:nucleotide}{nucleotide}:
the resistance \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} is essentially one specific substitution
escaping repair.

\textbf{14.} $10^9$ divisions at $\num{3e-10}$: on average a fraction
of one \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation}, so most cultures hold none or a few resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
(each \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} then multiplied by the divisions that follow it).

\textbf{15.} A \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} at an early division is inherited by a large
fraction of the culture, so the count is large; a late one gives only
a few resistant \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}. Because the timing is random, replicate
cultures give widely scattered counts --- itself a sign that the
\omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} arise before, not in response to, the antibiotic.

\textbf{16.} Rare: \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} occur seldom per division. Random: they
occur independently of their usefulness. The experiment establishes
randomness (the position argument); the counts of Part III measure
rarity.

\textbf{17.} It sorted the population: it killed the sensitive \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cells}
and let the resistant ones multiply --- selection.

\textbf{18.} \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{Mutation} supplied the resistant \omterm{def:g10:universal-dna:gene}{allele}, at random, before
any need; the antibiotic sorted the population, keeping only its
carriers.

\textbf{19.} The \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutation} changes a molecule involved in the drug's
action, not the colony's growth or appearance without the drug; a
genetic difference has a visible effect only in an environment where
it matters.

\textbf{20.} Identical positions on the three replicas proved that
resistance \omterm{def:g11:mutations:mutation}{mutations} arise before, and independently of, exposure to
the antibiotic; the measured rate is about $\num{3e-10}$ per \omterm{def:g10:cells-common-unit:cell}{cell}
division.
\end{solution}
