\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b2:unicellular-diversity} --- Diversity of Unicellular Organisms}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:1}
\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:unicellular}{Unicellular}: one cell performs all functions and lives alone;
\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:unicellular}{colonial}: identical cells stay attached but each could live alone;
\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:unicellular}{multicellular}: several cell types, interdependent, only the germ line
reproduces. \emph{E.~coli} and the yeast are \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:unicellular}{unicellular} (a bud
detaches and lives alone); \emph{Gonium} is \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:unicellular}{colonial} (16 identical
cells, each able to found a colony); \emph{Volvox} is \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:unicellular}{multicellular}
(mortal somatic cells, a few germ cells).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:2}
$S/V = 3/r$: \qty{6}{\micro m^{-1}} for the coccus,
\qty{0.06}{\micro m^{-1}} for the \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:protist}{protist}, a hundredfold difference.
The coccus respires faster per unit volume: every part of its
cytoplasm is within a fraction of a micrometre of the surface
through which oxygen and food enter, whereas the \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:protist}{protist}'s interior
is fed through a hundred times less surface per unit volume.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:3}
No nucleus (a \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{nucleoid}) versus a nuclear envelope; no mitochondria or
endomembranes, the respiratory chain sitting in the plasma membrane,
versus organelles; a flagellum that is a rotating rigid helix of
flagellin turned by protons, versus a $9+2$ cilium bending by
dynein and ATP; also size (\qty{1}{\micro m} versus tens) and a
peptidoglycan wall. A bacterium cannot phagocytose: its wall forbids
engulfing a particle, which an amoeba does with its pseudopods.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:4}
Cilia: locomotion and sweeping food toward the mouth; oral groove:
funnels particles to the gullet; food vacuole: digests engulfed
bacteria with lysosomal enzymes; \omterm{prop:b2:unicellular-diversity:osmoregulation}{contractile vacuole}: expels the
water that enters by osmosis; macronucleus: the working nucleus,
hundreds of gene copies transcribed for daily life; micronucleus: the
germinal diploid nucleus, used in meiosis and conjugation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:5}
$t = x^2/2D$: $(10^{-6})^2/10^{-9} = \qty{1}{ms}$ for the bacterium;
$(5\times 10^{-4})^2/10^{-9} = \qty{250}{s}$, about four minutes,
for the amoeba. The amoeba cannot rely on diffusion to distribute
metabolites: it needs cytoplasmic streaming and motor-driven
transport along the cytoskeleton, which the bacterium does without.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:6}
$Re = \rho vL/\eta$: \emph{Paramecium} $10^3\times 10^{-3}\times
2\times 10^{-4}/10^{-3} = 0.2$; bacterium $10^3\times 2\times
10^{-5}\times 2\times 10^{-6}/10^{-3} = 4\times 10^{-5}$; swimmer
$10^3\times 1\times 2/10^{-3} = 2\times 10^6$. At $Re \gg 1$ inertia
carries the swimmer on after the stroke; at $Re \ll 1$ viscous drag
stops the bacterium within a nanometre, so it moves only while it
beats.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:7}
$v_s = 2r^2\Delta\rho g/9\eta = 2\times 10^{-10}\times 100\times
9.81/(9\times 10^{-3}) = \qty{2.2e-5}{m/s}$; \qty{50}{m} takes
$50/2.2\times 10^{-5} = \qty{2.3e6}{s}$, 27 days. Halving the radius
divides $v_s$ by 4: 108 days. Reducing $\Delta\rho$ to
\qty{20}{kg/m^3} divides it by 5: 135 days.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:8}
$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi\times 100\times 25\times 25 =
\qty{2.6e5}{\micro m^3}$. In \qty{1800}{s}: $2.6\times 10^5/1800 =
\qty{145}{\micro m^3/s}$; since $\qty{1}{\micro m^3} =
\qty{1e-15}{L}$, this is \qty{1.5e-13}{L/s}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:9}
A doubling over \qty{1}{mm} is a rise of about \qty{0.07}{\%} per
micrometre (since $2^{1/1000} - 1 = 0.0007$), so \qty{0.07}{\%}
across the cell. A \qty{1}{s} run covers \qty{20}{\micro m}:
\qty{1.4}{\%}. To resolve a \qty{0.07}{\%} difference the cell would
have to count some $(1/0.0007)^2 = 2\times 10^6$ molecules at each
end, far more than bind its few thousand receptors in a second;
\qty{1.4}{\%} needs about \num{5000}, which is feasible. Temporal
comparison converts a hopeless spatial measurement into a possible
one by letting the run do the integrating.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:10}
Surface $4\pi r^2$ with $r = \qty{250}{\micro m}$: \qty{7.9e5}{\micro
m^2}; cytoplasmic volume $\approx$ surface $\times$ \qty{2}{\micro m}
$= \qty{1.6e6}{\micro m^3}$; ratio \qty{0.5}{\micro m^{-1}}. For
\emph{E.~coli}, a cylinder: $S/V = 2\pi r L/\pi r^2 L = 2/r =
\qty{4}{\micro m^{-1}}$. The giant is only eight times worse than
\emph{E.~coli}, not 500 times, because every point of its cytoplasm
is within \qty{2}{\micro m} of the surface. The vacuole stores
nitrate, its electron acceptor, so that it can respire sulfide in
the anoxic sediment between the rare stirrings that bring nitrate
(\cref{ch:b2:microbial-metabolism}).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:11}
A cell cannot swim and divide at once. A 16-cell colony that stops
swimming for the hours of division sinks, by \omterm{thm:b2:unicellular-diversity:stokes}{Stokes' law}, a
negligible distance (its radius is a few tens of micrometres, its
sinking speed micrometres per second); the whole colony can divide
and lose nothing. A \num{10000}-cell sphere of radius
\qty{500}{\micro m} would sink a hundred times faster ---
millimetres per second, metres in an hour --- out of the light: it
pays to keep most cells swimming while a few divide. The germ cells
are large because they must carry the resources to build a whole
daughter sphere of thousands of cells by successive divisions
without feeding, so they are provisioned by the soma.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b2:unicellular-diversity:12}
A clade is an ancestor and all its descendants; a grade is a level
of organisation reached by several lineages. \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:protist}{Protists} --- green
algae (with the land plants), diatoms (with brown algae), ciliates
(with dinoflagellates and \emph{Plasmodium}), amoebae (near animals
and fungi) --- share only the eukaryotic cell lived alone, and their
last common ancestor is that of all eukaryotes, including us: a
grade. ``\omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{Prokaryote}'' unites bacteria and archaea by what they lack;
the archaea are closer to eukaryotes than to bacteria, so the term
is again a grade. Cyanobacteria descend from one ancestor that
invented oxygenic photosynthesis and include all its descendants (the
chloroplast lineage aside): a clade.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b2:unicellular-diversity:1}
\textbf{1.} $1\times 1\times 0.1 = \qty{0.1}{mm^3} = \qty{0.1}{\micro L}$.
\textbf{2.} $24/\qty{0.1}{\micro L} = 240$ per \unit{\micro L} $=
\qty{2.4e5}{}$ cells per millilitre.
\textbf{3.} $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi\times 125 = \qty{524}{\micro m^3}$;
$S/V = 3/5 = \qty{0.6}{\micro m^{-1}}$.
\textbf{4.} $2.4\times 10^5\times 524 = \qty{1.26e8}{\micro m^3}$ per
millilitre, and $\qty{1}{mL} = \qty{1e12}{\micro m^3}$: a fraction
$1.3\times 10^{-4}$, \qty{0.013}{\%}.
\textbf{5.} $\qty{1000}{m^3} = \qty{1e9}{mL}$: $2.4\times 10^{14}$
cells.
\textbf{6.} The cyanobacterium is a filament of much smaller cells
(a few micrometres), blue-green and without a distinct chloroplast or
nucleus, and it does not swim with flagella; the alga is a single
ovoid cell with two flagella, an eyespot and one cup-shaped
chloroplast.
\textbf{7.} $384/24 = 16 = 2^4$: four doublings in \qty{48}{h},
$T = \qty{12}{h}$.
\textbf{8.} $k = \ln 2/T = 0.693/12 = \qty{0.058}{h^{-1}}$.
\textbf{9.} $t = \ln(10^8/2.4\times 10^5)/k = \ln(417)/0.058 =
6.03/0.058 = \qty{104}{h}$, about 4.3 days.
\textbf{10.} Nutrients (phosphate, nitrate) run out; the cells shade
one another so that light per cell falls; grazers (ciliates,
rotifers, \emph{Daphnia}) multiply on them; at \qty{1e8}{} per
millilitre the cells would fill a tenth of a percent of the volume
and exhaust the dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$.
\textbf{11.} Growth only half the time: the mean rate is halved,
\qty{208}{h}, 8.7 days.
\textbf{12.} The count measures the population, not individual cells:
if a cell releases 8 daughters every \qty{36}{h}, the population is
multiplied by 8 $= 2^3$ in three \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:division}{generation times} of \qty{12}{h}
each. The \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:division}{generation time} is the mean doubling time of the number,
whichever way the divisions are grouped.
\textbf{13.} $Re = 10^3\times 10^{-4}\times 10^{-5}/10^{-3} = 10^{-3}$.
\textbf{14.} $m = 1050\times 5.24\times 10^{-16} = \qty{5.5e-13}{kg}$;
$6\pi\eta r = 6\pi\times 10^{-3}\times 5\times 10^{-6} =
\qty{9.4e-8}{kg/s}$; $d = 5.5\times 10^{-13}\times 10^{-4}/9.4\times
10^{-8} = \qty{5.8e-10}{m}$: half a nanometre.
\textbf{15.} $v_s = 2\times 25\times 10^{-12}\times 50\times
9.81/(9\times 10^{-3}) = \qty{2.7e-6}{m/s}$, \qty{2.7}{\micro m/s}.
\textbf{16.} Sinking \qty{1}{m}: $1/2.7\times 10^{-6} =
\qty{3.7e5}{s}$, 4.3 days; swimming up at \qty{100}{\micro m/s}:
\qty{1e4}{s}, 2.8 hours. Swimming wins by a factor of 37.
\textbf{17.} $c_\infty = \qty{1e-2}{mol/m^3}$: $J = 4\pi\times
1.9\times 10^{-9}\times 5\times 10^{-6}\times 10^{-2} =
\qty{1.2e-15}{mol/s} = 7.2\times 10^8$ molecules per second.
\textbf{18.} \qty{100}{pg} $= \qty{1e-10}{g} = \qty{8.3e-12}{mol}$ of
carbon, doubled in \qty{12}{h} $= \qty{43200}{s}$:
\qty{1.9e-16}{mol/s} $= 1.2\times 10^8$ atoms per second. Supply over
demand: $7.2/1.2 = 6$.
\textbf{19.} Supply grows tenfold ($\propto r$), demand a
thousandfold ($\propto r^3$): the ratio becomes $6/100 = 0.06$; such
a cell could grow at only six percent of the rate, so a
diffusion-fed alga of that size is impossible without stirring,
internal transport or a much slower metabolism.
\textbf{20.} $\Delta\Pi = RT\Delta c = 8.314\times 293\times 99 =
\qty{2.4e5}{Pa}$, \qty{2.4}{bar}.
\textbf{21.} Area $4\pi(5\times 10^{-6})^2 = \qty{3.14e-10}{m^2}$;
influx $L_p A\Delta\Pi = 10^{-14}\times 3.14\times 10^{-10}\times
2.4\times 10^5 = \qty{7.6e-19}{m^3/s} = \qty{0.76}{\micro m^3/s}$.
\textbf{22.} $524/0.76 = \qty{690}{s}$, about eleven minutes.
\textbf{23.} $P = \Delta\Pi\times$ flow $= 2.4\times 10^5\times
7.6\times 10^{-19} = \qty{1.8e-13}{W}$.
\textbf{24.} One ATP: $5\times 10^4/6.02\times 10^{23} =
\qty{8.3e-20}{J}$: $1.8\times 10^{-13}/8.3\times 10^{-20} = 2.2\times
10^6$ ATP per second. Carbon fixation: $3\times 1.2\times 10^8 =
3.6\times 10^8$ ATP per second. The vacuole costs \qty{0.6}{\%} of
that --- cheap insurance against bursting.
\textbf{25.} Water influx \qty{0.76}{\micro m^3/s} (the cell's volume
in eleven minutes); without a \omterm{prop:b2:unicellular-diversity:osmoregulation}{contractile vacuole} the cell would
double its volume in about \qty{700}{s}; keeping dry costs at least
$2\times 10^6$ ATP per second, under one percent of what its
photosynthesis spends on carbon.
\end{solution}
