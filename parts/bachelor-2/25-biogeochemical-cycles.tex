\chapter{Biogeochemical Cycles}\label{ch:b2:biogeochemical-cycles}

In March 1958 a young chemist named Charles Keeling switched on an
infrared gas analyser on the north slope of Mauna Loa, in Hawaii,
three and a half kilometres above the Pacific, where the air is
as well mixed as air can be. He had expected to measure a constant;
within a year he had a sawtooth --- carbon dioxide rising every
winter and falling every summer as the forests of the northern
hemisphere breathed out and in --- and within a few years a slope
under the sawtooth that has not stopped since. The \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{Keeling curve}
is the biosphere's breathing seen from outside, with a fever chart
drawn over it. This chapter is about the cycles the curve records:
where the carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur of living things
are stored, how fast they move between \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoirs}, what sets those
speeds --- most of them the metabolisms of
\cref{ch:b2:microbial-metabolism} --- and what one species has done
to each cycle in two hundred years.

\section{Reservoirs, fluxes and residence times}

\begin{definition}[Reservoir, flux, residence time]\label{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}
\index{reservoir}\index{flux}\index{residence time}\index{steady state}\index{biogeochemical cycle}
A \emph{biogeochemical cycle} is the movement of an element between
\emph{reservoirs} (the atmosphere, the ocean, soils, living
biomass, rocks) by \emph{fluxes} measured in mass per year. A
reservoir of mass $M$ with total outflow $F$ has a \emph{residence
time} $\tau = M/F$, the average time an atom spends in it; at
\emph{steady state} inflow equals outflow and $M$ is constant. The
cycles are coupled through the stoichiometry of life --- the
\emph{Redfield ratio} of marine plankton, $\mathrm{C:N:P} =
106:16:1$ by atoms --- so that the supply of the scarcest element
sets the rate at which all the others are taken up.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The one-box model]\label{thm:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:box}
\index{box model}\index{relaxation time}
If a \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} loses its content at a rate proportional to what it
holds, $F_{\text{out}} = M/\tau$, and receives a constant inflow
$F_{\text{in}}$, then
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} = F_{\text{in}} - \frac{M}{\tau},
\qquad
M(t) = M^{*} + (M_0 - M^{*})\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad M^{*} = F_{\text{in}}\tau :
\]
the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir} relaxes to a \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{steady state} proportional to its input,
with the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} as the time constant. A step increase in
input raises the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{steady state} in proportion and the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir}
follows within a few $\tau$: a pool with a \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} of days
(atmospheric ammonia, the nitrate of a lake in spring) tracks its
sources; one with a \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} of centuries (deep-ocean carbon,
soil organic matter) integrates them, and the perturbation of a
slow pool outlives the perturbation that caused it.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The equation is linear with constant coefficients: the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{steady state}
$M^{*}$ makes the right side zero, and the deviation $m = M - M^{*}$
obeys $\dot m = -m/\tau$, so $m = m_0 e^{-t/\tau}$. If a \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoir}
has several outflows, $\tau$ is set by their sum, $1/\tau = \sum_i
1/\tau_i$: the fastest sink dominates.
\end{proof}

\section{The carbon cycle}

\begin{proposition}[The carbon budget]\label{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}
\index{carbon cycle}\index{airborne fraction}\index{carbon sink}\index{Keeling curve}
In units of gigatonnes of carbon (\unit{GtC}, $10^{12}$~kg), the
main \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{reservoirs} are the atmosphere (about 880 in the 2020s, 590
before industry), land plants (450), soils (1700), the surface
ocean (900), the deep ocean (\num{37000}) and sedimentary rocks
(\num{e8}); fossil fuels are the fraction of the last that
industry can reach, some \num{5000}. The natural \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{fluxes} are large
and nearly balanced: photosynthesis on land takes about 120 a
year and respiration and decomposition return it; the ocean
surface exchanges about 80 each way with the air. The human \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{flux} is
small against these --- some 10 a year from fossil fuels and 1
from deforestation --- but one-way, and the atmosphere has kept
\emph{about \qty{45}{\%}} of it (the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction}}),
the ocean taking a quarter and the land, fertilised by the extra
$\mathrm{CO_2}$, the rest. One part per million of atmospheric $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is
\qty{2.13}{GtC}; the concentration rose from 280 to \num{315}~ppm
between 1750 and 1958, and from 315 to over \num{420} between 1958
and the 2020s, currently by \qty{2.5}{ppm} a year. The \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence
time} of a $\mathrm{CO_2}$ molecule in the air, $880/200 \approx 4$ years,
is short; the lifetime of the \emph{excess}, set by the slow
mixing into the deep ocean and the slower weathering of rock, is
centuries to millennia --- a fifth of what is emitted today will
still be in the air in ten thousand years.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Three signatures show that the added carbon is fossil. Its
isotopes: fossil carbon has no $\mathrm{{}^{14}C}$ (it decayed away
millions of years ago) and is depleted in $\mathrm{{}^{13}C}$ as all plant
carbon is, and the atmosphere has been losing both --- the Suess
effect, measured in tree rings since the 1950s. Its oxygen: the
atmosphere's $\mathrm{O_2}$ falls in step, by the stoichiometry of
combustion, as Keeling's son showed in the 1990s, which a volcanic
or oceanic source would not produce. Its bookkeeping: the sum of
coal, oil and gas sold, converted to carbon, exceeds the increase
in the air by a factor of two, and the missing half is found in
the ocean's rising dissolved inorganic carbon and falling pH, and
in the land sink. The seasonal sawtooth, larger at Barrow in Alaska
than at Mauna Loa and nearly absent at the South Pole, is the
northern forests' summer uptake and winter release --- a direct
measurement of the biosphere's breathing.
\end{proof}

\section{The nitrogen cycle}

\begin{proposition}[The nitrogen cycle]\label{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:nitrogen}
\index{nitrogen cycle}\index{nitrogen fixation}\index{nitrification}\index{denitrification}\index{Haber--Bosch process}\index{anammox}
Nitrogen is abundant --- the air holds $4\times 10^{9}$~Tg of
$\mathrm{N_2}$ --- and scarce, because the triple bond of $\mathrm{N_2}$ is opened
by only two processes: lightning (a few Tg a year) and
\emph{biological \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:nitrogen}{nitrogen fixation}} by \omterm{def:b2:unicellular-diversity:prokaryote}{prokaryotes} with
nitrogenase, some 100 Tg a year on land and 100 in the sea, at a
cost of 16 ATP per $\mathrm{N_2}$. Fixed nitrogen then runs a redox
ladder driven by microbes (\cref{ch:b2:microbial-metabolism}):
\emph{ammonification} of dead matter to $\mathrm{NH_4^{+}}$;
\emph{nitrification} of $\mathrm{NH_4^{+}}$ to $\mathrm{NO_2^{-}}$ and $\mathrm{NO_3^{-}}$ by
aerobic \omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:trophic}{chemolithotrophs}; uptake of $\mathrm{NH_4^{+}}$ or $\mathrm{NO_3^{-}}$ by plants
and their reduction back to amine; and, where oxygen is absent,
\emph{\omterm{def:b2:microbial-metabolism:anaerobic}{denitrification}} of $\mathrm{NO_3^{-}}$ to $\mathrm{N_2}$ (with $\mathrm{N_2O}$ as a
leak) and \emph{anammox}, the anaerobic oxidation of ammonium by
nitrite to $\mathrm{N_2}$, which together return about as much to the air
as fixation takes. The \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} of a nitrogen atom in the
biosphere is a few hundred years; in the atmosphere, $4\times
10^{9}/400 \approx 10^{7}$ years. Since 1913 the \emph{Haber--Bosch}
process has fixed nitrogen industrially, now about 120 Tg a year,
and cultivated legumes and fossil-fuel combustion add 60 and 30:
human activity fixes as much nitrogen as all natural processes
together, and feeds half the people alive.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Evidence]
Hutchinson Forest at Hubbard Brook, New Hampshire: in 1965 one
entire watershed was clear-felled and kept bare with herbicide,
while a neighbouring one was left intact, and the streams draining
each were gauged and analysed for years. The stripped watershed
lost nitrate at sixty times the control's rate --- the whole
nitrogen capital of the soil, no longer taken up by plants, was
nitrified and washed out, taking calcium and potassium with it and
acidifying the stream. The experiment showed the cycle's tightness
in an intact forest (a few kilograms of nitrogen lost per hectare
per year, against hundreds cycled) and what breaks it.
\end{proof}

\section{Phosphorus and sulfur}

\begin{proposition}[Two cycles without a fast gas]\label{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:ps}
\index{phosphorus cycle}\index{sulfur cycle}\index{eutrophication}\index{dimethyl sulfide}
\emph{Phosphorus} has no gaseous form. It enters ecosystems by the
weathering of apatite in rock, some 20 Tg a year over the whole
land surface, is recycled within soils and lakes many times over
--- a phosphate ion in a lake's surface water is taken up within
hours --- and leaves to the sediments, where it stays for the
$10^{8}$ years of a rock cycle. Life is therefore phosphorus-thrifty
and phosphorus-limited: the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} in the ocean is some
\num{20000} years, and the deep sea's phosphate, brought up by
upwelling, sets its productivity. Mining now moves about as much
phosphorus onto farmland as weathering releases, and its runoff into
lakes causes \emph{eutrophication}: an algal bloom, its decay, the
consumption of oxygen, and the death of the fish --- Schindler's
whole-lake experiments in Ontario in the 1970s, with one half of a
lake fertilised with phosphorus and the other not, showed phosphorus
alone to be the trigger, and led to its removal from detergents.
\emph{Sulfur} has both a rock cycle (gypsum, pyrite) and a gas
phase: the $\mathrm{SO_2}$ of volcanoes and coal, the $\mathrm{H_2S}$ of anoxic
sediments, and \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:ps}{dimethyl sulfide}} from marine plankton, whose
oxidation products seed the clouds over the ocean. Coal burning
tripled the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{flux} of sulfur to the air in the twentieth century and
gave Europe and North America their acid rain; scrubbers reversed
it within decades, the cycle's \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} in the air being a
few days.
\end{proposition}

\section{The perturbed planet}

\begin{example}[What the numbers say]\label{ex:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:numbers}
Emissions of \qty{10}{GtC} a year with an \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction} of
$0.45$ leave \qty{4.5}{GtC}, or \qty{2.1}{ppm}, in the air each year
--- the observed slope. Cumulative emissions since 1850 are about
\qty{700}{GtC}, of which \qty{290}{GtC} is in the air (140~ppm), the
rest in ocean and land. To hold the atmosphere at any level, net
emissions must fall to what the slow sinks --- deep-ocean mixing and
weathering, together under \qty{1}{GtC} a year --- can take:
\emph{net zero} is not a slogan but the \omterm{thm:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:box}{box model}'s steady-state
condition with a very long $\tau$. For nitrogen, the doubling of
the fixed-nitrogen \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{flux} has doubled the nitrate in rivers, produced
dead zones at the mouths of the Mississippi and the Baltic, and
raised the atmosphere's $\mathrm{N_2O}$, a greenhouse gas with a \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence
time} of a century, by a fifth. \Cref{ch:b2:global-change} follows
the consequences for living things.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The Carbon Budget of a Century}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a century of emissions
passed through the box model of the atmosphere, the ocean's share
and the land's, the nitrogen cascade from a fertilised field to a
dead zone, and a lake's phosphorus budget, ending on the airborne
fraction, the atmospheric concentration in 2100, and the nitrogen
lost per hectare}]
\label{pb:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:1}
Data. Atmosphere in 1850: \qty{285}{ppm}; \qty{1}{ppm} $=
\qty{2.13}{GtC}$. Cumulative emissions 1850--2020: \qty{700}{GtC};
atmospheric $\mathrm{CO_2}$ in 2020: \qty{412}{ppm}. A field of
\qty{1}{ha} receives \qty{150}{kg} of nitrogen fertiliser a year;
the crop takes up \qty{60}{\%}, \qty{25}{\%} is denitrified, the
rest leaches. A lake of \qty{5e7}{m^3} flushes a tenth of its water
a year and receives \qty{5}{t} of phosphorus a year.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction}.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the atmospheric carbon in 1850 and in 2020, and the
        increase in GtC.
  \item Compute the \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction} over 1850--2020.
  \item Where is the rest? Give the two sinks and, from the
        proposition, their approximate shares.
  \item If the ocean has taken \qty{25}{\%} of the emissions, by how
        much has its dissolved inorganic carbon, \qty{38000}{GtC},
        risen in relative terms? Why does the surface ocean
        nonetheless acidify measurably?
  \item Why does the \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction} stay roughly constant while
        emissions grow exponentially? (Consider the \omterm{thm:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:box}{box model} with
        $Q \propto e^{t/T}$ and a sink $E/\tau$.)
  \item What would the \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction} become if emissions were
        held constant for many centuries?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The century ahead.}
Model the excess carbon $E$ (above the 1850 level) with
$\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}t = Q - E/\tau$, $\tau = 100$ years.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the excess in 2020 in GtC.
  \item With $Q$ held at \qty{10}{GtC/yr}, compute the steady-state
        excess and the concentration it implies.
  \item Solve for $E(t)$ from 2020 with $Q = 10$ and give $E$ in
        2100 and the concentration.
  \item Now let $Q$ fall linearly to zero between 2020 and 2070 and
        stay at zero. Compute $E$ in 2070 (integrate the equation;
        the particular solution for a linear $Q$ is linear plus a
        constant) and in 2100.
  \item Compare the two concentrations in 2100.
  \item Why is a single $\tau$ optimistic for the second scenario?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The nitrogen cascade.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the nitrogen taken up by the crop, denitrified and
        leached, per hectare per year.
  \item The leached nitrogen reaches a river as nitrate and then the
        sea, where plankton use it at the Redfield ratio. How much
        carbon does it allow the plankton to fix?
  \item That \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{carbon sinks} and is respired at depth, consuming
        oxygen at 1 $\mathrm{O_2}$ per C. Compute the oxygen consumed, in
        moles and in kilograms.
  \item A cubic metre of seawater holds about \qty{0.25}{mol} of
        $\mathrm{O_2}$ when saturated. How many cubic metres of bottom water
        does one hectare's leaching deoxygenate?
  \item The denitrified fraction escapes partly as $\mathrm{N_2O}$,
        \qty{1}{\%} of the nitrogen denitrified. Compute the $\mathrm{N_2O}$
        per hectare per year, and its warming equivalent in $\mathrm{CO_2}$
        ($\mathrm{N_2O}$ is 270 times $\mathrm{CO_2}$ per kilogram).
  \item Compare with the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ emitted to make the fertiliser
        (about \qty{4}{kg} of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ per kilogram of nitrogen by
        Haber--Bosch).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The lake.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the lake's phosphorus \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} if the only
        loss is flushing, and its steady-state concentration in
        \unit{mg/m^3}.
  \item Lakes with more than \qty{30}{mg/m^3} of phosphorus are
        eutrophic. Is this one?
  \item Sedimentation removes phosphorus with its own time constant
        of 5 years. Recompute the \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{residence time} and the
        concentration.
  \item The input is cut to \qty{1}{t} a year. Compute the new
        \omterm{def:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:reservoir}{steady state} and the time to get within \qty{10}{\%} of it.
  \item Why do many lakes recover more slowly than this calculation
        predicts?
  \item Compute the carbon the lake's phosphorus can build into
        plankton each year at the Redfield ratio, from the
        \qty{5}{t} input.
  \item State the result: the \omterm{prop:b2:biogeochemical-cycles:carbon}{airborne fraction}, the two
        concentrations in 2100, and the nitrogen leached per
        hectare.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
